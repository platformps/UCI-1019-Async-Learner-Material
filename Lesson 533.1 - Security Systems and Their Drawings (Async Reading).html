533.1.1 Analog, IP, and hybrid video surveillance

A video surveillance system is a set of cameras, the cables that join them to a recorder, and the recorder that keeps the video. The kind of camera decides the cable an installer pulls and terminates.

An analog camera sends its picture as an analog video signal on coaxial cable. It usually takes its power on a separate pair of conductors, from a power supply at the head end or near the camera; some high-definition analog cameras take it over the coaxial cable from the recorder. An IP camera digitizes and compresses the picture in the camera and sends it as network data on twisted-pair Ethernet cable. It can take its power on the same cable, by Power over Ethernet (PoE), from a PoE switch port, a recorder’s PoE port, or an injector. A hybrid system records both kinds of camera on one recorder: analog cameras on its coaxial inputs, IP cameras over the network.

533.1.2 Where the video is recorded

Kestrel’s estimators name three recording options. Each keeps the video in a different place, and each depends on something different to keep recording.

  • Network video recorder (NVR). A recorder on site that records IP cameras over the network. Some models power their cameras through their own PoE ports.
  • Digital video recorder (DVR). A recorder on site that records analog cameras over coaxial cable.
  • Cloud video management system (cloud VMS). The cameras send video over the internet to a provider’s service, which records it and serves it to viewers. Usually a subscription.

533.1.3 How access control is managed

An access-controlled door has the same parts whatever the system. The three architectures differ in where the people, credentials, and schedules for every door are managed.

The parts of an access-controlled door

  • Reader. Reads the credential a person presents and passes it to the controller.
  • Controller, or hub. Decides whether the credential may open the door now, switches the lock, and records each event.
  • Lock. An electric lock, such as an electric strike or a magnetic lock, that the controller switches.
  • Request-to-exit device. A push button or a motion sensor on the inside that tells the controller someone is leaving.
  • Door position switch. A contact that tells the controller whether the door is shut.
  • Power. For the controller, the reader, and the lock: from the controller’s own outputs, PoE, or a separate power supply.

The three architectures

  • Standalone. The controller is at the door, managed at the door or on a local panel. No network management.
  • Networked on-premises. Controllers, or a console, on the site network, managed from a server or console the owner runs.
  • Cloud-managed. Controllers on site, managed through the manufacturer’s cloud service. Usually a subscription.

Some suppliers use “on-premises” for any system managed on site, standalone or networked; others mean a networked system only. When a document says “on-premises” alone, find out which it means.

533.1.4 The credentials people carry

A credential is what a person presents at the reader. The three kinds differ in how the reader reads them, how easily they can be copied, and what the person has to carry.

  • 125 kHz proximity. A card or fob read by radio at 125 kHz. Most carry an unencrypted number.
  • 13.56 MHz smart. A card or fob read by radio at 13.56 MHz. Many carry encrypted data.
  • Mobile. The credential is held on a phone, in an app or the phone’s wallet.
The three credential types compared: copying, what the person carries, what the reader must support, and what removing one involves.
125 kHz proximity13.56 MHz smartMobile
CopyingA card that sends an unencrypted number can be copied by anyone who can read itHarder where the card type and the system use encryption; some early 13.56 MHz card types have published weaknessesDepends on the app and the phone’s own security
The person carriesA card or fobA card or fobA phone carrying the credential (in an app or the phone’s wallet), usually charged; some wallet credentials work for a few hours after the phone shuts down
The reader must support125 kHz13.56 MHz and that card typeThe maker’s mobile credential, by Near Field Communication (NFC) or Bluetooth
Removing oneThe credential is removed in the access system. No lock is re-keyed, whichever type it is.

A reader reads only the frequencies and card types it is built for. A site moving from 125 kHz cards to 13.56 MHz cards runs both until every card is replaced. During the change, it needs readers that read both, or a second credential for each person. A mobile credential depends on the person: some staff will not install a work app on a personal phone, and some people do not carry a smartphone.

Lesson 533.1 · Section 2 Choosing and Checking

533.1.5 A recommendation built from the site’s facts

An estimator asks an installer which system suits a site. The installer answers decision by decision, with the site’s own facts.

A field view makes three decisions: the recording option, the access-control option, and the credential type. For each, it names one option and cites the facts of that site that support it. It also cites any fact that weighs against it, or says that none does, and gives the reason in one sentence. For most decisions more than one option can be argued. The estimator weighs the facts, so a fact cited for an option has to support it, and a fact cited against it has to count against it.

A fact supports an option when the site has what the option depends on: cabling that suits the camera type, a network and people to manage a console, an internet line that a cloud service can rely on, or readers that read the credential. A fact weighs against an option when the option needs something the site lacks, or when the site sets a condition the option breaks.

Lesson 533.1 · Section 2 Choosing and Checking

533.1.6 Products from different makers: ONVIF profiles

A camera from one maker and a recorder from another work together only for the functions both have been built and tested to share.

ONVIF is an industry forum that publishes interface specifications for IP security products, grouped into profiles. A product conforms to a profile, not to ONVIF as a whole, and each profile covers a set of functions. Profile S covers video streaming from a camera to a recorder or viewer. Profile T covers advanced video streaming. Profile G covers recording and storage on the device. Profiles A and C cover access control.

  • Read the profile, not the word. A datasheet line that reads “ONVIF” with no profile does not say which functions work with another maker’s product.
  • Check the claim. ONVIF publishes a list of conformant products by profile. A product on that list for the profile you need has passed the forum’s conformance process.
  • Expect gaps outside the profile. Functions a profile does not cover, such as one maker’s video analytics, may work only between that maker’s own products.

533.1.7 Constraints that someone else decides

Some facts about a site set a rule that the installer does not decide. The installer recognizes the rule, names who decides it, and sends it to them through the site lead.

Kinds of constraint an installer escalates, and who usually decides each.
ConstraintWho decides
A procurement rule on which products may be boughtThe client’s procurement or compliance office

Covered equipment: escalate, never judge by brand

Some federal rules restrict the telecommunications and video surveillance equipment that certain organizations may buy or fund. Section 889 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2019 bars federal agencies, federal contractors, and many recipients of federal funds from buying covered equipment. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Covered List names equipment that the FCC bars from federal subsidy and from new FCC approval. The client’s procurement or compliance office decides, from the current text and lists, whether a rule applies to the client and whether a product falls under it. A rule can reach a component, a maker’s subsidiary, or a product sold under another name, so a brand on the box settles nothing.

The installer’s part: name the fact that sets the rule, the party who decides, and the products in question, and send them through the site lead with the work that waits on the answer. The installer does not state whether a product is permitted.

An escalation note on another job

Job. Cameras on the street front of the Marlow Road Clinic, a designated city landmark.

Fact. The city’s landmark ordinance requires the preservation office to approve any fixing to the street facade.

Who decides. The city’s preservation office, through the owner.

Sent. To the site lead: the two facade camera positions and their fixings from the drawings, for the owner to submit. The facade cameras wait for the approval; the interior cameras go ahead.

533.1.8 Checking an AI tool’s answer

An AI tool writes a fluent description of a product in seconds. Each claim in it stays a claim until a line in the manufacturer’s documents states the same fact.

Split the answer into single statements, each about one product and each stating one fact a reader could check. Then find the line in the manufacturer’s datasheet, installation guide, or support article that covers it, and mark the statement:

  • Confirmed. A line in the manufacturer’s documents states the same fact.
  • Contradicted. A line states something that cannot be true if the statement is true.
  • Unverifiable. No line covers the statement.

An unverifiable statement may still be true. The documents in front of you do not confirm it, so it goes to the site lead as a question, not into a record as a fact. A statement about the site or the job, rather than about the product, is not checked against a product document.

Lesson 533.1 · Section 3 Reading the Security Drawings

533.1.9 The camera schedule and the CCTV riser

A security drawing set shows each device in more than one place. Each kind of sheet answers a different question about it.

  • General notes and legend. The symbols, the abbreviations, the label scheme, and notes that apply to every sheet.
  • Plans and elevations. Where each device is mounted, with its position and height.
  • Schedules. One row for each device: its identifier, model, head-end device, port or terminal, cable label, and settings such as a channel name.
  • Riser and wiring diagrams. How each device connects to the head end: the pathway, the cable, the port or terminal, the power, and the network. A diagram is not to scale.

The head end is the equipment the devices connect back to, usually in a telecommunications room: the recorder for the cameras, the controller or hub for the doors, and the switches they join.

Match each question about a device to the sheet that answers it.

Reading a camera schedule

Read a schedule across one row at a time. Every entry in the row belongs to one camera, and the entries have to agree with each other and with the label scheme in the general notes: the identifier, the cable label, and the channel name all point to the same camera.

533.1.10 The door schedule and the access-control wiring diagram

A door schedule lists what each controlled door carries. The wiring diagram shows where each device’s conductors land.

A door schedule gives one row per door: the door number, the lock, the reader, the request-to-exit device, the door position switch, the controller, and the power supply, often with notes on who supplies the door hardware. Read it across the row, as you read a camera schedule.

An access-control wiring diagram draws each device of a door, the cable to it, and the terminal designation where each conductor lands on the controller or on a power supply. Terminal designations are the controller maker’s names, printed on the controller beside each terminal. Read them off the diagram and check them against the controller itself. A note such as “to confirm” on a diagram means the designer has not confirmed the value: ask before you land a conductor on it.

Which devices take power

  • Reader. Takes power and data from the controller: from a reader port, or from a PoE port on a controller that has them.
  • Lock. Takes power that the controller switches: from an output of the controller, or from a separate power supply through a relay on the controller.
  • Request-to-exit push button and door position switch. Contacts that the controller reads at an input; they take no power from the controller. An illuminated button needs power for its lamp. A push-to-exit on an egress door also breaks the lock’s power directly. A request-to-exit motion sensor needs power, from a separate supply or an auxiliary output on the controller.

533.1.11 Tracing a device to its head end

A trace follows one device from its mounting point to the port or terminal where its cable lands, and names where it takes power and where it joins the network.

  1. Device identifier. From the schedule, checked against the label scheme in the general notes.
  2. Cable label and cable type. From the schedule, the riser or wiring diagram, or the owner’s standard.
  3. Head-end device. The identifier of the recorder, controller, or hub where the cable lands.
  4. Port or terminal. For a port, its number; for a terminal, its designation on the wiring diagram.
  5. Power source. The port, output, or power supply the device takes its power from, or “none” for a contact that the controller only reads.
  6. Network boundary. The switch, the port, and the network the device joins. A device with no network connection of its own, such as a lock or a contact, joins through the head-end device it lands on.

Record the sheet each value came from. Where two sheets give different values for the same entry, record the value you used and its sheet, then finish the trace. The difference becomes a question for the site lead.

A power boundary is where one source of power ends and the next begins: a 120 V circuit that an electrician installs and a low-voltage supply that the installer wires, or a PoE port and the device it powers. A network boundary is the switch port where a device, or the head-end device it lands on, joins a network. A trace names both, because a technician investigates a dark or offline device from those two points.

Lesson 533.1 · Section 4 Discrepancies and the RFI

533.1.12 Drawing to drawing, drawing to field

A discrepancy is two values for one thing. The installer records both, with where each came from, and installs neither until the site lead decides.

  • Drawing to drawing. Two sheets, or two entries on one sheet, give different values for the same element: a different device, port, label, position, or model.
  • Drawing to field. A drawing gives a value that the site does not match: a position that measures differently, a box or pathway that is not what the drawing shows, or something already mounted where the device is drawn.

Where to look

  • Each schedule row against itself: the identifier, the cable label, and the channel name point to the same device.
  • Each identifier against the label scheme in the general notes.
  • Each device against its other appearances: the schedule against the plan or elevation, the riser, and the wiring diagram.
  • Each drawn position and element against the site, measured with a tape from the reference the drawing uses.

Lesson 533.1 · Section 4 Discrepancies and the RFI

533.1.13 The request for information

An RFI puts a discrepancy to the person who can decide it, in writing, before anyone wires to a guess.

An RFI goes to the site lead, who answers it or passes it to the designer. One RFI can cover several discrepancies in one area. It carries these parts:

  • Number, to, from, and date. They tie each answer to its question.
  • Sheets and elements. Each sheet and each element identifier, with both values and their sources.
  • Question. One the site lead can answer with a decision: “Which lock does door 104 take?”, not “The lock is wrong.”
  • Proposed resolution. The answer the installer recommends, for the site lead to accept or overrule.
  • Work held. The work that waits for the answer. Other work goes ahead.
An RFI on another job

RFI RLA-014. To: site lead. From: installer, door 104. Date: the day it is sent.

Sheets and elements. Door schedule, door 104: the lock is listed as a magnetic lock; the door 104 wiring diagram shows electric strike 104-L on RLY 1. Elevation of door 104, reader 104-R: drawn on the latch side, 8 in from the frame, where a fire alarm pull station is mounted.

Question. Which lock does door 104 take, and where does reader 104-R go?

Proposed resolution. Electric strike 104-L, as the wiring diagram shows, with the door schedule revised. Reader 104-R moved up the frame clear of the pull station, at a position the designer sets.

Work held. Lock and reader rough-in at door 104. The request-to-exit and the door position switch go ahead.

Lesson 533.1 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

533.1.14 What you covered

Know which system a device belongs to, recommend from the site’s facts, check every claim against the maker’s documents, and follow each device on the drawings to the rack before you pull a cable.

Systems on a security job

An analog camera sends video on coaxial cable to a DVR and usually takes power on a separate pair. An IP camera sends network data on twisted pair to an NVR or a video management system and can take its power by PoE. A hybrid recorder takes both. An NVR and a DVR keep video on site; a cloud VMS keeps it at a provider and depends on the site’s internet line. Standalone access control is managed at each door or on a local panel; networked on-premises access control is managed from a server or console the owner runs; cloud-managed access control is managed through the manufacturer’s cloud service. Some suppliers use “on-premises” for both of the first two; others mean a networked system only. A 125 kHz proximity credential usually carries an unencrypted number; a 13.56 MHz smart credential often carries encrypted data; a mobile credential lives on a phone, in an app or the phone’s wallet. Each reader reads only what it is built for.

Choosing and checking

A field view names one option per decision, the facts that support it, any fact against it, and a reason. ONVIF conformance is by profile. A procurement rule, an approval, an insurer’s requirement, or a data policy goes to the party who decides it, through the site lead; the installer never judges covered equipment by brand. Each statement from an AI tool is marked confirmed, contradicted, or unverifiable against a line in the manufacturer’s documents.

Reading the security drawings

General notes give the symbols and the label scheme, plans and elevations the positions, schedules one row per device, and riser and wiring diagrams the connections. A trace records the identifier, the cable label and type, the head-end device, the port or terminal, the power source, and the network boundary, each with its sheet.

Discrepancies and the RFI

A discrepancy is drawing to drawing or drawing to field. Record both values and their sources, install neither, and send one RFI with a question the site lead can answer with a decision, a proposed resolution, and the work held.
