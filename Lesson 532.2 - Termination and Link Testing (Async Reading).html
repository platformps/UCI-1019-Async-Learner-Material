In a field-terminated connector, the fiber is bonded on center in a ceramic ferrule and polished flush with the ferrule’s face.

D. Okafor sets the standard for the qualification: “One connector each, on a multimode test cable. You inspect your own end face and write down what you see before I look at it. If it needs more polish, say which film and why.”

The parts of a connector

The ferrule is a ceramic cylinder with a bore sized to the 125 micrometer (µm) glass; an SC ferrule is 2.5 mm across. The fiber runs through the bore and ends at the ferrule’s polished front, the end face. The body holds the ferrule on a spring and latches into an adapter. The boot slides up behind the body and keeps the fiber from bending sharply where it leaves the connector. On some designs, the installer also crimps the cable’s aramid yarn to the back of the body; the connector’s instructions say whether yours does.

Termination methods

Connectors reach the field in several forms. In an adhesive-and-polish connector, the installer bonds the fiber into the ferrule with epoxy or an anaerobic adhesive and polishes the end face by hand. A pre-polished connector holds a factory-polished fiber stub, which the installer joins to the field fiber inside the connector. A splice-on connector carries a short factory-terminated stub that the installer fusion splices to the field fiber. Each method has its own tools and steps. The qualification uses an SC multimode connector, anaerobic adhesive, and a dry hand polish.

532.2.2 Preparing the fiber

Preparation exposes a clean length of bare glass, sized to the connector, without nicking it.

Glasses stay on while anyone at the table strips, scribes, or polishes. Every piece of bare glass goes into a sealed trash container as soon as it comes off.

  1. Open the cable. Ring the outer jacket with a jacket ringer, slit it back to the ring, and cut the aramid yarn back with Kevlar cutters, leaving enough free strand to hold while you work.
  2. Fit the boot first. Slide the boot onto the strand, narrow end toward the cable. Once the connector is bonded, the boot cannot pass over it.
  3. Strip the buffer and coating. Use the stripper hole sized for each layer and strip to the length in the connector’s instructions; strip lengths differ between connectors.
  4. Clean the glass. Draw the bare fiber through a wipe dampened with isopropyl alcohol, then through a dry wipe. Touch the bare glass with nothing else.
  5. Dry fit. Slide the bare fiber into the connector without adhesive. It must pass through the ferrule without force. Then draw it back out.

A nick in the glass may not break it at once. It weakens the fiber, which can snap during insertion or polishing. Where the stripper has scored the glass, cut the end off and strip again, if the strand is long enough. A dry fit that meets resistance points to a strip that is too short, buffer left on the glass, or debris in the bore.

532.2.3 Bonding with anaerobic adhesive

An anaerobic adhesive stays liquid in air and cures where air is shut out, as in a ferrule bore filled by a fiber.

An anaerobic adhesive cures when it is cut off from oxygen and in contact with a surface that starts the cure. Metal does so. Glass and ceramic do so only slowly, so a primer, also called an activator, goes on the glass to start the cure. The qualification kit pairs an anaerobic adhesive with its primer. The product data sheets give the cure time; where a data sheet and a posted cure time differ, the data sheet governs.

Bonding the fiber

  1. Inject adhesive into the back of the connector with the team’s syringe, as the connector’s instructions direct.
  2. Apply primer to the bare glass, pointing the primer away from yourself and the people beside you. Let it dry for the time the primer’s data sheet gives.
  3. Insert the fiber straight into the connector, without twisting it, until the buffer seats against the back of the ferrule. The glass now protrudes past the end face.
  4. Slide the boot up against the body.
  5. Leave the connector still until the adhesive has cured. A fiber moved before the cure loses its bond and can draw back from the end face.

Keep the syringe capped between uses, and keep the adhesive and the primer capped whenever they are not in use.

532.2.4 Scribing and polishing

You scribe off the glass that protrudes past the ferrule, then polish what is left down to a flush, smooth end face, from coarse film to fine.

Scribe and break

Draw the carbide scribe once, lightly, across the glass just above the ferrule tip. Pull the excess glass straight away from the ferrule, and put it in your trash container at once: a scribed end is sharp and almost invisible. What remains is a short nub of glass standing above the end face.

Air polish, then puck polish

Polishing films carry an abrasive of a stated grit size, in micrometers. The qualification kit uses three: 12 µm, 3 µm, and 0.5 µm. Read the grit size printed on each film; film colors differ between makers.

  • Air polish on 12 µm film. Hold the film in your hand, unsupported, and rub the ferrule tip lightly against it until the nub is gone. Held film flexes around the nub rather than pressing it against a hard surface.
  • Puck polish on 3 µm, then 0.5 µm. Lay the film on the pad, seat the connector in the polishing puck, and polish in a figure-eight, keeping the puck flat on the film. The puck holds the ferrule square to the film.

Polish dry, with no water or alcohol on the film. Each film removes the scratches left by the coarser film before it. A finer film cannot remove a deep scratch, so skipping a grade carries the coarser film’s scratches into the finished end face. A figure-eight changes the direction of each stroke, so scratches cross rather than deepen along one line, and the film wears evenly. Use light pressure: hard pressure can crack the fiber or change the shape of the end face. Grit carried from a coarse film onto a finer one scratches the end face, so keep the films and the puck clean.

532.2.5 Judging the finished end face

Inspection decides whether a connector is finished, needs more polishing, or must be terminated again.

Clean the end face with your One-Click cleaner, then inspect it at your team’s inspection station. Nothing in this work is lit, but still inspect an end face only on a connector with no source connected. On the mock-up, the end face clears when the core zone and the cladding zone of IEC 61300-3-35, Zones A and B, show no contamination and no defect. Record what you see, the zone each mark sits in, your decision, and what you based it on.

Polish again, or terminate again

To remove a defect left by polishing, go back to the film that removes it, then work down through the finer films again. Missing or damaged glass cannot be polished back.

532.2.6 Decibels and optical power

Optical power is read in dBm, a level relative to one milliwatt, and loss in dB, a ratio between two levels.

The decibel (dB) expresses a ratio of two powers: the loss in dB is 10 × log10(power in ÷ power out). Decibels add along a link. A connector pair of 0.5 dB and a fiber of 1.5 dB make 2.0 dB.

Loss in decibels and the share of the light that remains.

The dBm states a power level in decibels relative to one milliwatt (mW): 0 dBm is 1 mW, −10 dBm is 0.1 mW, and −20 dBm is 0.01 mW. The levels in fiber are usually below 1 mW, so readings are usually negative.

Working with dB and dBm

  • The level after a loss is the level before it minus the loss: dBm − dB = dBm.
  • The loss between two levels is their difference: dBm − dBm = dB.
  • Never add one dBm level to another. The sum has no meaning.

532.2.7 The one-jumper reference

Insertion loss is the difference between a stored reference level and the level that reaches the meter through the link.

A light source launches a steady signal at a chosen wavelength. A power meter set to the same wavelength measures what arrives. The pair measures insertion loss: the light a link removes from the path, in dB.

Setting the reference

In a one-jumper reference, also called a one-cord reference, you connect a single test cord, the reference jumper, from the source to the meter, and store the level it delivers. Depending on the meter, the display then reads 0.00 dB, or it shows the stored level in dBm. You then disconnect the jumper from the meter only and connect the link between the jumper and the meter. The meter now shows the link’s loss in dB, or a new level in dBm: subtract the reading from the reference to get the loss.

The reference was stored with only the jumper in the path. The result therefore contains everything added between the jumper and the meter: the fiber of the link, its splices, and the connections at its ends.

Figure — Setting a one-jumper reference, then measuring
Text description of this diagram

The diagram, titled Setting a one-jumper reference, then measuring, has two rows.

What protects the reference

  • Leave the jumper on the source. Unplugging and replugging it at the source changes how much light it takes in, which voids the reference. If it comes off, store a new reference.
  • Match the wavelengths. Set the source and the meter to the same wavelength for every reading. A mismatch gives a loss figure that does not measure the link.
  • Inspect and clean first. Inspect and clean every end face before it is mated, the jumper’s included. Clean the meter’s port as the meter’s manual directs; the One-Click fits its SC adapter where the manual allows it.
  • Match the fiber. The jumper must match the link’s fiber: a 62.5/125 jumper for an OM1 link.

The standards

ANSI/TIA-526-14 sets the methods for measuring installed multimode cabling, including the one-jumper reference and the way the source fills the multimode core. It does not cover single-mode fiber. For the owner’s single-mode backbone, clause F6 of the fiber addendum calls for a one-jumper reference, the method ANSI/TIA-568.3-D gives for single-mode cabling, with tests at 1310 nanometers (nm) and 1550 nm.

532.2.8 The loss budget and the call

A loss budget is the most loss a link may show and still pass, built up from an allowance for each part of the link.

The allowances

Loss allowances for a link budget: the ANSI/TIA-568.3-D maximums, which the owner’s fiber addendum (F6) adopts.
ComponentAllowance
Each mated connector pair0.75 dB
Each splice0.3 dB
Multimode fiber3.5 dB/km at 850 nm; 1.5 dB/km at 1300 nm
Single-mode fiber, inside plant1.0 dB/km at 1310 nm and at 1550 nm

Count each mated connector pair and each splice in the measured path. Convert a length in feet to kilometers by dividing by 3,281. The fiber’s share is its length in kilometers times its allowance at the wavelength tested. An allowance is a ceiling, not the loss a component is expected to show: a well-made connector pair loses less than 0.75 dB.

The call

The margin is the budget minus the measured loss. A link passes at a wavelength when its margin there is zero or greater, and fails when the margin is negative. A link tested at two wavelengths has two budgets, two margins, and two calls.

532.2.9 Checking an AI reading

An AI assistant can work a budget, a margin, and a call from the figures you give it. You check every figure it used against the meter and your own record.

Kestrel’s crews use Microsoft Copilot to interpret test results. An AI assistant takes no measurement. It works from what you type, and it can misread a unit, copy a figure wrongly, or apply the wrong allowance while presenting the result with confidence. The meter reading and your own budget are the record. Work your own budget and call before you ask the assistant, so that you have something to check it against.

What to check in a reply

  • The readings it used match the meter display for each wavelength, figure for figure.
  • The units are right: a dBm reading is a level, not a loss. A reply that treats a dBm reading as the loss is wrong.
  • The allowances are the ones you gave it, at the right wavelength, for the right number of connector pairs and splices.
  • The length is converted correctly from feet to kilometers.
  • The arithmetic of the budget and the margin is right, line by line.
  • The call follows from the margin: zero or greater passes.

Where the reply and your record differ, decide which is right, and state why. Where the error is yours, correct your record and note the change.

532.2.10 The visual fault locator

A visual fault locator (VFL) sends bright red light down a fiber. The light shows where it arrives and where it escapes.

A VFL is a red laser, commonly near 650 nm, with a port that commonly takes a 2.5 mm ferrule. Connected to one end of a fiber, it lights the far connector when the fiber is continuous from end to end. Where light escapes along the way, at a break, a tight bend, or a cracked fiber, the red glow shows through a buffer or a light-colored jacket. Many VFLs also flash, which helps pick one fiber out of several at the far end.

The limits

  • Red light fades quickly in fiber. The manufacturer states how far the VFL reaches.
  • A dark or armored jacket hides the glow.
  • A VFL measures no loss. A lit far connector shows that light gets through, not how much.

Using it safely

Say “light on” before you switch it on and “light off” after. Never look into the VFL’s port or into the end of a fiber it is connected to. Look at a lit connector from the side, and disconnect the VFL before you inspect an end face.

532.2.11 How an OTDR draws a trace

An optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) sends pulses of light into one end of a fiber and measures the light that comes back over time.

Glass scatters a small part of the light at every point along a fiber, and some of that scattered light travels back toward the instrument. This return, backscatter, weakens steadily with distance. At an abrupt change, such as a connector joint or the glass-to-air surface at the end of a fiber, a larger part of the light comes back at once: a reflection.

The OTDR times each return and converts the time to distance, using the fiber’s refractive index, which you set on the instrument from the cable’s data sheet. A wrong index setting puts every event at the wrong distance. The trace plots the returned level in dB against distance. Backscatter draws a line sloping down from left to right, its slope the fiber’s attenuation in dB/km; reflections stand up from it as spikes. The OTDR tests from one end of the fiber; a light source and power meter need both ends.

The software lists what it finds in an event table: each event’s type, distance, loss, and reflectance. The table is the software’s interpretation of the trace. Read it against the trace itself and against what you know of the link.

532.2.13 Pulse width and dead zones

The pulse width sets how far a trace reaches and how much fiber it cannot see after a strong reflection.

The pulse width is how long the OTDR’s laser stays on for each pulse, in nanoseconds (ns). A wider pulse carries more energy, so its backscatter stays above the noise farther down the fiber: the trace reaches farther. A wider pulse also covers a longer stretch of fiber at each instant. With a wider pulse, two events close together merge into one, and the receiver takes longer to recover after a strong reflection. A narrower pulse separates close events and shows detail near a reflection, but its trace turns ragged with noise sooner. Averaging many pulses reduces the noise, at the cost of a longer test.

Dead zones

A strong reflection briefly overloads the OTDR’s receiver. The event dead zone is the distance after a reflective event within which the OTDR cannot show a second event apart from it. The attenuation dead zone is the longer distance after it within which the OTDR cannot measure the loss of a second event. Both grow with the pulse width.

The instrument’s own connection causes the largest reflection, so the fiber nearest the instrument falls in a dead zone. A launch cable, a reel of fiber between the OTDR and the link, moves the link’s first connector past that dead zone, so the OTDR can read its loss. A receive cable at the far end adds fiber beyond the link’s last connector, so the OTDR can read that connector’s loss too.

532.2.14 Measuring a splice on a trace

A splice sits on a line that is already falling, so a measurement across it takes in some fiber loss as well.

In a two-point loss measurement, you place two markers on the trace, A before the splice and B after it, and read the drop in level between them. That drop includes the loss of the fiber between A and B, so it grows as the markers move apart. If B sits inside the drop, where the trace is still falling steeply, the reading can also miss part of the splice.

In splice loss mode with least squares approximation (LSA), the software fits a straight line to the fiber on each side of the splice and reads the gap between the two lines at the splice. The fitted lines follow the fiber’s slope, so the figure leaves out the fiber’s own loss and averages out the noise on single points. Place each fitted line on straight fiber, clear of the drop.

The two methods check each other: the two-point loss is about the LSA figure plus the distance between the markers times the fiber’s slope.

532.2.15 Ghosts

A ghost is an echo of a strong reflection: a spike on the trace where nothing in the fiber produced one.

Light reflected by a strong reflective event travels back toward the OTDR. Part of it reflects again at the instrument’s connector, travels out to the event, and returns a second time. Having made the trip twice, it arrives at about twice the time of the first return, so the OTDR plots it at about twice the event’s distance. Echoes of echoes can appear at three times the distance and beyond.

A tall spike at distance d is labeled Strong reflection at distance d. Farther on, a spike followed by a fall into noise is labeled End of fiber. In the noise beyond the end, a spike at distance 2d is labeled Ghost: spike, no step after it.

How to tell a ghost from an event

  • It shows a spike with no step after it: the trace carries on at the same level, because a ghost marks no loss.
  • Its distance is about a whole multiple, usually twice, of the distance of a strong reflection.
  • It can appear beyond the end of the fiber, in the noise, where no fiber exists.

An event table often stops at the end-of-fiber event, so a ghost beyond the end shows only on the trace itself. Check a suspect spike against the distances of the strong reflections before you record it as an event.

532.2.17 What you covered

An installer terminates and inspects a connector, measures a link against its budget, checks any AI reading against the meter, and reads the events on a trace.

Terminating a connector

Fit the boot first, strip to the connector’s length, clean the glass, and dry fit the fiber. Inject adhesive, prime the glass, seat the fiber, and leave it still until cured. Scribe the excess, air polish the nub on 12 µm film held in the hand, then puck polish on 3 µm and 0.5 µm, dry, in a figure-eight. On the mock-up, the end face clears when Zones A and B show no contamination and no defect. Polish again from the film that removes a polishing defect; terminate again where glass is missing or damaged.

Measuring insertion loss

The steps of an insertion-loss test and the rule that governs each.
StepRule
ReferenceOne jumper from source to meter; the jumper stays on the source; same wavelength at both
LossThe dB reading, or, with readings in dBm, the reference level minus the reading
Budget0.75 dB for each mated connector pair, 0.3 dB for each splice, plus the fiber’s dB/km times its length

AI checkReadings, units, allowances, length, arithmetic, and call, each against the meter and your own record

ANSI/TIA-526-14 sets the one-jumper method for multimode cabling. For the single-mode backbone, clause F6 of the owner’s fiber addendum sets it, citing ANSI/TIA-568.3-D.

Reading an OTDR trace

Backscatter draws the sloping line; reflections draw spikes. A connector is a spike then a step; a fusion splice is a step with no spike; the end of the fiber is a spike, then noise. A wider pulse reaches farther and hides more after a strong reflection; a narrower pulse resolves close events. Two-point loss includes the fiber between the markers; the LSA figure leaves it out. A ghost is a spike with no step, at about twice the distance of a strong reflection. Each instrument answers one question.
