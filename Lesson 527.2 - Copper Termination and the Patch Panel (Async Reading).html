The owner accepts cabling one permanent link at a time.

On the mock-up, each outlet cable runs from its box in the wall, up the cavity, and along the cable ladder to the Mobile Infrastructure Rack (MIR). There it drops to its team’s patch panel, a panel of numbered ports. The owner’s cabling addendum names the pieces of that path.

527.2.2 Twisted-pair cable and its markings

A four-pair cable carries its own description on the jacket: its category, its conductor size, its fire rating, and its footage marks.

Four pairs, and why they are twisted

A data cable for an outlet holds eight insulated copper conductors in four pairs. Each pair is two conductors twisted together: one solid-colored and one white with a stripe of the same color. The pairs are blue, orange, green, and brown, so the conductors are named blue and white-blue, orange and white-orange, and so on.

Each pair carries its signal as the difference between its two conductors. Noise from outside the cable, and from the signals on the other pairs, reaches both conductors of a tightly twisted pair almost equally, so the difference between them hardly changes. The four pairs are twisted at different rates, so that no two pairs run side by side for long. Signal that leaks from one pair into another is crosstalk. Wherever a pair is untwisted, at a termination or at a damaged point, crosstalk rises.

Category

A cable’s category is the performance grade it is built and tested to. The owner’s standard calls for Category 6 cable in the production building; for the mock-up, the cabling addendum (A2) sets Category 5e. Plugs, jacks, and patch panels carry categories too. A link built from parts of different categories is rated no higher than its lowest-rated part.

Reading the jacket

The jacket print states, among other things:

  • the category, for example CAT 6;
  • the construction, for example Unscreened Unshielded Twisted-Pair (U/UTP) cable, which has no foil or braid around the cable or around any pair;
  • the number of pairs, for example 4PR;
  • the conductor size in American Wire Gauge (AWG), for example 23 AWG;
  • the fire rating: Communications Riser (CMR) for cable rated for vertical runs between floors, or Communications Plenum (CMP) for cable rated for air-handling spaces;
  • a footage mark, a printed length in feet, repeated every foot.

In AWG, a smaller number means a thicker conductor: 22 AWG is thicker than 24 AWG. Footage marks count up along the cable on some boxes and down on others. The length of a piece is the difference between the marks at its two ends: a piece cut at 0846 and at 0836 is 10 ft long, whichever way the marks run.

Solid and stranded conductors

Solid and stranded conductors compared.
ConductorWhat it isWhere it is used

527.2.3 Matching the plug, the jack, and the panel to the cable

Before you terminate anything, compare the cable’s markings with the manufacturer’s figures for each part.

The manufacturer’s package or data sheet states what each part accepts. On the mock-up, a product card for each part carries those figures. Read four things:

  • Category. The categories the plug or jack is rated for.
  • Conductor type. Solid, stranded, or both. A plug’s contacts are shaped for one type or made to suit both; a punch-down slot holds a solid conductor and may not hold a stranded one.
  • Conductor size. A range in AWG, for example 26–24 AWG.
  • Format. For a patch panel, the jack it takes: a keystone panel takes any keystone jack of the series its card names.

A plug or a jack fits a cable when the cable’s category, its conductor type, and its conductor size are all among what the part accepts. A jack fits a panel when the panel takes the jack’s format. Where the package gives no figure for an item, record “not given”; do not supply one from memory.

527.2.4 T568B and T568A

A termination scheme assigns each conductor to a pin. Both ends of every link and every cord follow one scheme, so pin 1 at one end reaches pin 1 at the other.

An RJ-45 plug and jack have eight contacts, pins 1 to 8. Hold a plug with its latch down, its contacts up, and the cable toward you: pin 1 is on the left. The Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA) standard ANSI/TIA-568 gives two assignments, T568A and T568B. The owner’s cabling addendum (A1) sets T568B for every jack, panel port, and patch cord on the mock-up.

Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled Pin numbers and the T568B and T568A assignments. A note under the title reads: Plug held latch down, contacts up, cable toward you: pin 1 on the left.

Eight boxes in a row are numbered 1 to 8 from left to right. Under them, the T568B row gives each pin’s conductor: pin 1 white-orange, pin 2 orange, pin 3 white-green, pin 4 blue, pin 5 white-blue, pin 6 green, pin 7 white-brown, pin 8 brown.

Brackets under the T568B row join pins 1 and 2 as the orange pair, pins 4 and 5 as the blue pair, and pins 7 and 8 as the brown pair. A longer dashed bracket joins pins 3 and 6, passing around pins 4 and 5, labeled green pair, pins 3 and 6 (around 4 and 5).

A lower row, in gray, gives T568A: pin 1 white-green, pin 2 green, pin 3 white-orange, pin 4 blue, pin 5 white-blue, pin 6 orange, pin 7 white-brown, pin 8 brown.

A note at the bottom reads: T568A puts the green pair on pins 1 and 2 and the orange pair on pins 3 and 6. The blue and brown pairs do not move.

Pins 3 and 6 carry one pair, whose conductors sit either side of the blue pair. On a plug, laying the conductors in pin order, rather than pair by pair, puts green on pin 6, not on pin 4.

A cord terminated to the same scheme at both ends is a straight-through cord: each pin reaches the same pin at the far end. A cord with T568A at one end and T568B at the other swaps the orange and green pairs between its ends. On a link that must be straight-through, that swap is a fault, whichever end used the wrong scheme.

527.2.5 Stripping and the pair twist

Every termination starts with a square cut and a stripped jacket, and ends with each pair twisted as close to its contacts as the part allows.

Stripping the jacket

Cut the cable end square with the cable cutter. A cable stripper has a blade set to cut the jacket’s thickness: close it on the cable, turn it once around the jacket, and pull the jacket off. The blade scores the jacket without reaching the conductors. A utility knife has no depth stop, so it can cut into the conductors’ insulation under the jacket.

The strip length is the length of jacket removed. The manufacturer of the plug or jack sets it, because the strip length decides where the jacket ends: inside a plug, or at the back of a jack. The owner’s cabling addendum (A3) gives no figure.

Look at the cut edge of the jacket and at each conductor’s insulation before you go on. A conductor with nicked or cut insulation can short to its neighbor. A nicked solid conductor can break when it is bent into the part. If you find a nick, cut the end off square and strip again.

The pair twist

Untwist each pair only as far as the part needs. The owner’s cabling addendum (A3) requires each pair to stay twisted to within 0.5 in (13 mm) of its termination, the limit ANSI/TIA-568 sets for Category 5e. Long untwisted pairs at a termination add crosstalk that the cable itself does not have.

For the same reason, the jacket reaches the back of the jack or sits inside the plug, so that the pairs keep their twist and their position up to the termination. On the mock-up, no more than ~1/4 in of pairs may show between the jacket and the back of a jack.

Untwisting by spinning the cable

Spinning the cable end to loosen its pairs untwists them along the jacket as well, out of sight. Separate each pair by hand, from the end back toward the jacket.

Lesson 527.2 · Section 2 Terminating Copper

527.2.6 Crimping an RJ-45 plug

A crimped plug makes contact with all eight conductors and holds the cable by its jacket.

The parts of the plug

  • Contacts. Eight flat contacts sit in the plug, raised until the plug is crimped. The crimper drives each one down through a conductor’s insulation to make contact with the copper.
  • Jacket crimp. A bar or wedge in the plug body that the crimper presses onto the cable jacket. It holds the cable, so that a pull on the cord does not pull on the conductors.
  • Latch. The clip on the underside that locks the plug in a jack.

Two plug designs

On a standard plug, you trim the conductors straight across to the manufacturer’s length before you insert them. They stop inside the plug against its front wall. On a pass-through plug, the conductors pass out through the front of the plug; the crimper’s blade trims them flush as it crimps, or you trim them afterward, as the manufacturer sets out. Check which kind you hold, and whether your crimper trims pass-through plugs, before you terminate the first end.

Terminating one end

  1. Strip to the plug’s strip length and inspect the conductors.
  2. Untwist the pairs only as far as you need, and lay the conductors flat and side by side in T568B order, pin 1 to pin 8.
  3. On a standard plug, trim the conductors straight across to the manufacturer’s length. On a pass-through plug, leave them long and trim them after insertion, as the manufacturer sets out.
  4. With the latch down, slide the conductors into the plug until each reaches the front. The jacket must end inside the plug, under the jacket crimp.
  5. Check the order through the clear plug body, from the side and from above.
  6. Crimp fully: a ratcheting crimper releases only at the end of its stroke.

What to look at

Look at the plug from the side. All eight contacts should sit level, pressed down to the same depth. A contact that stands higher than the others may not have reached its conductor; the wiremap tester then shows an open on that pin. Look along the conductors through the body: the order should match T568B at the front of the plug, where the contacts are.

527.2.7 Punching down a keystone jack

You terminate a solid cable on a keystone jack without stripping its conductors. The jack then snaps into a faceplate, a cover, or a patch panel.

The termination block

The back of a punch-down keystone jack carries a termination block with eight slots. Each slot is an insulation displacement contact (IDC): when a conductor is pressed into it, the slot’s two edges cut through the insulation and grip the copper. The conductor keeps its insulation up to the slot.

A color label beside the slots shows two rows, one for T568A and one for T568B. The slot order on the block is usually not the pin order. Manufacturers arrange the slots so that the two conductors of each pair land side by side, and the arrangement differs from one jack to another. Follow the B row of the label on the jack in your hand, not the pin table and not the layout of another jack. A few jacks terminate without a punch-down tool, by a cap that presses the conductors into place; follow the manufacturer’s instructions for those.

The punch-down tool

A punch-down tool with a 110 blade seats a conductor in a 110-style slot. One side of the blade cuts. Hold the cutting side toward the end of the conductor, away from the jack, so that the tool trims the waste end as it seats the conductor. With the cutting side toward the jack, the tool cuts the conductor between the slot and the cable, which leaves only the waste end in the slot.

Terminating a jack

  1. Strip to the jack’s strip length and inspect the conductors.
  2. Lay each pair into its two slots by the B row of the label, keeping the pair twisted to within 0.5 in of its slots and the jacket at the back of the jack.
  3. Seat each conductor with the punch-down tool, cutting side out.
  4. Check that each conductor sits at the bottom of its slot and that no trimmed end sticks out.
  5. Fit the jack’s cap, if it has one, and snap the jack into its faceplate, cover, or panel port until it latches.

527.2.8 What a wiremap tester reports

A wiremap tester checks each of the eight conductors from one end to the other. Where a conductor does not reach its pin at the far end or touches another conductor, the tester names the fault and the pins.

The tester has a main unit and a remote. One goes at each end of the cord or link. The tester checks each conductor between the two units and shows the result as a map of pins 1 to 8 at each end, with a pass or the name of a fault and the pins involved. A separate length test reports the length of each pair. It runs from the main unit alone, with nothing plugged into the far end: no remote and no other device. Where any short exists, the wiremap screen of the tester on the mock-up shows only “Short”, whatever else is wrong.

What a wiremap test cannot see

In a split pair, every pin reaches the same pin at the far end, but two pins that should share one twisted pair are on conductors of two different pairs. The same mistake at both ends causes it: for example, both ends putting pins 3 and 4 on one twisted pair and pins 5 and 6 on another, instead of 3 with 6 and 4 with 5. A tester that checks only continuity and pin mapping shows a pass. The owner’s cabling addendum (A7) records the limit: a wiremap and length test verifies a link; it does not certify it. Certification, under A8, uses a certification tester.

527.2.9 Testing a cord and a permanent link

A cord plugs straight into the tester. A permanent link is tested from its outlet jack to its panel port, with a tester lead, a short patch cable, at each end.

A patch cord

Plug one end of the cord into the main unit and the other into the remote, and run the wiremap test. Record the result as the tester shows it: “pass,” or the fault and the pins the tester names. Record every attempt, not only the last one.

A permanent link

A permanent link ends on two jacks, so the tester needs a tester lead at each end. Put the remote on the panel port and the main unit on the outlet jack, or the other way around; the direction does not change the map. The owner’s cabling addendum (A7) sets the pass: all eight conductors continuous, each reaching the same pin at the far end and connected to no other conductor, with a length not over 295 ft (90 m). For the length test, remove the remote and leave nothing plugged into the far end. The tester on the mock-up measures from ~8 ft (2.5 m) to ~656 ft (200 m) and shows 0 outside that range. Record the wiremap result and the length as the tester shows them.

When a test fails

Read the fault and the pins. Then look at the terminations:

  • A reversed pair or a crossed pair is wrong at one end only. Compare each end with its scheme: through the plug body for a cord, or against the jack’s color label for a link. Re-terminate the end that does not match.
  • An open or a short can sit at either end or in the cable. Look for a conductor not at the bottom of its slot, a contact standing high, nicked insulation, or a trimmed end touching its neighbor.

Re-terminate a plug by cutting the cable square, as close to the plug as you can, and terminating that end again. Re-terminate a jack with a new jack. Retest after each change, and record what you re-terminated and the new result. If you cannot find the fault, tell the site lead.

Lesson 527.2 · Section 4 The Patch Panel at the Rack

527.2.10 Patch panels and the rack

A patch panel gathers the permanent links of an area in numbered ports at the rack, where patch cords connect them to equipment.

Two kinds of panel

Keystone panel

  • A frame of empty ports
  • Each cable is terminated on a keystone jack, which snaps into its port
  • A faulty port is repaired with a new jack

Punch-down panel

  • Fixed jacks, with a punch-down block on the back of each port
  • Each cable is punched down on the panel itself
  • The ports cannot be removed one at a time

On a keystone panel, the jack goes into its port from the back or the front, as the panel’s manufacturer sets out. The panel’s product card says which, and where its port labels go.

The rack

A standard equipment rack takes equipment 19 in wide, mounted between its vertical rails. Height in a rack is counted in rack units (U): 1U is 1.75 in. A 48-port panel is commonly 2U high, with its ports in two rows of 24.

A team’s ports on a shared panel

Where several teams terminate on one panel, the rack sheet, the posted list of what goes where in the rack, gives each team a block of consecutive ports. A mark above the first port of the block carries its identifier. The port numbers of the owner’s port table count from that mark. The panel’s own numbers run on across every block.

Installing a jack in its port

  1. Find your block from the rack sheet and its mark, then count from the first port of the block to each of your ports.
  2. Snap each terminated jack into its port, from the side the panel’s product card shows, until it latches flush with the panel face.
  3. Pull each jack gently from the front: a seated jack stays latched.

The rack is shared. Move, unplug, or switch off nothing that is not yours; if equipment is in the way, tell the site lead.

527.2.11 Dressing cable to the panel

Dressing is laying each cable along a set route from the cable ladder to its port and tying the cables of that route into a bundle, so that the next technician can trace and work on any one of them.

The route

Cables leave the ladder at the rack and come down through cable managers and D-rings. Cable managers are the troughs and fingers mounted beside or between the panels; D-rings are open rings that guide cable down the rack. Each cable follows the route its team is given, without crossing another bundle, to the back of its own port. The 10 ft service loop of the owner’s cabling addendum (A5) stays coiled on the ladder: it is spare cable for a later re-termination or a move, not slack to dress into the rack.

Bend radius

The owner’s cabling addendum (A4) sets a minimum bend radius of four times the cable’s outside diameter. A tighter bend flattens the cable and changes the spacing and twist of its pairs, which adds crosstalk and reflects part of the signal. The limit is a radius. A bend gauge, the round object that checks it, has a diameter of twice that radius.

Figure — Bend radius and the gauge that checks it
Text description of this diagram

A note at the bottom right reads: A bend that follows the gauge is at the limit. A bend tighter than the gauge fails.

Ties

The owner’s cabling addendum (A4) allows hook-and-loop ties only, tightened by hand, with no cable jacket deformed. A tie pulled tight presses the jacket out of round and changes the pairs’ geometry at that point, as a tight bend does. Tighten each tie until the bundle holds its shape, and stop before any jacket is pressed in. Do not support a cable from another service, such as a pipe or another trade’s bracket.

527.2.12 Labeling the port, the outlet, and the cable

Each end of a permanent link carries a label that names the other end, so that a technician at the outlet can find the port and a technician at the rack can find the outlet.

The owner’s schemes

In the production building, the owner’s standard (27 05 53 2.1) identifies a telecommunications room by floor and letter, a patch panel by room and number, and an outlet by its panel and port. Outlet 2A-PP01-014 is port 014 of panel PP01 in room 2A. Labels are machine-printed and fixed at both ends of every cable.

On the mock-up, each outlet has an identifier built from its wall, face, bay, and outlet code. The owner’s cabling addendum (A6) assigns each outlet a port in its wall’s block, in a fixed order by bay and outlet, and sets three labels for each link:

Lesson 527.2 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

527.2.13 What you covered

A permanent link is terminated to T568B at its outlet and at its panel port, dressed, labeled at both ends, and verified with a wiremap test.

The link and its parts

The permanent link runs from the outlet jack to the panel port and includes both terminations; patch cords are not part of it. A cable’s jacket gives its category, its conductor size in AWG (a smaller number is a thicker conductor), its fire rating, and its footage marks. Permanent links use solid conductor. A plug or jack fits a cable when its category, conductor type, and size range all include the cable’s; the manufacturer’s figures decide the fit.

Terminating copper

T568B: white-orange, orange, white-green, blue, white-blue, green, white-brown, brown, pins 1 to 8. Strip to the manufacturer’s length without nicking a conductor. Keep each pair twisted to within 0.5 in of its termination. On a plug, the jacket sits under the jacket crimp and all eight contacts press down level. On a keystone jack, follow the B row of the jack’s own label. Seat each conductor with the cutting side of the 110 blade toward the waste end.

Wiremap testing

A wiremap tester names an open, a short, a reversed pair, or a crossed pair, with the pins. A reversed or crossed pair is wrong at one end: re-terminate the end that does not match its scheme. A split pair passes a tester that checks only continuity and pin mapping; a certification tester finds it.
