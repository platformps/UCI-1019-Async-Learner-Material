Lesson 530.2 — Pathways, Bonding, Firestop and Code

What holds the cable up, what ties the metalwork together, which cable the code allows where, and how a hole through a fire-rated wall is closed again.

On Level 2 of the Harlow Street Operations Center, a 12 in ladder tray runs the length of the corridor above the ceiling, and the corridor walls on both sides are 1-hour fire rated. Every cable bound for an office leaves that tray, passes through one of those walls and continues on J-hooks to its outlet. Before Kestrel Communications’ crew hangs the first J-hook, the foreman confirms what supports the cable along each stretch and how the tray and racks are bonded. The foreman also confirms which cable type the code and the owner allow in that ceiling, and which tested firestop system closes each opening in the wall.

This lesson covers each of those subjects in turn. The first section covers cable supports and the limits on spacing, sag, fill and separation, with the raised-floor and overhead pathways of an equipment space. The second covers the bonding infrastructure, from the busbar in the equipment room to the conductor on each rack. The third covers the electrical code: how it is adopted, how its articles are arranged, the cable ratings for plenum, riser and general-purpose spaces, and how to find and cite a section. The fourth covers fire-rated assemblies and the firestop systems that restore their rating.

The worked examples use a hypothetical building, the Mercer Avenue Clinic, with its own owner standard and its own numbers.

A note on the standards and the code

TIA-569, from the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA), covers pathways and spaces. TIA-607 covers telecommunications bonding and grounding. NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code (NEC), published by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA), is the electrical code most United States jurisdictions adopt into law. The standards describe good practice; the code, once adopted, is the legal minimum. An owner standard may require more than either.

Section numbers in the code move between editions. The jurisdiction adopts an edition, the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) enforces it, and the job follows that edition. Where this page names an article or a section, it does so for the 2023 edition; the 2026 edition has been published and renumbers many communications rules. Check which edition applies to the job before you cite anything. NFPA offers free read-only access to its codes at nfpa.org once you create a free account.

530.2.1 Holding the cable up

Lesson 530.2

Pathways, Bonding, Firestop and Code

Cable runs from room to room on supports chosen for each stretch of the route. The metal of those supports and of the racks is bonded together. The electrical code decides which cable type may be installed in each space. Every opening the route makes in a fire-rated wall or floor is closed with a tested firestop system. This lesson covers the supports, the bonding, the code and the firestop, in that order.

A route from a telecommunications room to an outlet passes through several kinds of space, and each kind of space has its own support.

A pathway is whatever carries the cable along its route: a tray, a run of J-hooks, a conduit. TIA-569, from the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA), sets out the pathway types and their limits. The designer chooses one for each stretch of the route; the drawings show which. Ladder rack, cable tray, J-hooks, conduit and surface raceway cover most of the work in an office building.

The choice follows the space. A long shared route in an accessible ceiling takes tray; a branch to a room takes J-hooks; any stretch where the cable will be hidden in the building’s construction takes conduit. A support never rests on, or hangs from, the ceiling grid: the grid is built to carry tiles, not cable. Cable laid on it pushes the tiles down and is disturbed every time a tile is lifted.

Match each route to the support that suits it.

530.2.2 Spacing, sag, fill and separation

A pathway is checked for how far apart its supports are, how far the cable droops between them, how full each support is, and how far it stays from sources of interference.

Spacing and sag

J-hooks are hung at a stated maximum spacing, measured center to center. TIA-569 sets a maximum of 5 ft (1.5 m) for non-continuous supports, and an owner standard may set a shorter one. Between two hooks the bundle sags; an owner standard commonly limits that sag, for example to 12 in at mid-span. Cable pulled so tight that it does not sag is under tension it should not carry. A bundle that droops onto the ceiling tiles hangs from hooks too far apart.

Counting the J-hooks for a branch

The number of hooks follows from the length of the branch and the maximum spacing. Divide the length by the spacing and round the number of spans up to a whole number. Then decide where the first support is. Where the branch starts at a wall sleeve, the sleeve carries the cable at that end. The first hook sits one span along, and each span ends at a hook.

Figure — A 22 ft branch at 5 ft maximum spacing (Mercer Avenue Clinic, hypothetical)
Text description of this diagram

From the sleeve, a plum cable runs right, sagging between supports. Five J-hooks, numbered 1 to 5, hang on rods from the structure at equal spacing; hook 5 is at the far end of the branch.

The arithmetic for that branch: 22 ft ÷ 5 ft = 4.4 spans, rounded up to 5 spans. The sleeve supports the start, so the branch takes 5 J-hooks, one at the end of each span. The actual spacing is 22 ft ÷ 5, about 4 ft 5 in, inside the 5 ft maximum. A shorter maximum spacing can add hooks to the same branch.

Fill

Every support has a capacity. A J-hook is rated by its manufacturer for a number of cables of a stated type; the rating is printed in its product data. A cable tray is limited to a percentage of its cross-section, commonly 50 % by owner standards, so that cables lie without crushing the bottom layer and there is room for more cable. Conduit fill is calculated from the number and diameter of the cables, against a limit on how much of the conduit’s cross-section they may occupy. A route carrying more cables than one hook holds needs a larger hook, a second run of hooks, or tray.

Separation from interference

Copper data cable picks up interference from electrical equipment that produces strong magnetic fields: motors, transformers, fluorescent light ballasts and power cables. The owner standard sets a minimum distance from each source. Distances on drawings are read from the plan, the section and the electrical plan together: a cable route that clears a transformer on the floor plan can still pass beside it in the ceiling space above.

530.2.3 Raised-floor and overhead pathways

In an equipment room or a data hall, cable runs either under a raised floor or overhead, and each route shares its space with something else that has a prior claim on it.

A raised floor is a grid of removable tiles on pedestals, standing a foot or more above the concrete slab. An overhead pathway is tray or ladder rack hung from the structure or supported on top of the cabinets. Many rooms use both: power under the floor and data overhead, or the reverse.

Under a raised floor

  • The space is often the cooling supply: cold air is pushed through it to perforated tiles in front of the equipment. Cable bundles block that air.
  • Cable runs in tray or wire basket on the slab or on the pedestals, in defined routes, kept out from under perforated tiles.
  • Every tile cut-out for cable is fitted with a brush or grommet seal so cold air does not escape through it.
  • Technicians lift tiles to reach the cable, so a route cannot run under equipment that sits on the tiles above it.
  • Article 645 of the electrical code covers information technology equipment rooms. Its rules for cable under the floor apply only where the room meets the article’s conditions.

Overhead

  • The space is shared with lighting, sprinkler pipes and heads, ducts and return air. The tray keeps the clearance below sprinkler heads that the fire protection drawings require, so that the spray is not blocked.
  • The tray is supported from structure or from the cabinet tops, never from a ceiling grid.
  • Access is from a ladder or a lift, so the route needs working clearance above and beside the tray.
  • Where the space above a ceiling carries return air, the cable must be rated for that space.
  • Cable drops from the tray to each cabinet at a defined point, usually with a waterfall fitting that keeps the bend radius.

On a drawing, a clash between a pathway and something else in the same space shows only when the sheets are read together. The telecommunications plan gives the tray route and its elevation, the mechanical plan the ducts and the air-handling space, the fire protection plan the sprinkler mains, and the electrical plan the light fixtures. A section through the corridor or the hall, where the set has one, shows them stacked.

Lesson 530.2 · Section 2 Bonding and Grounding

530.2.4 Bonding and grounding

Grounding connects a system to the earth. Bonding connects metal parts to each other so they stay at the same voltage. A telecommunications bonding system does the second and connects to the building’s ground at one point.

Grounding is the connection of an electrical system to the earth, through the building’s grounding electrode system: ground rods, the metal water pipe where it enters, the building steel, a concrete-encased electrode. The electrical contractor installs it at the service equipment, where power enters the building.

Bonding is the connection of metal parts to each other with conductors, so that a fault or a surge raises all of them together and no voltage appears between two parts a person could touch at the same time. Racks, cabinets, ladder rack, cable tray, the shields of shielded cable and the protectors on incoming copper are all bonded.

TIA-607 sets out the telecommunications bonding system: busbars in the equipment room and each telecommunications room, conductors between them, and conductors from each rack and pathway to the nearest busbar. The electrical code sets the minimum where telecommunications bonding meets the building’s electrical system. Article 250 covers grounding and bonding in general, including the terminal at the service where other systems connect. The communications articles in Chapter 8 cover the grounding of the protectors on incoming cable. An owner standard usually adopts TIA-607 and adds its own sizes.

A bonding conductor is not a power conductor

Bonding conductors carry current only during a fault or a surge. They are sized, routed and terminated to the bonding standard, not to a load: as straight and short as the route allows, with no sharp bends, and with listed compression lugs or exothermic welds rather than twisted connections.

530.2.5 From the primary busbar to the rack

A telecommunications bonding system has one busbar in the equipment room, one in each telecommunications room, a backbone conductor joining them, and a conductor from every rack to its room’s busbar.

Recent editions of TIA-607 renamed two of the busbars. Drawings and owner standards use both sets of names.

Further right are three more racks, joined to each other by short red links, with one red conductor leaving the first rack. They are labeled daisy-chained: not acceptable. A note under them reads: Lifting the first rack’s conductor on the right would leave all three racks unbonded.

No daisy-chaining

Each rack, cabinet, ladder rack section and tray section is bonded to the SBB by its own conductor. Racks are not bonded to each other in series, rack to rack to rack, because disconnecting one link for a move or a repair then removes the bond from every rack beyond it. Where many cabinets stand in a row, a common practice is a bonding conductor along the row with a separate tap to each cabinet, so that no cabinet’s bond passes through another cabinet.

Sizing the bonding backbone

The TBB is sized by its length, because a longer conductor needs a larger cross-section to keep its impedance down. American Wire Gauge (AWG) numbers run backwards: the larger the number, the smaller the wire, and the sizes larger than 1 AWG are written 1/0, 2/0 and 3/0. TIA-607 gives a table of length bands, beginning at 6 AWG for the shortest backbone and reaching 3/0 AWG for the longest; owner standards usually reproduce it. To size a TBB, take its length from the drawings or a stated figure, find the band in the owner’s table, and read the minimum size. A drawn conductor larger than the minimum complies; a smaller one does not.

At the Mercer Avenue Clinic, the TBB from the PBB to the SBB in TR 3A is stated as 30 ft. The clinic’s owner table has a band of 26–33 ft at 2 AWG, so the minimum is 2 AWG, and the 2 AWG drawn on its riser complies. For comparison, 4 AWG is smaller than 2 AWG, and 1/0 AWG is larger than 1 AWG.

Lesson 530.2 · Section 3 The Electrical Code

530.2.6 How the code is built and adopted

The National Electrical Code is a model document until a state or city adopts it into law, and the edition it adopts is the one that applies to the job.

NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code (NEC), is published by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) every three years; the three most recent are 2020, 2023 and 2026. On its own it is a model code. It becomes law when a state, county or city adopts it, often with local amendments, and often an edition or two behind the latest. The authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) — usually the local building or electrical inspection department — enforces the edition that was adopted and interprets it where the text leaves a question. A project’s general notes or specification name the code and edition the work complies with; where they do not, the AHJ can say which edition is in force.

How the code is arranged

The arrangement of NFPA 70, 2023 edition, as it bears on telecommunications work.
Part of the codeWhat it holds
Article 90Introduction: purpose, scope, and how the code is arranged
Article 100Definitions used throughout, such as plenum and abandoned cable
Chapters 1–4General rules: wiring methods in Chapter 3 (Article 300), grounding and bonding in Chapter 2 (Article 250)
Chapters 5–7Special occupancies, equipment and conditions: Article 645 for information technology equipment rooms and Article 770 for optical fiber cables
Chapter 8Communications systems: Article 800 general requirements, Article 805 communications circuits, with further articles for radio, television and broadband

Chapter 8 stands apart. It is not subject to the rules in Chapters 1 through 7 except where a Chapter 8 section refers to one of them, so a general wiring rule applies to a communications cable only where the communications articles bring it in. The 2020 and 2023 editions reorganized this part of the code: rules common to all communications systems were gathered into Article 800, the former communications article was renumbered as Article 805, and some listing requirements moved in the 2023 edition. The 2026 edition has since been published and renumbers many communications rules again. A section number memorized from one edition may be somewhere else in another, so check which edition applies to the job before you look anything up.

Articles, parts and sections

Each article opens with its scope (section 1) and usually its definitions, and is divided into parts by subject. A section number is the article number, a point and the section: 300.1 is the scope of Article 300. Subsections are lettered and numbered below that, as in 300.1(A)(1). A section holds text of different weight:

  • Requirements, using shall or shall not, are enforceable.
  • Exceptions, printed directly below a requirement, relax it in stated conditions and are enforceable.
  • Informational notes explain or point to other documents. They are not requirements.

The building code is a separate document. Most jurisdictions adopt the International Building Code (IBC), whose Chapter 7 governs fire-resistance-rated walls and floors and how openings through them are protected. The electrical code requires openings made for electrical and communications wiring to be firestopped; the building code sets how a rated assembly is built and protected.

Lesson 530.2 · Section 3 The Electrical Code

530.2.7 Plenum, riser and general-purpose cable

The code lists communications and optical fiber cables by how they behave in a fire, and the space a cable runs through decides which listing it needs.

A cable jacket burns and gives off smoke. The code requires cables to be listed by fire test and permits each listing only in the spaces where its behavior is acceptable. The cable type is printed on the jacket every few feet. The letters break down as follows: CM is communications cable; OFN is optical fiber, nonconductive (no metal in the cable). A letter added after CM or OFN gives the rating: P, R or G, and for copper also X.

Cable ratings for communications and nonconductive optical fiber cable, highest first.

PlenumCMPOFNPSpaces used for environmental air, such as a ceiling space that carries return air; and anywhere a lower rating is permitted

Substitution goes upward only

A higher rating may be installed wherever a lower one is permitted: CMP may replace CMR, CMR may replace CMG or CM, and so on down the table. The reverse is not permitted. The same order holds for optical fiber: OFNP, then OFNR, then OFNG and OFN. A conductive optical fiber cable (OFC, with metal in it) may not replace a nonconductive one.

Reading the space, not the ceiling

The rating a horizontal cable needs depends on whether the space it runs through carries air. A ceiling space where return grilles open into the void, with no return duct, is a space used for environmental air, called a plenum in everyday trade speech. Cable in it must be plenum rated unless it is in a raceway the code permits there. The same ceiling with a ducted return is not a plenum; general-purpose cable may be permitted there. You tell whether a ceiling space carries air from the mechanical drawings and by looking above the tiles, not from the ceiling’s appearance. Article 300 carries the general rule for wiring in spaces used for air handling, and the communications and optical fiber articles carry the rules for their cables in those spaces.

An owner standard may require more than the code: a rule that horizontal cable is riser rated as a minimum everywhere, for example, or plenum rated throughout. The stricter of the two applies.

Abandoned cable

The 2023 edition defines abandoned cable as installed cable that is not terminated at both ends at equipment and is not identified for future use with a tag. The communications and optical fiber articles require the accessible portion of abandoned cable to be removed, because it adds fuel to a ceiling space and serves no purpose. Cable kept for later use is tagged as such. On a job that replaces old cabling, removing the abandoned cable is part of the work.

Lesson 530.2 · Section 3 The Electrical Code

530.2.8 Finding and citing a section

Start from the subject, narrow to the article, read its scope and parts, and cite the section with its edition.

A code question on site sounds like “which cable can go above this ceiling” or “what do we do with the old cable”. The answer is a section of the code. A written answer names it so that an inspector, a designer or the next technician can check it.

Finding the section

  1. Name the subject and the cable or equipment. A copper data cable carries a communications circuit; a fiber cable falls under the optical fiber article; a power circuit to a rack is general wiring. That decides the chapter.
  2. Open the table of contents and pick the article. Chapter 8 for communications, Article 770 for optical fiber, Chapters 2 and 3 for bonding and general wiring methods, Chapter 6 for an information technology equipment room.
  3. Read the article’s scope (its section 1) to confirm the article covers your case, then read the titles of its parts and sections to find the one on your subject.
  4. Read the whole section, including its exceptions, and follow any reference it makes to another article. Read the definition in Article 100 of any term the section turns on.
  5. Check the edition. Confirm you are reading the edition the jurisdiction adopted.
Searching the free online code

NFPA’s free access at nfpa.org shows the code read-only after you create a free account, and lets you choose the edition. A keyword search finds every use of a word, including in informational notes and unrelated articles. Use it to find candidates, then confirm each one against the article’s scope before you rely on it.

A worked search

Question: does a general wiring rule in Chapter 3 apply to a communications cable? Subject: how the code’s chapters relate to each other. Article: the introduction, Article 90, whose sections cover purpose, scope and arrangement. Section: 90.3, the section describing the code’s arrangement. Its content: Chapters 1 through 4 apply generally, Chapters 5 through 7 supplement or modify them for special cases, and Chapter 8 is not subject to Chapters 1 through 7 except where a Chapter 8 section refers to them. Answer: only where a Chapter 8 section refers to that Chapter 3 rule.

Lesson 530.2 · Section 4 Fire-Rated Assemblies and Firestop

530.2.9 Fire-rated assemblies on a drawing

A fire-rated wall or floor holds back a fire for a stated time, and every hole through it has to be closed so that it still does.

A fire-rated assembly is a wall, floor or ceiling built to a tested design that resists fire for a stated time: 1 hour, 2 hours. The building code decides which walls and floors are rated: corridors, stairs and shafts, walls between occupancies, and in a multi-storey building usually every floor slab. A cable pathway through a rated assembly is a penetration. The building code and the electrical code both require each one to be sealed with an approved method that restores the assembly’s rating. The drawings say which method.

Finding the rated assemblies

  • The legend. A rated wall is drawn with its own line type — a heavier line, a hatched or dashed core, a colored edge — defined in the legend with its rating.
  • The architectural plans and wall types. Each wall carries a type tag; the wall type schedule gives its construction and rating.
  • Life safety plans. Many sets include a code or life safety plan (often G- or LS- sheets) showing every rated wall and its hours.
  • Notes and tags. A sheet note such as “corridor walls are 1-hour rated” or a firestop tag at a crossing marks the assembly where it meets the pathway.
  • On site. Rated walls above the ceiling are often stenciled with their rating, and run to the underside of the structure rather than stopping at the ceiling.
Through penetrations and membrane penetrations

A through penetration passes all the way through the assembly: a sleeve through a wall, a cable bundle through a slab. A membrane penetration opens only one face: an outlet box set into one side of a rated wall. Both are sealed, each with its own products.

On a plan, read every place the pathway crosses a rated line. Each crossing is a penetration and should carry a firestop tag. A crossing with no tag is a question for the designer.

Lesson 530.2 · Section 4 Fire-Rated Assemblies and Firestop

530.2.10 Firestop systems

A firestop is a tested system, not a product: specific materials, in a specific assembly, around a specific penetrating item, installed as drawn in its listing.

A listed firestop system has been fire-tested as a whole — wall or floor, penetrating item, sleeve, packing and sealant together — under the test standard ASTM E814 or UL 1479. It is published with a drawing and a list of limits: wall type and thickness, sleeve size, how large the opening may be, how much of it the cables may fill, and which products. It is rated only as installed to that drawing. A different sealant, a larger hole or a fuller sleeve makes an untested system.

Ratings

  • F rating — the time, in hours, for which the system stops flame passing through the opening. It must at least match the rating of the assembly.
  • T rating — the time for which the system also keeps the temperature on the unexposed side from rising more than 325 °F above its starting temperature. The building code requires it in some floor penetrations.
  • L rating — air leakage through the system, where smoke control matters.

Reading a system number

Systems listed by UL carry a designation such as W-L-3112. The parts read in order:

Lesson 530.2 · Section 4 Fire-Rated Assemblies and Firestop

530.2.11 Labels, inspection and re-sealing

Each penetration is labeled, recorded and inspected before it is hidden, and resealed every time cable is added through it.

A firestopped penetration above a closed ceiling cannot be seen, so before the ceiling closes, the installer records the evidence that it was built correctly. The requirements come from the owner’s specification and the AHJ; ASTM E2174 sets out a standard practice for inspecting installed firestop systems. A typical owner specification asks for the following.

What a typical owner specification requires for each firestop penetration.
RequirementWhat it means on site
Install to the listed systemThe approved system drawing on site, and every condition of it met: products, opening size, fill, depth of seal
LabelA label on each face of the assembly, giving the system number, the installer and the date; many owners add the manufacturer and a warning not to disturb it
PhotographEach penetration photographed before it is concealed, with its label legible in the picture, filed against its tag
InspectThe AHJ or the owner’s inspector checks each penetration before the ceiling is closed; the contractor gives notice and does not close the ceiling until the inspection is done
Restore after changesWhen cable is added through an existing penetration, the installer restores the seal to the listing within the time the owner sets

Re-sealing after an add

A common firestop defect in an occupied building is a seal opened for new cable and not closed again. Before opening a penetration, read its label and find its system drawing. Remove only enough seal to pass the cable, check that the new fill is still within the listing’s limit, and reseal with the system’s products. Then update the label date and record the work. A sleeve that would be overfilled by the new cable needs a new penetration or a different system, which is a question for the designer, not a decision for the crew.

An open penetration is a path for fire and smoke

A penetration left open overnight between two fire compartments defeats the wall. Where the cable work cannot be completed and the penetration sealed in one shift, close the opening temporarily with the listed products before leaving, and record it.

Lesson 530.2 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

530.2.12 What you covered

Support the cable to suit each space, bond every rack on its own conductor, install the cable rating the space requires, and close every opening in a rated assembly with its listed system.

Cable supports follow the space: ladder rack in the telecommunications room, cable tray for the main shared route, J-hooks hung from structure for branches above accessible ceilings, conduit where the cable will be hidden or needs protection, and surface raceway on a finished wall. Each has limits: spacing (TIA-569 sets 5 ft for J-hooks; an owner may set less), sag, fill (a hook’s rated count, a tray’s percentage), and separation from motors, transformers and fluorescent fixtures. Count J-hooks by dividing the branch length by the maximum spacing and rounding the spans up. Under a raised floor the cable shares the cold-air supply; overhead it shares the space with sprinklers, lights and ducts.

Grounding connects to the earth; bonding connects metal parts together. A telecommunications bonding system has a PBB (formerly TMGB), connected by the BCT to the electrical service ground. A TBB, sized by length, runs to an SBB (formerly TGB) in each telecommunications room, and each rack and pathway section has its own conductor to the SBB, never daisy-chained.

The NEC (NFPA 70) applies in the edition the jurisdiction adopted, enforced by the AHJ. A 2026 edition exists and renumbers many communications rules, so check which edition applies. Chapter 8 covers communications and is not subject to Chapters 1–7 except where it refers to them; Article 770 covers optical fiber; Article 250 covers grounding and bonding; Article 300 covers general wiring methods; Article 645 covers information technology equipment rooms. Whether a ceiling space carries air is read from the mechanical drawings and above the tiles. Abandoned cable is removed where accessible. To find a section, start from the subject, pick the article, read its scope and parts, read the whole section with its exceptions; cite the document, edition, section, what it requires and how it applies.

Rated walls and floors are found through the legend, wall types, life safety plans, notes and stencils, and every crossing is a penetration with a firestop tag. A listed firestop system is rated only as installed to its drawing; its F rating at least matches the assembly, and a T rating adds a limit on temperature rise. A UL designation reads assembly, construction, then penetrant series. A sleeve lines an opening, a putty pad protects an outlet box, a collar closes a plastic pipe, and sealant or putty closes the annular space. Each penetration is labeled on both faces, photographed and inspected before it is concealed, and resealed whenever cable is added.
