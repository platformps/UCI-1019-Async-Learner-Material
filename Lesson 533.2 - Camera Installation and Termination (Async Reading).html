How an installer reads where a camera goes and what it must show, runs and terminates its cable, tests and labels the cable, then mounts the camera and aims it at its target.

Kestrel Communications holds the telecommunications contract for the Harlow Street Operations Center, project HSOC-26-014. The owner, Calder Regional Services, wants a camera on every bay of the Level 2 installation mock-up before it lets the security subcontract. 533.2.1 What a coverage drawing tells the installer

A camera installation ends when the camera shows what the owner asked to see. The installer reads the camera’s position and its target from the coverage drawing, checks the pathway, and runs a cable from the camera box to its Network Video Recorder (NVR). The installer terminates, tests, and labels that cable, then mounts the camera and aims it at its target.

A coverage drawing shows where each camera goes, the area it is expected to cover, and what it must show.

The security designer issues the coverage drawing. It is usually a floor plan, sometimes with a section or an elevation that gives heights. For each camera, the installer reads three things from it.

  • The camera’s position. Its location identifier, the surface it mounts on, and its mounting height. The camera schedule in the same set of drawings gives its model and the port it connects to at the head end.
  • The field of view. A wedge drawn from the camera across the area it covers. Where the designer drew the wedge from the camera’s datasheet, the sheet prints its angle. Where the designer drew it to show direction only, the sheet marks it schematic.
  • The observation target. What the camera must show, such as a door, a counter, a gate, or a vehicle lane, and the detail the owner requires there.

533.2.2 Field of view, and the test target

A wider field of view shows more of the scene, with each object in it smaller.

A camera spreads its image across its field of view. Take two cameras with the same resolution, one with a wide lens and one with a narrow lens, aimed at the same door from the same position. The narrow lens shows less of the scene, and the door larger. A camera farther from its target also shows the target smaller.

A fixed lens has one field of view, set by its maker. Aiming is the only adjustment the installer makes to it. A varifocal lens lets the installer set the field of view on site, then the focus. A fixed-focus lens gives a sharp image only beyond a nearest distance, which its datasheet states as the close-focus distance. A target nearer than that blurs.

Whether a printed line or a face is readable at the target depends on more than the lens. The camera’s resolution, the distance, the light, and the compression the NVR applies all affect it. A datasheet figure cannot settle it for a given site.

Why an owner accepts a camera on a test target

An owner can accept a camera on what it shows rather than on a calculation. A test target is a card placed at the observation target. It carries an identification line in large type and a strip of smaller characters. The owner’s documents state the acceptance. On HSOC-26-014, the Security Addendum (clause E3) requires the whole card in the frame, its identification line legible in recorded video, and the image upright.

Anyone on the job can repeat the check: the installer, the site lead, or the owner’s representative. A camera that shows the line on the card shows the detail the owner asked for at that position.

Recorded video can carry less detail than the live view. The stream an NVR records is not always the stream it shows live, and compression removes fine detail. Where the acceptance names recorded video, read the line in a recorded clip.

533.2.3 Checking the pathway before you work

Before you work with tools, check the box, the conduit, and the mounting surface against the drawings.

You make the check by eye, in a few minutes. It confirms that the cable has a clear path, that the camera has a surface to fix to, and that the identifier at the position matches the drawings. A fault recorded before the work starts cannot be the installer’s. Found after the pull, the same fault is hard to tell apart from the installer’s own work.

Record each item as you find it: in place, missing, loose, or what you see. Report a missing or different item to the site lead before you work around it. A workaround the site lead has not agreed to changes the installation without changing the drawings.

533.2.4 The cut length

A camera cable is one continuous run with no join. You cut it once, to a length worked out before the pull.

A cable cut short cannot be spliced to make up the length. The installer cuts a new one; the short length is scrap. A cable cut far too long leaves coils on the cable ladder that the design does not provide for.

On HSOC-26-014, the owner’s standard (clause 27 05 00 4.7) sets a cable’s cut length as the sum of six parts. The Security Addendum (clause E2) applies the rule to a camera cable, with the drop measured from the cable ladder down to the NVR’s shelf.

533.2.5 Pulling the cable through the conduit

The cable goes into the conduit at its open end and comes out at the box with no kink, no cut in its jacket, and no more pull on it than the cable allows.

Tape the leading end of the cable into a smooth point with one wrap of electrical tape, so that it does not catch on the joints inside the conduit. Push it in at the open end and feed it steadily toward the box. A short run with few bends often takes a push alone.

Where the cable stops, push a fish tape, a flat steel or fiberglass tape on a reel, from the box to the open end. Tape the cable to its head and draw it back through. Where the fish tape will not pass the bends, push a pull string through instead, and draw the cable through on it.

The limits on the cable

The owner’s cabling addendum sets the limits below. It takes the bend radius and the pulling tension from ANSI/TIA-568, the cabling standard of the American National Standards Institute and the Telecommunications Industry Association (ANSI/TIA).

A bend gauge, a round object eight times the cable’s diameter across, checks a bend by eye: no bend may be tighter than its curve. Hold it against the bend where the cable leaves the conduit.

Check the jacket where the cable leaves each end of the conduit. Record a cut or scuffed jacket, or a kink, and show it to the site lead before you terminate the cable.

Safety glasses

Wear safety glasses whenever you cut, strip, or crimp cable, and whenever you drill. A conductor end or a chip of metal or plastic can fly toward the eye.

533.2.6 Fitting an RJ45 plug to T568B

Each end of a camera cable takes a plug. At the head end, the plug goes straight into the NVR’s port; at the camera end, it joins the camera’s lead.

An RJ45 plug is an eight-position modular plug, the connector on the end of an Ethernet cord. The T568B assignment of the ANSI/TIA-568 standard sets which conductor goes to each of its eight pins. Both ends of the cable take the same assignment.

Figure — T568B on an RJ45 plug
Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled T568B: the conductor on each pin of an RJ45 plug. It shows the eight contact positions of a plug, numbered 1 to 8 from the left. Pin 1 white-orange, pin 2 orange, pin 3 white-green, pin 4 blue, pin 5 white-blue, pin 6 green, pin 7 white-brown, pin 8 brown.

Brackets beneath group the pairs: the orange pair on pins 1 and 2, the blue pair on pins 4 and 5, the brown pair on pins 7 and 8, and the green pair on pins 3 and 6, its bracket passing under the blue pair. A panel on the right, headed Reading the plug, says: contacts toward you, clip away; pin 1 is on the left. W- is the white conductor striped with its pair’s color: W-O is white-orange. O orange, G green, Bl blue, Br brown.

Hold the plug with its contacts toward you and its clip away from you: pin 1 is on the left. Each pair is a solid-colored conductor twisted with a white conductor striped in the same color. Three pairs sit side by side. The green pair lands on pins 3 and 6, on either side of the blue pair.

The order of work

  1. Strip the jacket back far enough to set the conductors in order. If a conductor’s insulation is nicked, cut the end square and strip again.
  2. Untwist each pair only as far as the plug needs, and keep each pair twisted to within ~1/2 in of the plug. Lay the eight conductors flat in the T568B order.
  3. Trim the conductors straight across to the length the plug needs. On a pass-through plug, leave them long.
  4. Push the conductors into the plug until each reaches the front, with the jacket inside the plug under its strain relief.
  5. Crimp. The conductors of a pass-through plug come out through its front; a pass-through crimper with a blade trims them flush as it crimps. Without a blade, trim them flush with cutters after the crimp.
  6. Inspect the plug from the contact side, clip away, for the conductor order, and from the side for the jacket.

Plugs are made for solid conductors, for stranded conductors, or for both; the packaging states which. A camera cable cut from horizontal cable has solid conductors. The plug maker’s instructions set the strip length and the crimper to use.

Lesson 533.2 · Section 3 Plug, Test, and Label

533.2.7 The wiremap test, and what it does not prove

A wiremap tester shows whether each conductor runs from a pin at one end to the same pin at the other, touching no other conductor.

The tester has two parts: a main unit, plugged onto one end of the cable, and a remote, plugged onto the other. The main unit sends a signal down each conductor in turn and reads where it arrives. Its screen shows the pin map, or names the fault and the pins it involves.

On HSOC-26-014, a cable passes when the tester shows all eight conductors continuous, each reaching the same pin at the far end and connected to no other conductor. The Security Addendum (clause E2) requires that pass before the head-end plug goes into the NVR.

The faults a wiremap test finds

  • Open. A conductor does not reach the far end: it is nicked through, cut, or short of its contact in the plug.
  • Short. Two conductors touch, where their insulation is damaged or a crimp has cut through it.
  • Reversed pair. The two conductors of one pair are swapped at one end.
  • Crossed pairs. Two pairs are swapped at one end, as in a plug wired to another assignment.

Match each tester reading to its fault.

What a pass does not prove

A split pair uses one conductor from each of two pairs as if the two were a pair. When both ends are wired the same wrong way, every pin still reaches the same pin. The cable passes a wiremap test. The two conductors are not twisted together, so they lose the twist’s protection against interference. The link can then fail at speed. A certification tester finds a split pair; a wiremap tester that checks only continuity and pin map, such as the one on this job, does not. The owner’s cabling addendum names this limit.

A pass also says nothing about how the cable performs at its rated speed, or about whether the NVR’s port will power the camera. A wiremap test verifies a cable. It does not certify it.

Lesson 533.2 · Section 3 Plug, Test, and Label

533.2.8 Labeling the cable

A cable label names the position the cable serves, so that a technician at either end can tell where the cable leads without tracing it.

The label carries the location identifier from the label scheme of the drawings; on HSOC-26-014, the scheme on sheet SEC-001 of the Kestrel security drawings. The same identifier goes on both ends. The owner’s standard sets how the label is made and where it sits: machine printed, flat, legible, and within a set distance of each end.

Label each end of a camera cable once that end is cut to its final length: the head end before the cable is laid among other cables, and the camera end after the final trim at the box. An unlabeled cable on a full ladder has to be traced to find its far end.

The position, not the camera

The identifier names where the camera is, not which camera it is. When a camera fails, its replacement takes the same position, the same cable, and the same identifier. The installer records the camera’s own identity against the position: its model, and its Media Access Control (MAC) address, which most cameras carry on their label.

On a hospital job whose scheme reads floor and zone, device type, and number, the camera numbered 12 in zone 3C takes the identifier 3C-CAM-12. Its cable carries 3C-CAM-12 at both ends.

533.2.9 Mounting the camera

The manufacturer’s instructions set how the camera fixes to its surface. The job’s documents set what it fixes to.

A turret camera has a base, and a ball that holds the lens and turns in the base. The manufacturer’s template gives the hole pattern for the base and the position of the cable entry. The instructions give the fixings and the order of work.

On a job such as HSOC-26-014, where the camera sits on a plate over a box, the joint between the camera’s lead and the cable sits inside the box. Fold the joint and the slack into the box before the plate goes on. Once the camera is fixed, no connector, conductor, or loop of cable shows outside the box and the plate.

Fit a plate or template marked TOP with that mark up. Fit every fixing the instructions name, and seat the base flat. Pressed lightly by hand, neither the camera nor the plate moves.

An upright image

A turret camera has three adjustments. Pan turns the camera left and right. Tilt raises and lowers the lens. Rotation turns the lens about its own axis, which turns the image. After pan and tilt bring the target into view, the image can lean. Rotation brings it level.

533.2.10 Power from the NVR’s own ports

Many NVRs carry Power over Ethernet (PoE) ports of their own. A camera on one of them takes its power and sends its video over the same cable.

Each camera’s cable ends at an NVR port. The port supplies power only after it detects a powered device on the cable, so a laptop plugged into it receives none. A separate network port joins the NVR to the building network.

Two limits apply: the most one port supplies, and the total the NVR supplies across all its ports. The camera’s datasheet gives its maximum draw. The NVR’s datasheet gives both limits.

533.2.11 Aiming the camera at its target

Aiming takes two people: one at the camera, and one reading its live view on the NVR.

The partner opens the camera’s channel in the NVR’s live view, on a laptop or on a monitor at the NVR, and calls the moves. Where the live view offers a choice of stream, choose the main stream. On many cameras, the one on HSOC-26-014 among them, the second stream has a far lower resolution than the main stream.

  1. Loosen the adjustments as the manufacturer’s instructions show.
  2. Pan and tilt to the partner’s calls until the whole target, its border included, sits in the frame.
  3. Rotate until the target stands upright in the image.
  4. Lock the adjustments.
  5. Check with the partner that the view has not moved. Tightening a ring or a screw can shift the camera.
  6. Capture the view as the work order asks.

Capture the NVR’s page only, with no account name in view. On HSOC-26-014, no password, credential, or account name appears in any record, photograph, or screenshot (Security Addendum, clause E9).

Lesson 533.2 · Section 5 Where Fiber Meets Copper

533.2.12 Where a camera link meets fiber

A camera beyond the reach of copper, on a pole across a yard or in another building, is reached on fiber. The link changes between fiber and copper on the way.

An Ethernet channel on twisted-pair copper reaches 100 m (328 ft), cords included. On HSOC-26-014, the owner’s standard limits a permanent link to 90 m (295 ft) (27 15 00 2.2). Fiber carries Ethernet far beyond either figure. A fiber cable with no metal in it also conducts no electricity between buildings.

Where the link changes between fiber and copper, a device converts the signal between light and electrical pulses. Two kinds of device do it.

  • A Small Form-factor Pluggable (SFP) transceiver. A module that plugs into a fiber port on a switch, or on another device built to take one. The switch then carries the fiber link like any other port.
  • A media converter. A standalone unit with a fiber port and a copper port, usually on its own power supply. It sits where the fiber ends: in a cabinet on the pole, or in a rack. Some models also supply PoE on their copper port; the datasheet states the standard and the power.

Lesson 533.2 · Section 5 Where Fiber Meets Copper

533.2.13 Matching the two ends of a fiber link

The two ends of a fiber link work together only when their optics match and each transmitter reaches the receiver at the other end.

Read four things on the labels and datasheets of the transceiver and the converter at each end.

What must match at the two ends of a fiber link.
What must matchWhere to read itFor example
Optics and wavelengthThe optical type on the label1000BASE-SX sends at 850 nanometers (nm); 1000BASE-LX at 1310 nm
Fiber modeThe datasheet, and the cable’s jacketSX runs on multimode fiber; LX normally on single-mode
ConnectorThe port, and the device’s documentsMost SFPs take an LC duplex connector; a converter’s documents state its own
SpeedThe Ethernet type on the labelA gigabit transceiver at one end, a gigabit converter at the other

A duplex link uses two fibers, one for each direction. Each end has a transmit port (TX) and a receive port (RX). The fiber from the TX at one end reaches the RX at the other, in both directions. With the two fibers swapped at one end, each TX faces the other end’s TX: neither receiver sees light, and neither link light comes on.

A link light is not a loss test

A link light on a switch port or on a converter shows that its receiver detects a signal it can use. It does not show how much light the run loses against its loss budget. A run close to its limit can light both ends and still fail its loss test. The loss test, made with a light source and a power meter, is the acceptance test of the run.

Light you cannot see

Transceivers and converters send infrared light, which the eye cannot see. Never look into a fiber end, a cord, or a port. Cap any port or cord that is not connected.

Lesson 533.2 · Section 6 Wrapping Up

533.2.14 What you covered

Read the camera’s position and target, check the pathway, cut and pull the cable, terminate, test, and label it, then mount the camera, power it, and aim it.

The coverage drawing

A coverage drawing gives each camera’s position, its field of view as a wedge, and its observation target. A wedge marked schematic shows direction only; the angle comes from the datasheet. A wider field of view shows more of the scene, with each object smaller. An owner who accepts a camera on a test target accepts it when the card’s identification line reads in the image the acceptance names.

Before the pull

Check the box, the conduit, and the mounting surface by eye, record what you find, and report a difference before you work around it. Cut the cable once, to the sum of its six parts rounded up to the next whole foot. Push it through the conduit, or draw it with a fish tape or a pull string, within the bend radius and the pulling tension.

Plug, test, and label

T568B puts white-orange, orange, white-green, blue, white-blue, green, white-brown, and brown on pins 1 to 8. A wiremap test finds opens, shorts, reversed pairs, and crossed pairs. It does not find a split pair, and it does not certify the cable. The label names the position, at both ends; the record carries the camera’s identity.

Mount, power, and aim

The manufacturer’s instructions set the fixings. The joint sits inside the box, and the base seats flat. An NVR’s PoE ports power its cameras within a per-port limit and a total. Aim with a partner on the main stream: pan, tilt, rotate until the target is upright, lock, and check again.

Where fiber meets copper

Beyond the reach of copper, an SFP transceiver or a media converter changes the link between fiber and copper. The optics, the fiber mode, the connector, and the speed match at both ends, and each TX reaches the RX at the far end. A link light is not a loss test.
