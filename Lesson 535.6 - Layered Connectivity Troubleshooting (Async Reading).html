Lesson 535.6 - Layered Connectivity Troubleshooting

How an installer finds out why a connection that should work does not, tells a fault in the cabling from a fault in a setting, corrects what the work order allows, and hands the rest to the network team with the evidence.

Kestrel Communications turned up the network on the Level 2 installation mock-up of the Harlow Street Operations Center, project HSOC-26-014, and handed it to the owner, Calder Regional Services. The next morning, Calder reports that the laptops in the Level 2 bays do not reach the network.

D. Okafor, the Kestrel site lead, sends the installers back with one instruction: test in order from the link up, and write each reading down before changing anything. An installer corrects a cord at an outlet, or a patch in the rack where the work order allows it. A switch setting goes to the network team, with the reading that names it, unless a work order assigns that change. After every correction, the installer runs the same tests again to prove the path.

535.6.1 Turning a report into tests

Lesson 535.6

Layered Connectivity Troubleshooting

A fault report says that something does not work, not where the fault is. The installer turns the report into tests, takes them in order from the link up, and names the fault from the readings. The installer then corrects what the work order allows, escalates the rest with the evidence, and writes the result down so that the next technician can check it after the job is closed.

5 sections  ·  11 topics  ·  11 activities

By the end of Lesson 535.6 you will be able to
  • Use ping and traceroute to isolate reachability.
  • Read link indicators and switch port status.
  • Confirm physical-layer integrity with a cable tester.
  • Distinguish a cabling fault from a configuration fault.
  • Determine what to fix and what to escalate.
  • Check an Artificial Intelligence (AI) fault hypothesis against the test results.
  • Document root cause and remediation.

A report arrives as a sentence from someone who was not reading the equipment. The first job is to turn it into tests.

The camera in the loading bay is down. That sentence reports a symptom and holds no reading. Five questions narrow it.

Five questions to ask before the first reading, and what each answer changes.
AskWhat the answer changes
Which outlet?A room names an area; an outlet label names one position and its row in the records
What does “down” mean?No lights, or lights and no picture, are different reports: the first points to power and cabling, the second to the network
When did it last work?Never worked points to the installation; stopped yesterday points to whatever changed yesterday
What changed?Furniture moved, work in the rack, a device replaced: the change is the first suspect
Is anything else affected?One outlet puts the fault on its path; several failing together put it in something they share, such as a switch

A report often carries a diagnosis: the cable is bad. Treat it as one more symptom: the person who made it took no readings.

The order of the tests

Test from the bottom up, and record every result. Most tests depend on the ones below them, so a failure low in the order usually makes the tests above it fail as well. The lowest test that fails is where to look first.

The order follows the layers of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model. Tests 1 and 2 check Layer 1, the physical layer: the cable and its link. Tests 3 and 4 read the switch: the port where it sees the laptop’s Media Access Control (MAC) address, and the Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN) the port is in. Test 5 checks the laptop’s address and its gateway. Test 6 looks up a test name: a name the network team has set the site’s Domain Name System (DNS) server to resolve and has posted for the installers.

Figure — The test order, from the link up
Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled The test order, from the link up: look first at the lowest test that fails. Six boxes are stacked, numbered from the bottom. 1, Link: does the adapter show a link, and is the switch port light on? 2, Cable: does the cord pass the cable tester? 3, Port: is the port connected, and where does the switch see the MAC address? 4, Network: is the switch port in its planned VLAN, read with show interfaces status. 5, Address and gateway: does the laptop hold an address on the planned network, read with ipconfig /all, and does the gateway answer a ping? 6, Name: does the test name resolve, read with nslookup.

The topics that follow take each test in turn. The five questions do not change the order of the tests. They change where you expect the first failure to fall.

535.6.2 What the laptop reports about itself

The laptop at the outlet is the first instrument. ipconfig /all reports whether each adapter has a link and what address it holds.

Turn the laptop’s Wi-Fi off before you test a wired path. With Wi-Fi on, a ping, a name lookup, or a trace can succeed over the wireless network and no longer test the cable. ipconfig /all still reports each adapter separately, so read the Ethernet adapter’s section.

ipconfig /all — the Ethernet adapter on another job, other lines left out
C:\> ipconfig /all

Ethernet adapter Ethernet:

   Physical Address. . . . . . . . . : AA-11-BB-22-CC-33
   DHCP Enabled. . . . . . . . . . . : Yes
   IPv4 Address. . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.70.114(Preferred)
   Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.255.0
   Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.70.1
   DHCP Server . . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.70.1
   DNS Servers . . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.70.53
  • Physical Address — the adapter’s MAC address, as Windows writes it.
  • DHCP Enabled — Yes when the laptop asks a Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) server for its address.
  • IPv4 Address, Subnet Mask, Default Gateway — the address it holds, the size of its network, and the router to which it sends traffic bound for other networks.
  • DHCP Server and DNS Servers — the server that gave the address, and the DNS servers the laptop asks for names.

Asking for an address again

A DHCP server assigns an address for a set time; that assignment is a lease. A laptop asks for a lease when its link comes up. A correction that leaves the link up, such as moving a switch port to another VLAN, does not make the laptop ask again. Make the laptop ask:

  • ipconfig /release gives up the current DHCP address;
  • ipconfig /renew asks the DHCP server for a new one.

Both act on every adapter that uses DHCP unless you name one. Where the address still starts with 169.254, wait ~1 min and run ipconfig /renew again: a switch port that has just come up may need ~30 s before it passes traffic. On a managed laptop, either command may stop with The requested operation requires elevation. Where it does, unplug the laptop’s cord for ~5 s and plug it back in: Windows asks for a new lease when the link returns.

535.6.3 Ping, name lookup, and trace

Three commands test the upper part of the path: ping for reach, nslookup for names, and tracert, the Windows form of traceroute, for the route.

Reading a ping result

A ping sends four short messages to an Internet Protocol (IP) address and reports what came back. Ping the gateway first: it is on the laptop’s own network, so a reply proves the local path without a router in between.

  • A reply from the address you pinged: the device is reachable from where you stand.
  • Request timed out. The message left and nothing came back. Several causes give this result, including a device set never to answer a ping.
  • A failure the laptop reports about itself. Destination host unreachable, from the laptop’s own address, means the laptop found nothing at that address on its own network. PING: transmit failed. General failure. means the laptop could not send at all, for example with no link or no usable address.

Read the address a reply came from. A reply carrying your laptop’s own address reports the laptop’s failure, not the device’s answer.

Name lookup

People and programs use names; the network uses addresses. nslookup asks the laptop’s DNS server for the address of a name.

nslookup — a name on another job resolves
C:\> nslookup intranet.northgate.example
Server:  dns1.northgate.example
Address:  192.168.70.53

Name:    intranet.northgate.example
Address:  192.168.70.80
  • Server and the first Address — the DNS server that answered, by name and address. Where the server has no name for its own address, the line reads UnKnown.
  • Name and the second Address — the name you asked about, and the address it resolves to.

A lookup that fails prints DNS request timed out. when the server did not answer, or a line ending Non-existent domain when it answered with no such name.

A gateway that answers a ping while a name fails to resolve puts the fault at the DNS server, on the way to it, or in the name, not in the cabling.

The trace

tracert lists each hop on the way to an address: one line for each router and a last line for the destination, each with three round-trip times. -d stops it looking up a name for every hop. -h 5 stops it after five hops. On Linux and macOS, the command is traceroute.

tracert — to the recording server on another job’s camera network
C:\> tracert -d -h 5 192.168.80.10

Tracing route to 192.168.80.10 over a maximum of 5 hops

  1    <1 ms    <1 ms    <1 ms  192.168.70.1
  2     1 ms     1 ms     1 ms  192.168.80.10

Trace complete.
  • Hop 1 — the laptop’s gateway. Hop 2 — the destination. Trace complete. — the route works end to end.
  • A line of * * * with Request timed out. is a hop that did not answer. Where later hops answer, that router is set not to reply; the line is not a fault.

A trace adds nothing inside the laptop’s own network. Use it when a ping across a router fails: the last hop that answered shows how far the traffic got. Where hop 1 does not answer and the gateway does not answer a ping either, the laptop has not reached its own gateway. The search then returns to the lower tests.

Lesson 535.6 · Section 2 Reading the Switch and the Cable

535.6.4 Port lights and port status

A port light shows whether a port has a link and whether traffic is passing. The switch’s status report names the port’s state.

The lights

The meaning of a port light varies by manufacturer, model, and the mode of the front panel, so read the legend on the chassis before the lights. On Catalyst switches of the kind in this lesson, in their default mode, a port light is:

  • off where the port has no link, or is shut down in the configuration;
  • green where the port has a link, and flashing green while traffic passes;
  • amber for ~30 s after a link comes up, while the switch checks the port for loops (a second path between switches, around which traffic would circulate without end) before forwarding traffic, unless the port is set to skip that check.

A light at one end says little about the other. A lit switch port and a dark laptop adapter point to the path between them, or to a lit port that does not serve this outlet.

What the switch says

On the mock-up, the team switches in the rack are Catalyst switches, read from a console session; the PoE switch is a UniFi switch, read in its controller. From a console session on a Catalyst switch, show interfaces status prints one line per port. On these switches as they are usually configured, it runs at the user prompt, >.

show interfaces status — five ports on another job’s switch, other ports left out
Switch> show interfaces status

Port      Name               Status       Vlan       Duplex  Speed Type
Gi0/19                       notconnect   140          auto   auto 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi0/20                       connected    140        a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi0/21                       disabled     140          auto   auto 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi0/22                       connected    150        a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi0/23                       connected    140        a-full  a-100 10/100/1000BaseTX
  • Status — connected: a link. notconnect: the port is enabled and no link has formed. disabled: the port is shut down in the configuration. A fourth value, err-disabled, means the switch shut the port itself after an error.
  • Vlan — the VLAN the port is in. Compare it with the VLAN the plan gives the port.
  • Duplex and Speed — how the link came up. a- marks a value the port negotiated. Gi0/23 came up at 100 megabits per second (Mbps) where 1000 was expected.
  • Name — a description someone typed on the port; empty here. Type — what the port is built for: copper, at 10, 100, or 1000 Mbps.

The switch names its ports by model: a Catalyst 2960G names port 20 Gi0/20, and a Catalyst 3750G names it Gi1/0/20. Where the output stops at --More--, press the space bar.

Where the switch sees the laptop

The switch learns each MAC address from the traffic it receives. Its MAC address table names the port each address arrived on. To look up one address, write it the switch’s way: Windows writes AA-11-BB-22-CC-33, and the switch writes the same 12 hexadecimal digits as aa11.bb22.cc33.

show mac address-table address — one laptop on another job
Switch> show mac address-table address aa11.bb22.cc33
          Mac Address Table
-------------------------------------------

Vlan    Mac Address       Type        Ports
----    -----------       --------    -----
 140    aa11.bb22.cc33    DYNAMIC     Gi0/20
Total Mac Addresses for this criterion: 1
  • Vlan — the VLAN the traffic arrived in. Type DYNAMIC — learned from traffic. Ports — the port it arrived on.
  • Total Mac Addresses for this criterion — how many entries matched: 0 where the switch has not heard the address.

On older software releases, the command is written show mac-address-table address.

The switch drops a port’s entries when its link goes down, and drops any entry after ~5 min of silence. Keep the laptop sending, for example with ping -t to its gateway, while you, or a second installer, read the switch. Where the laptop holds a 169.254 address, run ipconfig /renew first, or unplug the laptop’s cord for ~5 s and plug it back in: its request for a lease puts the MAC address in the table.

Lesson 535.6 · Section 2 Reading the Switch and the Cable

535.6.5 What the cable tester answers

A cable tester checks Layer 1, the physical layer: how a cord or a run is wired, and how long it is.

The tester has a main unit and a remote unit. To test a patch cord, plug one end into each unit and run the wiremap test: the main unit reports, pin by pin, where each of the eight conductors arrives. To test an installed run, the main unit goes on the panel port and the remote unit on the outlet, each through a short cord, with nothing else plugged in.

The faults a wiremap test reports, and how each behaves in service.
FaultWhat is wrongHow it behaves in service
OpenA conductor does not reach the far end: a broken wire, or a pin not seatedUsually no link. An open on the pair on pins 4 and 5 or pins 7 and 8 can leave a 100 Mbps link working
ShortTwo conductors touch, often where a plug was crimped over bare copperNo link. A Power over Ethernet (PoE) port may refuse to power a short
Reversed pairThe two conductors of one pair swapped at one endOften carries traffic; it can survive in service for years
Crossed pairsA whole pair landed in another pair’s position at one endDepends on which pairs. Where 1-2 and 3-6 are crossed, ports with automatic crossover usually link normally; other crosses may give no link or a slower one. The wiremap still names it a fault

The tester on the mock-up names an open by the pin it affects and shows a short as a short. It does not detect a split pair: every conductor reaches its pin, but one signal pair is made of wires from two different twisted pairs. A split pair passes this tester. In service it may link and then lose traffic at any speed, worst on longer runs. A tester that measures crosstalk, the signal one pair picks up from another, detects it; a certification tester is one.

Length is a separate test on the mock-up’s tester: the remote unit comes off and nothing is plugged into the far end. The tester measures from ~8 ft (2.5 m) to ~656 ft (200 m), and shows 0 outside that range. Where a length reads far shorter than the run, the tester has found a break and is reporting the distance to it.

What the tester does not answer

  • The switch configuration. A port shut down or in the wrong VLAN tests perfectly on a cable tester, because neither is a fault in the cabling.
  • Performance. A wiremap shows how the cabling is wired, not whether it meets a category. Certification is a different test with a different instrument.
  • Anything it was not plugged into. A clean test on a cord clears that cord and nothing else on the path.

Some testers also report what is at the far end of a live run, such as a port’s speed or the presence of PoE. Read the manual of the tester you carry before you rely on such a reading.

535.6.6 Locating a fault by substitution

Swapping one part for a known-good one locates a fault in a path you cannot see into, provided you change one thing at a time.

A wired path has fixed parts: the outlet, the run, and the panel port. It also has two cords, which people handle and move. A cord can be swapped in seconds. The fixed parts cannot.

What each substitution clears, and what it leaves untested.
SubstitutionA good result clearsIt does not clear
A known-good cord from the laptop to the outletThat cordAnything past the outlet
A known-good cord in the rackThat cordThe run, and the switch port
Another laptop on the same outletThe path, which serves bothThe first laptop

Rules for substitution

  • One change at a time. Change two things, get a working path, and you know only that the fault was in one of two places.
  • Known-good means tested. A cord out of a bag is unverified until it passes a wiremap test.
  • Keep and label what failed. A failed cord with no label goes back into the bag and reaches another outlet.
  • Write each swap down as you make it. The record needs the order of the swaps; memory loses the order first.

A substitution locates a fault; it does not explain it. “The cord was bad” is a location. The tester’s reading on the failed cord, such as an open on pin 6, is the explanation the record needs. Where both cords are cleared and the fault stays, what remains is the run, the panel port, and the switch port. Testing the run is a separate task. The outlet is out of service while the test runs.

Check Your Understanding

A laptop has no link. You replace its cord and, at the same time, move the rack cord to a different switch port. The link comes up. What have you established?

Lesson 535.6 · Section 3 Naming the Fault

535.6.7 A cabling fault or a configuration fault

The faults in this lesson are of two kinds: a path that cannot carry the traffic, or a switch setting other than the one the plan gives. The readings usually tell which.

A cabling fault is in the physical path: a conductor that does not reach, a short, a cord not seated, or a cord in the wrong port. A cord patched to the wrong switch port carries traffic to the wrong place; it is a physical fault, because moving the cord corrects it. A configuration fault is in a setting: a port shut down, a port in the wrong VLAN, a speed or duplex fixed at one end. At the laptop the two kinds can look the same. They separate at the switch and at the tester.

Readings that point to a cabling fault, and readings that point to a configuration fault.
ReadingPoints to cablingPoints to configuration
Status, show interfaces statusnotconnect: enabled, and no linkdisabled: shut down
Vlan, same outputUnaffected: a cabling fault does not change the Vlan columnA VLAN other than the planned one
Speed and Duplex, same outputa-100 on a device and a port that both support 1000 MbpsA value with no a-: fixed at this end
MAC address lookupNot found, or found on another portFound on the planned port, in the wrong VLAN
Wiremap on the cordNames a faultClean: the cord is cleared; look at the settings and the rest of the path

Two faults can look alike: a port that is shut down shows Media disconnected at the laptop, as a cord that carries no signal does. One fault can also hide another. A correction that brings the link up can then reveal a second fault above it. After each correction, test again from the bottom.

Match each reading to what it names.

Lesson 535.6 · Section 3 Naming the Fault

535.6.8 Checking an AI hypothesis

An AI chat tool given a set of readings returns a likely fault in seconds. Its answer is a hypothesis; the readings decide whether it holds.

An AI tool such as Microsoft Copilot answers from patterns in the text it was trained on. It has not seen your equipment. It tends to name the most common fault for the symptoms you describe, whether or not your readings support it. It writes with the same confidence whether it is right or wrong.

How to ask

  • Give it the readings as the screen showed them: the adapter’s state and address, the ping, the lookup, the trace, the tester’s result, and the switch’s status line. A summary such as no network gives it nothing to reason from.
  • Ask for a domain (physical or configuration), the fault, and the reading that would confirm it.
  • Keep every credential out. No password, passphrase, or sign-in detail goes into the prompt.

How to check the answer

Hold the hypothesis against each reading in turn. A hypothesis errs where a reading contradicts it: a reply from the gateway contradicts “the cable is bad.” It agrees where every reading fits it. Mark the domain and the fault separately: a tool can place a fault in the right domain and name the wrong fault. Act on a hypothesis only after a reading supports it.

Lesson 535.6 · Section 4 Fix, Escalate, Record

535.6.9 What you fix, and what you escalate

Whether a fault is yours to correct depends on where it is and on the work order. An installer corrects cords at the outlets of the installer’s own bay, and cords in the rack where the work order allows patching. A switch setting is the installer’s to change only where a work order assigns that port and that change.

On the mock-up, section 5 of the Kestrel Network Plan sets the boundary. An installer handles the cords of the installer’s own laptop and at the outlets of the installer’s own bay, and patches in the rack only where a work order allows patching. Only the network team may change a switch setting that no work order assigns.

Where the boundary falls on common faults, and what the installer does.
FaultYours?What you do
A cord that fails the wiremapYesReplace it with a known-good cord, label the failed one, retest
A cord not seated at an outlet of your own bayYesReseat it, confirm the link, and record where you found it
A rack cord not seated, or patched to a port other than the one the record or work order namesYes, where the work order allows patching at the rackReseat it, or move it back to the port the record or work order names; confirm at both ends, and record where you found it
Any other cord move in the rackNoEscalate, with the reading that calls for it
A port shut down, or in the wrong VLANOnly where a work order assigns that port and that changeCorrect it where assigned. Otherwise escalate, with the reading that names it
No DHCP answer on a sound port in the planned VLAN; a name that does not resolveNoEscalate with the readings
Anything on equipment the work order does not nameNoReport it. Do not touch it

An escalation the network team can act on

An escalation gives the port as the switch names it, the reading that identifies the fault, and the change that would clear it.

An escalation from another job

Switch SW-2, port Gi0/24. show interfaces status at 10:42 reads connected, VLAN 140, Duplex full, Speed 100, with no a-: both values are fixed at the switch. The site plan gives negotiated values. Laptop cord and rack cord pass the wiremap. Request: return Gi0/24 to negotiated speed and duplex.

A fault handed over with the readings that place it outside your part of the path is finished work. The readings tell the next person what is already ruled out.

Lesson 535.6 · Section 4 Fix, Escalate, Record

535.6.10 Root cause, remediation, and the retest

A fault record states what was wrong, what was changed, and the reading that proves the change worked.

The fields of a fault record and what goes in each.
FieldWhat goes in it
Symptom as reportedThe words of the report, and the outlet it named
What you foundThe readings, with their values, in the order taken
Root causeThe thing that, once changed, made the symptom stop
RemediationWhat you changed, and what you put back or handed in
RetestThe same tests after the change, with their values

A root cause is not a symptom

Cable problem is a symptom. Short between two conductors in the rack cord, at the plug on the switch end, shown by the wiremap is a root cause. Read a root cause back and ask whether someone could produce the same fault from it. If they could, it is a cause. It works now is not a cause: it belongs in the retest field, with the reading beside it.

The retest

Run the same tests after the change, in the same order, and write down the values. A repair with no after-reading is a claim. Where the change was made at the switch and the laptop’s link stayed up, make the laptop ask for an address on the corrected network first: run ipconfig /release and ipconfig /renew, or unplug its cord for ~5 s.

Lesson 535.6 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

535.6.11 What you covered

Turn the report into tests, take them from the link up, name the fault from the readings, correct what is yours, escalate the rest with evidence, and retest the same way.

Starting from a fault report

Five questions turn a report into tests: which outlet, what “down” means, when it last worked, what changed, and whether anything else is affected. Test from the bottom up, Layer 1 first, record every result, and look first at the lowest test that fails: link, cable, port, network, address and gateway, name. With Wi-Fi off, ipconfig /all shows the Ethernet adapter as Media disconnected, with a 169.254 address, with an address off the planned network, or with a lease on it. ipconfig /release and ipconfig /renew, or unplugging the cord for ~5 s, make the laptop ask again. Ping the gateway first; nslookup tests the name; tracert -d shows how far traffic gets across routers.

Reading the switch and the cable

A port light is off with no link or with the port shut down, and amber for ~30 s after a link comes up. In show interfaces status, connected, notconnect, and disabled separate a link, no link, and a shutdown; the Vlan column names the port’s VLAN. show mac address-table address (on older releases, show mac-address-table address) names the port the switch heard a laptop on. The wiremap names opens, shorts, reversed pairs, and crossed pairs; the tester on the mock-up does not detect a split pair. No cable tester reads a switch setting. Substitute one part at a time, with a cord that has passed the wiremap.

Naming the fault

A cabling fault is in the physical path, including a cord in the wrong port; a configuration fault is in a setting. notconnect and a failing wiremap point to cabling; disabled and a wrong Vlan point to configuration. An AI hypothesis holds only where every reading fits it; mark its domain and its fault separately.

Fix, escalate, record

An installer replaces a failed cord and reseats a cord at an outlet of the installer’s own bay. Where the work order allows patching at the rack, the installer also reseats a rack cord or moves it back to the port the record or work order names. Any other cord move in the rack, and any switch setting no work order assigns, goes to the network team with the port as the switch names it, the reading, and the change needed. A fault record holds the symptom as reported, the readings, the root cause, the remediation, and the retest with its values.
