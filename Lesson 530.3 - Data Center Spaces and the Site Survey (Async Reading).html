How a data hall is laid out and cabled, how a cabinet is planned, and how the drawings are checked against the building before work starts.

The Harlow Street Operations Center has a data hall on Level 1, with rows of server cabinets on a raised floor and its own distribution cabinets for the cabling. Kestrel Communications will cable it. Upstairs, before the Level 2 cabling starts, Kestrel’s site lead has walked the floor and the telecommunications room with the general contractor’s superintendent, tape measure and drawings in hand, and written down what they found. The second half is the site survey: what it is for, how to measure and record, how to sort what you find, and how to write a request for information (RFI).

The worked examples use hypothetical sites with their own names and numbers.

A note on the standards and the equipment

TIA-942, from the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA), defines the spaces and cabling of a data center. ANSI/TIA-568, from the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and the TIA, covers the cabling itself. An owner’s standard and the contract set out how field differences from the drawings are reported; the survey and RFI steps on this page are common practice. Each project’s documents govern them.

Cabinet heights, equipment weights and airflow directions vary by model. Read the manufacturer’s data for the equipment in front of you before you plan where it goes.

530.3.1 The spaces of a data center

Lesson 530.3

Data Center Spaces and the Site Survey

A data center divides its floor into distribution areas, each with its own role in the cabling. Its rows face each other in a pattern that keeps the equipment cool. This lesson names those areas and follows a link through them, covers aisles and containment, and plans what goes where in a cabinet. It then covers the site survey: measuring the building against the drawings, sorting what you find, and asking the design team in writing where the drawings cannot be built.

A data center uses the same cabling hierarchy as an office building, with its own names for the spaces and more of them.

TIA-942, from the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA), sets out the spaces of a data center. TIA-942 calls most of them distribution areas. Most are a group of cabinets in the data hall, not a room. The hierarchy is the one used in any building — a main cross-connect, optional intermediate cross-connects, horizontal cross-connects, then the equipment — drawn at the scale of rows of cabinets.

The spaces of a data center under TIA-942.
SpaceWhat it holdsIts counterpart in an office building
Entrance roomWhere provider and campus cables enter: protectors, demarcation, often the providers’ equipment. Can be a room of its own or part of the hallEntrance facility
Main distribution area (MDA)The main cross-connect and the core routers and switches. One per data centerMain cross-connect in the equipment room
Intermediate distribution area (IDA)An intermediate cross-connect, between the MDA and some horizontal distribution areas, used in large or multi-room data centers. OptionalIntermediate cross-connect
Horizontal distribution area (HDA)The horizontal cross-connect for a group of rows, with the access switches that serve themTelecommunications room
Zone distribution area (ZDA)A connection point partway along the horizontal cabling (a zone outlet or consolidation point), often under the floor or overhead in a row; no cross-connect. OptionalA consolidation point in an open office
Equipment distribution area (EDA)The cabinets holding the servers and storage, where the horizontal cabling ends on patch panelsWork area

The abbreviation ER means two things. In an office building ER is the equipment room; in a data center, the entrance room. Read the legend of the set in front of you. A data hall drawing usually labels each distribution area beside its cabinets and gives the cabinet positions by row and number.

530.3.2 Following a link through the spaces

Trace a link from a server cabinet outward, space by space, naming the cable between each pair, until it reaches the main distribution area.

Tracing a link means answering, at each step: which space am I in, what cable leaves it, and which space does that cable go to. The chain holds two kinds of cable. Horizontal cabling runs from an HDA to the EDAs it serves, through a ZDA where there is one. Backbone cabling runs between the distribution areas: HDA to MDA, HDA to IDA, IDA to MDA, and MDA to the entrance room. A trace from an EDA therefore always starts with horizontal cable and changes to backbone at the first HDA.

The method

  1. Find the cabinet on the data hall plan by its row and number.
  2. Find its serving HDA. The plan’s notes or labels say which HDA serves which rows, and whether a ZDA stands between the HDA and some of the cabinets.
  3. Find what feeds that HDA: the MDA directly, or an IDA. The notes or the backbone diagram say which.
  4. Write the chain as space, cable, space until it ends at the MDA.

Had the plan note put a ZDA between HDA-1 and Row 2, the horizontal cable would be in two pieces, each recorded, with the ZDA named between them. Had HDA-1 been fed from an IDA, the backbone would be in two pieces with the IDA between them. Every space and every cable in the chain is named. A trace that skips one cannot be checked against the drawing.

Which cabinets are not EDAs

A row usually holds its HDA in one of its own cabinet positions, at the end of the row. That cabinet holds patch panels and switches, not servers, and it is not an EDA, even though it stands in the row with the server cabinets. Data hall plans number it with the row and label it with its function.

530.3.3 Reading a data hall plan

A data hall plan is drawn at the scale of cabinets, and most of what it says is in its labels, its arrows and its notes.

An enlarged plan of a data hall shows every cabinet position as a small rectangle, usually 24 in wide and 42 in or 48 in deep, set out in rows. At the scales used for a hall, commonly 1/8″ or 1/4″ = 1′-0″, the rectangles are too small to carry much text, so the plan relies on a set of conventions.

What a data hall plan shows, and where to look for it.
To findLook for
Which way a row facesAn arrow or the word FRONT beside each row. Two rows whose fronts point at each other have a cold aisle between them, the aisle the equipment draws its cool air from
Which cabinet is whichA row letter or number and a position number, written inside or beside each rectangle: Row 2, cabinet 06, labeled 2-06
Which cabinets are distribution areasA label with the function (MDA, IDA, HDA) on or beside the cabinet, often with a different fill, and a note saying which rows each one serves
Where a zone point isA small symbol in an aisle or under the floor, labeled ZDA, with a note on what feeds it and which cabinets it serves
The aisles and the airPerforated tiles, floor tiles with holes that let the cold air up, drawn in the cold aisles; aisle labels; the cooling units along a wall. Containment, the doors and roof that close off one aisle, is drawn as doors across the aisle ends, or a note says there is none
The pathwaysOverhead tray drawn as a dashed or hatched band over the rows, or under-floor tray noted on a separate plan; where the backbone enters the hall
The raised floorA note giving its height and whether the space under it supplies cold air

A typical set of hall notes says which cabinets are equipment distribution areas, which HDA serves which rows and what feeds it, what a ZDA serves, and whether containment is installed. Read the notes before tracing anything: two rows drawn identically can be served by different HDAs, and only the note says so.

Reserved positions are often drawn as an outline with no cabinets in it, labeled for future rows. Nothing in a future area is installed or cabled, but the hall’s pathways are sometimes sized to serve it, so a tray that looks oversized for the present rows may be correct.

A cabinet’s position and its function are separate

An HDA occupies a cabinet position in its row and has a position number like any other cabinet. The plan gives both: the position (for example 1-01) and the function (HDA-1). Use the function when tracing a link and the position when finding the cabinet on the floor.

Lesson 530.3 · Section 2 Air and Racks

530.3.4 Hot aisles, cold aisles and containment

Equipment takes in cool air at its front and exhausts hot air at its back, so rows are turned to face each other front to front and back to back.

Most servers and storage draw air in through the front and push it out the back. A row of cabinets set out that way produces a wall of hot air behind it. If the next row faced the same way, it would breathe the first row’s exhaust. Rows are therefore set out in alternating directions: two rows facing each other across a cold aisle, where cool air is supplied, and back to back across a hot aisle, where exhaust collects and returns to the cooling units.

Containment

Without a barrier, hot exhaust spills over the top of the rows and around their ends into the cold aisle, and cold air escapes past the ends without entering any equipment. Containment closes one of the aisles.

Cold-aisle containment

  • Doors across the ends of the cold aisle and a roof over it.
  • The cold aisle becomes a closed supply chamber; the rest of the room runs warm.
  • Fits an existing raised-floor hall without changing the return.

Hot-aisle containment

  • Doors and a roof enclose the hot aisle, ducted to the ceiling return or to the cooling units.
  • The rest of the room stays at the supply temperature.
  • Needs a return path above the ceiling, or cooling units in the row.

Either way, every gap in the row has to be closed for containment to work: blanking panels in empty rack units, seals between cabinets, brush grommets at floor cut-outs. Cable work can open these gaps. A removed blanking panel or a cable bundle through an unsealed tile cut-out lets air bypass the equipment. A bundle behind a cabinet blocks its exhaust.

On a data hall plan, rows show their front with an arrow or the word FRONT, and the aisles between them are labeled; perforated tiles are drawn in the cold aisles. Where a hall has no containment, the plan says so in a note or shows nothing across the aisle ends.

530.3.5 Rack elevations

A rack elevation is a drawing of one rack or cabinet, position by position, drawn before anything is mounted in it.

Racks and cabinets are measured in rack units (U): 1U is 1.75 in of vertical space. A 42U cabinet has 42 positions, numbered from the bottom up. A rack elevation shows each piece of equipment at the positions it occupies, its height in U, and what is left empty. It is drawn to settle weight, airflow, cabling and service access before the equipment arrives.

What a rack elevation plans for, and how.
ConcernPlanning rule
WeightThe heaviest items at the bottom: an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) and its batteries, then the heaviest servers. A top-heavy cabinet can tip when a loaded server is slid out on its rails. Add up the equipment weights and check them against the cabinet’s rated load and the floor’s rated load
AirflowEvery item breathing front to back, from the cold aisle to the hot aisle. A switch that breathes side to side needs a baffle, or a position where it does not take in another unit’s exhaust. Blanking panels fill every empty position on the front
CablingPatch panels close to where the cable enters the cabinet: at the top for overhead entry, low for under-floor entry. A horizontal cable manager between a patch panel and the switch it patches to. Power distribution units (PDUs) mounted vertically at the rear, outside the U positions
Service accessRoom in front of and behind the cabinet to slide equipment out on its rails, positions for growth left together rather than scattered, and nothing mounted so that removing it means removing something else first

Reading a rack elevation sheet

A set usually draws rack elevations on one sheet, several cabinets side by side, each titled with its cabinet name and its position in the hall. Some show the front and rear of each cabinet as two views, because PDUs, vertical cable managers and the bonding connection are mounted at the rear. A schedule beside the elevations often lists each item with its make and model, height in U, weight and power draw, and totals them per cabinet. Check the totals against the cabinet’s load rating and its power feed before anything is ordered or mounted.

An elevation states the height of every item in U and its position number, so that two people mounting the same cabinet from the drawing put each item in the same place. Count the U: the items and the empty positions must add up to the cabinet’s height.

530.3.6 The training rack and the production row

A mobile infrastructure rack holds in one 42U frame the same kinds of equipment that a production data hall spreads across its rows and rooms.

The mobile infrastructure rack is a 42U rack on casters used for training. It holds servers, switches, routers, power distribution units and an uninterruptible power supply. A production data center holds the same component types, each in a different space: the servers in the equipment distribution areas, the switching in the distribution areas, the routing toward the providers, and the power in the cabinets and in the electrical room. The table maps each component on the training rack to the space where it sits in a production hall.

Component types on a training rack and where the same type sits in a production data center.
On the training rackIn a production data centerWhat changes at production scale
ServersServer cabinets in the equipment distribution areasMany cabinets per row, each with its own patch panels and rack PDUs
SwitchesAccess switches in the horizontal distribution areas; core switches in the main distribution areaSwitching is split by role and placed in different cabinets
RoutersThe main distribution area and the entrance room, at the connection to the providersDuplicated, with links to more than one provider
Power distribution unitsRack PDUs in every cabinet, fed from floor-standing PDUs or panels in the hallTwo PDUs per cabinet on separate feeds, labeled A and B
UPSA central UPS system in its own electrical room, feeding the hallSized for the whole hall, with battery or flywheel rooms of its own

A single rack also mixes jobs that a production hall separates: there is one patch field, one cable manager set, one power path. The same rules apply to it as to a production cabinet: weight at the bottom, front-to-back airflow, blanking panels, managers between panels and switches.

530.3.7 What a site survey is for

A site survey checks the building against the drawings before work starts, so that differences are found on paper rather than with a crew standing in front of them.

Drawings show what was designed. The building shows what was built, and the two drift apart: a column is boxed in larger than drawn, a pipe is rerouted, casework is fixed where an outlet was planned. Owner standards and contract documents commonly require the contractor to verify dimensions and conditions on site before installing and to report every difference in writing. A site survey, or site walk, is how that verification is done.

Before you go

  • The current drawings. The latest revision of every sheet you will check, with the revision dates confirmed against the sheet index.
  • The owner standard and the specification sections you will check the space against.
  • Access. Badges, escorts, keys, working hours, and who on site can open ceilings and locked rooms. Arrange them before the day.
  • Instruments. A tape measure, a laser distance meter, a flashlight, a ladder where you are permitted to use one, and a camera.
  • Personal protective equipment the site requires.
  • A record form: date and time, who walked, who accompanied, the drawings carried, and a numbered line for each finding.

Walk with someone who can answer questions on the spot, such as the general contractor’s superintendent or the owner’s facilities lead. Write any explanation heard during the walk into the record, with the name of the person who gave it.

530.3.8 Measuring and recording

A finding is useful only if someone who was not on the walk can read where it is, what was measured, and what the drawing shows instead.

Measuring

  • Say what you measured to. Room dimensions to the finished inside faces of the walls; heights above finished floor (AFF); the bottom of a duct or beam, not its center.
  • Measure what the drawing dimensions. If the drawing gives a room as wall-to-wall, measure wall-to-wall. A figure taken a different way cannot be compared.
  • Measure twice where it matters. A dimension that will be reported as a difference is measured again, with a second instrument where possible.
  • Count what the drawing counts. Sleeves, outlets, receptacles and doors are counted, not estimated.
  • Look above the ceiling. Where tiles can be lifted with permission, check the space the pathway will use: ducts, pipes, structure, and whether return grilles open into the void.

Recording

Each finding is written as one numbered entry carrying its location, the measured value with its unit, what the drawing or standard shows, and a photograph reference. The entry states the fact and does not yet say what to do about it.

Photographs

A photograph supports a finding only if a reader can tell where it was taken and what it shows. Take a wide shot that places the condition in the room, then a close shot of the condition itself. Put a measuring tape or a laser reading in the frame where the finding is a dimension. Number the photographs in the order taken and write the number on the entry at the same moment, so that the two cannot drift apart. Before you photograph inside an occupied building, check the site’s rules on cameras: some owners forbid photographs of security equipment, people or screens.

The record is signed or initialed by whoever walked, and copied to the people the contract names. It is a document the project relies on, so a correction to it is made as a dated addition, not by overwriting an entry.

530.3.9 Sorting what the survey finds

Each finding gets one primary class, and that class decides what happens to it next.

The classes of survey finding, and what happens to each.
ClassWhat it isWhat happens to it
Field differs from the drawingsThe building as it stands does not match what the drawings, or a note on them, showA request for information to the design team, through the channel the contract names. The affected work waits for the answer
Drawings do not meet the owner standardThe field matches the drawings, but what is drawn falls short of the owner standardA request for information as well. Where an owner standard ranks itself above the drawings and requires an RFI when two documents conflict, the drawings cannot simply be built
Site constraintA condition that limits how, when or where the work is done, while the drawings are not in error: working hours, access, protection of finishes, occupied areas, lead timesInto the work plan and the schedule; to the project manager where it affects cost or time
ObservationA fact worth recording that needs no action: work by another trade not yet started, a condition that matches both the drawings and the standardRecorded, with no action; checked again on a later visit where it may change

Start with one question: does the building match the drawings? If not, it is a field difference. If it does, ask whether what is drawn meets the owner standard. If it does not, the finding goes to an RFI as a drawing that does not meet the owner standard. A finding that changes only how or when the work is done is a site constraint, and one that changes nothing is an observation.

Where two classes apply, note the second in the entry’s notes column: an outlet drawn behind fixed casework, in a room open only after hours, is a field difference, with the access hours noted as a site constraint.

530.3.10 Writing a request for information

A request for information asks the design team one question about the documents, in writing, with everything the designer needs to answer it.

A request for information (RFI) is the contract’s route for a question the documents do not answer: two documents disagree, a drawing cannot be built as shown, or something needed is missing. It goes from the contractor to the design team through the channel the contract sets, usually the general contractor, who logs it and forwards it. The answer comes back in writing. The work is built to that answer and to any revised sheet that comes with it.

What goes in an RFI

The fields of a request for information.
FieldWhat it holds
Number, date, projectThe RFI’s own number in the project log, the date sent, the project name and number
To and fromThe design team, through the general contractor; the person and company asking
ReferenceEvery document involved: sheet and detail numbers, revision, specification section and clause
ConditionWhat the documents show and what the building shows, with the measured values and photographs from the survey
QuestionOne question, worded so that it can be answered
Proposed solutionOptional. A suggestion the designer may accept, change or reject, marked as a suggestion
Impact and date neededWhich work is held until the answer arrives, and by when the answer is needed to keep the schedule

What makes a good question

  • One question per RFI. Two questions in one RFI are answered, logged and closed together. The second is often missed.
  • Answerable as asked. “Where is the outlet to be located?” can be answered. “Please advise” asks the designer to work out the question first.
  • Not already answered. Check the drawings, the specification and previous RFI answers first. An RFI whose answer is already in the documents still has to be logged, answered and closed by the design team.
  • Facts, not blame. State the condition and the reference; leave out who caused it.
What not to do while the answer is pending

Do not proceed on an assumption. Work built to a guess may have to be removed when the answer differs. The cost of removing it usually falls on the contractor who built it. Do not redesign in the field. Moving an outlet, rerouting a pathway or changing a cable type is the designer’s decision, because it can affect other trades, the code and the owner’s standard. A proposed solution in the RFI is the place for your suggestion. Owner standards often state this directly: where documents conflict, submit an RFI and do not proceed with the affected work until it is answered.

After the answer

The general contractor keeps an RFI log: number, subject, date sent, date answered, and the answer. Check the log for open RFIs before planning each stretch of work. When an answer arrives, read it against the question: it may answer only part of the question, or change something else. Mark the answer on the working drawings at the affected location with the RFI number, brief the crew that will do the work, and keep the written answer with the job file. Where the answer comes with a revised sheet, the revised sheet replaces the old one in the set. Check the sheet index and the revision again. An answer that would add cost or time goes to the project manager before the work starts: a design answer is not by itself an instruction to spend more.

530.3.11 What you covered

Name each data center space and the cable between them, plan a cabinet for weight, air and access, and check the drawings against the building before asking the designer, in writing, what cannot be built.

TIA-942 names the spaces of a data center, and a data hall plan shows them through its labels, FRONT arrows and sheet notes. The entrance room takes the provider cables. The MDA holds the main cross-connect and core equipment. An optional IDA holds an intermediate cross-connect. Each HDA holds the horizontal cross-connect for a group of rows, like a telecommunications room. An optional ZDA is a point along the horizontal cabling with no cross-connect. The EDA cabinets hold the servers. Trace a link from the EDA: horizontal cable to its HDA, through a ZDA where there is one, then backbone to the MDA, through an IDA where there is one. Name every space and cable.

Rows face front to front across a cold aisle with perforated tiles and back to back across a hot aisle. Cold-aisle containment closes the supply aisle and the room runs warm; hot-aisle containment closes the exhaust aisle and the room stays cool. Both depend on closing every gap: blanking panels, seals, grommets.

The mobile infrastructure rack is a 42U training rack holding servers, switches, routers, PDUs and a UPS, the component types a production data center spreads across its EDAs, distribution areas, entrance room and electrical room. The same elevation rules apply to it.

A site survey checks the building against the current drawings and the owner standard before work starts. Measure what the drawing dimensions, to stated faces and heights, twice where it matters; count what the drawing counts; look above the ceiling. Record each finding with its location, value and unit, what the drawing shows, and a photo reference. Give it its primary class, and note any second class in the entry’s notes column. A field difference from the drawings and a drawing that does not meet the owner standard each go to an RFI. A site constraint limits how or when the work is done and goes into the plan. An observation is recorded with no action. An RFI carries its number, the references, the condition with measurements, one answerable question, an optional suggestion and the impact. While it is pending, the affected work does not proceed on an assumption and is not redesigned in the field.
