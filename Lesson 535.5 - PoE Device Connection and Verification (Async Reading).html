535.5.1 What the device draws, and what the port sets aside

Two figures decide whether a powered device runs on a switch port: what the device draws, and what the port sets aside for it.

In PoE, the switch is the power sourcing equipment: it supplies the power. The camera, access point, or telephone at the far end is the powered device: it consumes the power. A standards-based switch port supplies nothing until it has worked through three stages.

  1. Detection. The port applies a low voltage and looks for the signature a powered device presents. A laptop presents none, so its port stays unpowered.
  2. Classification. The device tells the port which power class it belongs to.
  3. Power. The port switches on full power, and the switch counts the port against its shared pool.

Each end of the link has its own figure.

  • What the device draws. The maximum is stated on its specification sheet.
  • What the port sets aside. A fixed figure for the class the device declared.

Counting by class, or by draw

Some switches count each powered port at the figure for its declared class. Others count the power the port is delivering, which is usually lower. Where you do not know how a switch counts, size the job by class, the larger figure.

The classes, and what each one costs the pool

A class is a bracket, not a measurement. A device that draws 7 W and a device that draws 12 W both fall in Class 3. A port that counts by class sets aside 15.4 W for either. The figures below come from the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) standards 802.3af and 802.3at.

535.5.2 The PoE standards and their ceilings

Three IEEE standards define PoE, and each sets a ceiling on what one port supplies.

The port ceiling is the most one port will supply. Some of that power is lost in up to 100 m of cable, so each standard also sets the most a device may draw at its end of the link. Compare a device’s stated maximum draw with that second figure.

The three PoE standards: the trade name, the classes, the most one port supplies, and the most a device may draw.
StandardTrade nameClassesMost one port suppliesMost a device may draw
IEEE 802.3af (Type 1)PoE0 to 315.4 W12.95 W
IEEE 802.3at (Type 2)PoE+0 to 430.0 W25.5 W
IEEE 802.3bt (Type 3)PoE++ or four-pair PoE (4PPoE)up to 660 W51 W
IEEE 802.3bt (Type 4)PoE++ or 4PPoEup to 890 W71.3 W

802.3bt adds Classes 5 to 8. A port sets aside 45 W for Class 5, 60 W for Class 6, 75 W for Class 7, and 90 W for Class 8. It carries the power for these classes on all four pairs of the cable. A port built to a later standard powers the devices of the earlier ones: an 802.3at port runs a Class 2 telephone at the telephone’s class.

What a ceiling means on the job

No setting raises a port’s ceiling. An 802.3af port cannot run a device that needs 20 W, however much the pool has left. Such a device may fail to start, may start and restart, or may run with some functions off, depending on the model.

A switch maker may publish a per-port figure above the standard’s. Read the maker’s figure for the switch in front of you on its specification sheet.

Passive power is not PoE

Some switches and injectors offer passive power: a fixed voltage, often 24 V or 48 V, placed on the cable with no detection and no classification. A passive output can damage a device that was not built for it. A port labeled 802.3af, 802.3at, or 802.3bt detects first. Never select a passive mode, or connect a passive injector, unless the device’s specification sheet calls for passive power at that voltage.

535.5.3 Reading power from a specification sheet

A specification sheet states a device’s power in three lines, usually in a block headed Hardware, Electrical, or Power.

The power lines of a hypothetical ceiling access point’s specification sheet, and what each tells you.
LineValueWhat it tells you
Power methodPoE (802.3af)The device takes its power from the cabling; the value names the standard it needs. A line that lists only a power adapter means the device needs an electrical outlet
Supported voltage range44 to 57 V direct current (DC)The band the device accepts at its input. A standards-based port supplies a voltage inside that band. A single fixed voltage such as 24 V points to passive power
Maximum power consumption11.2 WThe figure to compare with what a device may draw on the port, and the figure that gives the class. 11.2 W is above 6.49 W and not above 12.95 W, so it falls in Class 3

What a sheet does not always make clear

  • The class is often missing. Read the wattage against the class brackets to find it.
  • “PoE” alone states no power. A sheet that says PoE and gives no wattage and no class leaves the draw unknown. Ask the manufacturer for the figure; do not assume the lowest class.
  • “PoE+” means the device may need Class 4. An 802.3af port might not run it. Confirm the wattage before anyone orders the device.
  • Maximum is not typical. The stated maximum is what the device may draw with every function running: heaters, infrared lights, every radio. Plan on the maximum. Several devices that reach it at once can exhaust a pool sized on typical figures.

535.5.4 Sizing a switch against a work order

A work order names devices. A PoE switch has a budget: the total power its pool can give. Add up what the devices need before anyone climbs a ladder.

Start from what the switch has left. The controller or the switch reports its PoE budget and the power already in use. For a new work order, size against the budget less the power in use, not the whole budget: the ports already in service keep their share. Then find each device’s class from its sheet and add what the switch sets aside.

A work order on another job (hypothetical): 12 devices against a switch with a 240 W budget and 38.5 W in use, counted by class.
LineWatts
Room left: 240 − 38.5201.5 W
4 ceiling access points, 11.2 W each, Class 3: 4 × 15.461.6 W
6 desk telephones, 4.6 W each, Class 2: 6 × 7.042.0 W
2 outdoor cameras, 19.8 W each, Class 4: 2 × 30.060.0 W
Total set aside: 61.6 + 42.0 + 60.0163.6 W
Left after the work order: 201.5 − 163.637.9 W

The work order fits, provided the switch has 12 free PoE ports and the two outdoor cameras are on 802.3at ports.

Adding the draws on a switch that counts by class

The same 12 devices draw at most 44.8, 27.6, and 39.6 W, for 112.0 W. Taken from the 201.5 W of room, that sum leaves 89.5 W, which overstates the room by 51.6 W on a switch that counts by class. Add the figure the switch counts: the class figure or the draw. Where you do not know which figure that is, add by class.

How many fit

To find how many devices of one kind a budget can carry, divide the room left by what one device sets aside, and round down. On the job above, 37.9 ÷ 15.4 = 2.46, so two more Class 3 devices fit, not three.

A switch at the limit of its budget refuses power to the next device, or on some models drops power from a port already in service. Many sites keep part of the pool in reserve for later additions; the site’s design standard sets how much.

Match each stated maximum draw to the class its bracket gives.

535.5.5 Switch or injector

PoE comes from one of two places: the switch port itself, or a midspan injector placed between a switch port and the device.

A PoE switch is endspan equipment: the power and the data leave the same port. A midspan injector is a separate unit with its own electrical supply. It takes data in on one port from any switch port, PoE or not, and sends data and power out on another port toward the device. Injectors serve where the switch has no PoE, where its ports are full or its budget is spent, or where a device needs more than the switch’s ports supply.

Figure — Endspan and midspan
Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled Endspan and midspan, and shows two rows. The upper row, Endspan: power from the switch port, shows a PoE switch port joined to a powered device by one line labeled data and power.

The lower row, Midspan: power from an injector, shows any switch port joined by a line labeled data only to an injector with its own power supply, and the injector joined to a powered device by a line labeled data and power.

Reading an injector

  • The ports are marked. One takes the data in, often marked Data In or LAN (Local Area Network); the other feeds the device, often PoE Out. A cord in the wrong port leaves the device without power.
  • The standard is on its label. An injector built to 802.3af, 802.3at, or 802.3bt detects and classifies the device as a switch port does. A passive injector does not.
  • Its power is its own. The switch does not count an injector’s output against its budget; the injector’s rating is the ceiling for its one device.
  • One power source to a device. Do not also connect a device fed by an injector to a PoE port.

The whole path from the switch port to the device, injector and cords included, stays within 100 m.

On the mock-up, the injector your instructor demonstrates is a standards-based 802.3bt unit; the instructor confirms the standard on its label before the demonstration. Do not use a passive injector on the mock-up.

Lesson 535.5 · Section 2 Powering and Patching the Device

535.5.6 Connecting the device through the installed link

The path from a switch port to a device has fixed parts and moving parts. The cords are the moving parts.

The horizontal cable, the outlet, and the panel port were installed, tested, and labeled when the building was cabled. Two cords complete the path: the patch cord from the panel port to the switch port in the rack, and the device cord from the outlet to the device. Anyone can move a cord, so check each cord with the same care as a termination.

Before you handle a cord

Take three facts from the work order and the port map (the site’s record of which switch port and network serve each outlet), and read each one on the hardware.

  • The outlet. Its label is on the faceplate.
  • The panel port the outlet’s cable lands on. Its label is on the patch panel.
  • The switch port the work order assigns. On many sites, only the network team patches in the rack. On the mock-up, you connect and disconnect device cords at the outlets of the bay assigned to you. In the rack, you reseat a patch cord, or move it back to the port the record or work order names, only where the work order allows patching. Any other cord move is escalated to the network team.

A cord one position off is a quiet error: a neighboring PoE port also powers the device, so the device starts while its record names the wrong port.

535.5.7 The controller and the port view

A UniFi switch takes its settings from a controller running the UniFi Network application. The installer reads and changes the switch’s ports in the application.

On the mock-up, the controller is a UniFi Cloud Key, opened in a web browser at the address the site lead posts. The switch is adopted by the controller: the controller holds its settings and sends each change to the switch, which applies it a short time later.

What the port view shows

The application lists each device it manages. Select the switch to see its ports, then select a port to see its details. The names and the layout change between versions; these are the readings you look for.

Finding the port that serves your device

A device’s MAC address is printed on its label. The controller lists each wired client with its MAC address and the switch port it was heard on. Match the two: a port is yours when the client on it carries your device’s MAC address. A port number read off a cord in the rack is a second check, not the first.

Where a label carries no MAC address, unplug the device at its outlet and watch which port stops delivering power, then plug it back in. Until its port is on the right network, the client list may show the device with no Internet Protocol (IP) address; the MAC address and the port still appear.

An account that can change more than the work order assigns

An account in the Network application can usually change any port, network, or wireless setting the application manages. The work order sets which one setting you may change, on which port, and for how long. Read first, record the starting value of the assigned setting, then change that setting and nothing else.

535.5.8 Power, speed, and duplex

The switch reports what its port supplies, and the device shows whether it is running. Read together, the switch’s figure and the device’s behavior separate a supply problem from a device problem. The switch also reports how the link came up.

At the switch, the reading is a number: the PoE power on the port. It is usually below the device’s stated maximum, because a device seldom runs everything at once. The figure includes the small loss in the cable. At the device, the reading is behavior: a start-up sequence, a light, a sound. Take both before you change anything.

Each port also has a PoE setting. On UniFi switches it is usually named PoE or PoE+ when the port may supply power and Off when it may not; some models also offer a passive mode. Never select it for a standards-based device.

A dark device is not one fault

Three rows of the table above arrive as the same report: the device does not work. The reading at the switch separates them.

Speed and duplex

When a link comes up, the two ports settle by autonegotiation on the best speed and duplex both support, unless someone has fixed a value on one end. On copper switch ports, speed is 10, 100, or 1000 Mbps. Duplex is whether both ends transmit at once: full duplex means they do, and half duplex means they take turns.

Speed and duplex readings on a port, what each means, and what to do.
ReadingWhat happenedWhat to do
Full duplex, at the device’s top speedBoth ends settled on the best they shareRecord it
100 Mbps, full duplexThe device supports only 100 Mbps, or the path could not carry 1000 MbpsCheck the device’s sheet. If it lists 1000 Mbps, look at the path next
Half duplexThe far end is a half-duplex device, or someone fixed its settings so that it does not negotiateRecord and report it. The owner of the far end removes its fixed setting. The network team removes any fixed setting at the switch end at the same time

A link at 10 or 100 Mbps carries data on two pairs, pins 1 and 2 and pins 3 and 6; a link at 1000 Mbps uses all four. A fault on the pair on pins 4 and 5 or pins 7 and 8 can leave a link that works at 100 Mbps and cannot reach 1000 Mbps. An 802.3af or 802.3at port may carry its power on either set of pairs, so the same fault can also stop the power. Read the power and the speed together.

Lesson 535.5 · Section 4 The Network, the Reach, and the Record

535.5.9 The port’s network

A switch port puts its device’s traffic onto one network. The device answers only when its IP address belongs to that network.

A network carries traffic in frames: blocks of data, each with the MAC address of its sender and of its destination. A tag is a field in a frame that names the Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN) the frame belongs to. A camera sends its traffic untagged: its frames carry no tag. The switch port decides which network those frames join. In the UniFi Network application, the network for untagged traffic is the port’s native network, which the controller ties to a VLAN. An access point sends its own traffic untagged too. The traffic of each wireless network it serves carries the tag of the VLAN that wireless network is mapped to, unless that VLAN is the port’s native network.

A static IP address is one typed into the device, not given to it by a server. A camera set to a static IP address on the camera network answers only when its port’s native network is the camera network. The /24 after an address means that its first three numbers name its network: 192.168.80.21 /24 belongs to the network 192.168.80.0/24.

On the mock-up, the network team sets a camera’s IP address and addressing method once, before installation, and writes them on the card kept with the camera. Read them there. The controller’s client list may show no IP address for a wired camera.

Changing a port’s network

  • Change only the port the work order assigns, and only after its connected client’s MAC address matches your device’s label.
  • Record the starting network first.
  • Apply, then wait. The controller sends the change to the switch; allow ~1 min before you test.
  • Put it back when the work order says the change ends, and read the port again to confirm.

Some sites set a port through a saved port profile rather than one field at a time. Where a port follows a profile, its native network may be fixed by the profile; the site lead or the network team says how to change it.

535.5.10 Proving that the device reaches the network

A device that is powered and linked may still not answer. Reach is a separate fact. A ping tests it.

Two readings settle reach.

  • The switch has heard the device. A client MAC address on the device’s port shows that the device sent traffic and that it crossed the cabling to the switch.
  • The device answers a ping. A ping sends a short message to an IP address and waits for an answer. A reply shows that the device holds that address, is on the network its port gives it, and can be reached from where you ran the ping.

Where you ping from

A laptop on the same network as the device reaches it through the switch alone, so a ping tests the cabling, the port, and the device. On many sites the laptop sits on the office network and the device on a separate network. On the mock-up, the camera network is separate from the network the laptops use. A ping then crosses the gateway, the router that joins the networks. The gateway, or a firewall beside it, holds rules that decide which traffic may pass between the networks; the Kestrel Network Plan states what may pass on the mock-up.

Across a gateway, a reply is strong evidence: the device answered, and the whole route carried the traffic. A failure is weaker evidence, because the gateway and its rules are now part of what was tested.

ping — a laptop on an office network reaches a camera on another network (another job)
C:\> ping 192.168.80.21

Pinging 192.168.80.21 with 32 bytes of data:
Reply from 192.168.80.21: bytes=32 time=2ms TTL=63
Reply from 192.168.80.21: bytes=32 time=1ms TTL=63
Reply from 192.168.80.21: bytes=32 time=1ms TTL=63
Reply from 192.168.80.21: bytes=32 time=1ms TTL=63

Ping statistics for 192.168.80.21:
    Packets: Sent = 4, Received = 4, Lost = 0 (0% loss),
Approximate round trip times in milli-seconds:
    Minimum = 1ms, Maximum = 2ms, Average = 1ms
  • Reply from 192.168.80.21 — the answer came from the camera’s own address.
  • bytes=32 — the size of each message. time — how long the round trip took, in milliseconds.
  • TTL (Time to Live) — a count each router lowers by one as it forwards the reply. Its starting value depends on the device.
  • Sent = 4, Received = 4, Lost = 0 — the summary line to record.

What a failed ping does not prove

A failed ping prints Request timed out. for each message, or a line saying that the destination is unreachable. Either has several causes: a device set never to answer a ping, an address other than the one recorded for it, a port on another network, or a firewall rule. Record the result as Windows printed it, beside the power and MAC readings.

Lesson 535.5 · Section 4 The Network, the Reach, and the Record

535.5.11 Joining the lab wireless network

An access point is powered like a camera. A client that joins its wireless network, takes an address, and reaches the network behind it proves the access point is working.

A wireless network is known by its Service Set Identifier (SSID), the name a laptop lists when it scans. The network team creates each SSID, sets its security, and maps it to a network. The installer joins the SSID as a client and proves that the access point serves it.

Most small-site SSIDs use Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 (WPA2)-Personal or WPA3-Personal: every client signs in with the same passphrase. The passphrase is a credential. Type it when Windows asks for the network security key. Never write it in a record, show it in a screenshot, or type it into an Artificial Intelligence (AI) tool.

The client check

  1. Join the SSID from the Wi-Fi list in Windows, and type the passphrase.
  2. Open a command prompt (search the Start menu for cmd), and run netsh wlan show interfaces to confirm which SSID the laptop joined.
  3. In the same window, run ipconfig and read the IPv4 address of the Wi-Fi adapter. Check that it is on the network the SSID is mapped to.
  4. Ping the gateway of that network, and open a local service, such as a controller’s sign-in page, in the browser.
  5. Forget the network: Settings > Network & internet > Wi-Fi > Manage known networks, select the SSID, then Forget. Reconnect to the network the laptop used before.
netsh wlan show interfaces — a laptop on another job’s staff network, other lines left out
C:\> netsh wlan show interfaces

    Name                   : Wi-Fi
    State                  : connected
    SSID                   : NORTHGATE-STAFF
    Authentication         : WPA2-Personal
    Signal                 : 86%
  • Name — the Wi-Fi adapter. State — connected when the laptop has joined an SSID.
  • SSID — the wireless network the laptop joined. Authentication — its security type.
  • Signal — the strength the laptop receives from the access point.

The output names the security type and never shows the passphrase. Do not run netsh wlan show profile with key=clear, which prints a stored passphrase, during a client check.

Reading the address

An address on the SSID’s network, with a reply from its gateway, proves the access point passed the laptop’s traffic to the network the SSID is mapped to. An address that starts with 169.254 is a link-local address: Windows chose it because no address server answered. The laptop joined the SSID; the network behind it gave no address. The network team owns that network and the address server behind it.

Lesson 535.5 · Section 4 The Network, the Reach, and the Record

535.5.12 Recording the connection

The device outlasts the work order. The next technician works from the record. Readings are the part of it that technician can check against the equipment.

A port map entry ties one outlet to what serves it: the outlet’s label, the panel port, the switch and port it is patched to, the network that port puts it on, and the device with its address. The outlet and the panel port were fixed when the building was cabled. The switch port and the network can change; the port map records them.

Lesson 535.5 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

535.5.13 What you covered

Size the device to the port, connect it, read its power and link, put its port on its network, prove that it answers, and write down what each reading said.

Sizing the device to the port

A standards-based PoE port detects a device, classifies it, and only then supplies power. A switch that counts by class sets aside the figure for the declared class; a device that declares no class is Class 0, at 15.4 W. Size a work order against the budget less the power in use, adding what the switch sets aside.

On a specification sheet, maximum power consumption is the line to compare and the line that gives the class. A passive output is not PoE and can damage a device not built for it.

Powering and patching

A PoE switch port supplies power itself; a midspan injector adds power on the cord to one device, from its own supply and outside the switch’s budget. Before handling a cord, confirm the outlet, the panel port, and the assigned switch port. Check each cord’s category, condition, length, and seating.

Reading the port in the controller

The UniFi Network application shows each port’s PoE power, its class where the version shows it, its speed and duplex, its native network, and its client’s MAC address, which identifies the port that serves your device. Power shown with a dark device points to the device; no power with room in the budget points to the path or the device. A device whose sheet lists 1000 Mbps and that links at 100 Mbps points to the path.

The network, the reach, and the record

A port’s native network decides which network its device’s untagged traffic joins. Change it only on the assigned port, with its starting value recorded, and put it back when the work order ends. A ping reply proves reach; a failed ping across a gateway has several causes. A laptop that joins an SSID and takes an address on its network proves the access point serves clients. The passphrase stays out of every record. A port map entry records the outlet, the panel port, the switch port, the network, and the device with its address, beside the readings, the date, and a name.
