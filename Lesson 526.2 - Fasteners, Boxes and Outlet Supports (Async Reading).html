Kestrel Communications is the telecommunications contractor on project HSOC-26-014, the Harlow Street Operations Center. Kestrel’s site lead directs the crew on the mock-up. Before production rough-in starts on Level 2, the owner, Calder Regional Services, requires an installation mock-up of the typical Level 2 work area. The mock-up is built on five free-standing training walls. Each bay of a wall stands for one work area. Each bay carries three data outlets: a flush outlet in an opening cut in a finished wall, a second outlet on a box with a plaster ring, and a surface outlet fed by surface raceway. A plaster ring is a cover that holds the outlet in front of the box. Surface raceway is a channel with a snap-on cover, fixed to the face of the wall. The owner’s representative inspects the supports of the flush outlet and the surface outlet before any cable is pulled to them.

The mock-up walls are not built like the walls of the building. Each face is a set of brown hardboard panels screwed to an aluminum frame, with an open cavity behind them and no studs. A screw point or a saw blade that passes through the panel enters a cavity that carries cable. Each bay’s upper panel starts as a practice panel: installers practice fastening and cutting on it, then replace it with a fresh panel.

Worked examples on this page use products and dimensions chosen to show a method. On the mock-up, the figures come from the project documents and from the product cards, the printed cards set out on site that give each product’s name and figures.

526.2.1 What a hardboard panel can hold

The mock-up panels are thin hardboard with an open cavity behind them, and no stud or blocking.

Hardboard is a dense, thin sheet of wood fiber pressed under heat. On the mock-up, each panel measures 60 cm by 80 cm (23 5/8 in by 31 1/2 in) and is held to the aluminum frame by four panel screws, one near each corner. Behind each panel is the cavity between the wall’s two faces. Cable runs through that cavity, bundled and tied to the frame’s horizontal members. It leaves through the open top of the frame to the cable ladder, the open tray that runs above the walls.

The threads of a screw grip only the panel’s thickness, so a screw that keeps turning after its head seats tears out the threads it has cut in the panel and leaves a stripped hole. The point of the screw comes out in the cavity, where it can reach a cable. A device that hangs off the panel levers against the few threads that hold it.

The owner’s rules for fastening to the panels

Clause 27 05 00 4.5 of the owner’s standard, mock-up edition, sets these rules:

  • Boxes, brackets, straps, and raceway bases are fixed with pan-head or washer-head screws.
  • Each screw is short enough that its point stays clear of cable in the cavity.
  • A hollow-wall anchor or a through-bolt is used only where a screw strips its hole, or to mount a device heavier than a box or a plate.
  • No fastener goes into the aluminum frame.
  • No cut or fastener falls within 2 in of a panel edge or a panel screw.

A through-bolt passes through a drilled hole in the panel and is held by a washer and nut on the cavity side. Its hole is sized for the bolt to pass through freely. General note 5 on sheet TW-001 calls the plate in this rule a backplate: a flat mounting plate, such as a camera mounting plate.

General note 4 on sheet TW-001 applies the 2 in rule at a panel joint, the frame rail between the upper and lower panels. Electrical metallic tubing (EMT) and raceway may cross a joint where sheet TW-201 shows them, but their strap and base screws stay 2 in clear of the panel edges and panel screws. A one-hole strap is a formed metal strap that wraps over the EMT and is fixed to the wall by a single screw through its one hole; clause 27 05 00 4.8 sets one screw per strap.

A pan head is a low dome with a flat underside. A washer head carries a built-in washer. Both press flat on the face of the panel and spread the clamping force over a ring of material around the hole. A flat head is tapered underneath and is made to sit in a countersunk hole. In a thin sheet that taper works as a wedge, which can split the surface or pull the head through it.

Material near a panel edge breaks out more readily than material in the middle of the sheet. The frame stands behind every panel edge. Each panel screw threads into a nut in the frame’s channel. A fastener close to an edge or a panel screw can reach the aluminum.

Screw length

The mock-up work orders put a figure on “short enough”: a screw is no longer than the panel thickness plus 1/2 in. The length of a pan-head or washer-head screw is measured from under the head to the point, so a screw within the limit leaves no more than 1/2 in of its length behind the panel. If a panel measures 3/8 in thick, the longest screw allowed is 7/8 in. On the mock-up, the panel thickness is given for each wall. Use that figure before you choose a screw.

526.2.2 Screws, bits, and drives

Choose a screw by its head, its length, and its thread. Choose a bit by the hole it makes or the drive it turns.

The parts of a screw

A screw has a head, a drive recessed into the head, a shank, a thread, and a point. Its size is written as a gauge and a length: #10 × 1 in is a number-ten screw one inch long. The gauge is a number that stands for a diameter: the larger the number, the thicker the screw. Gauges #6, #8, and #10 are common in low-voltage work. The thread stands out from a solid core, also called the root.

The thread suits a screw to a material:

  • A wood screw has a coarse thread for solid wood and wood products.
  • A sheet-metal screw has a sharper thread that runs up to the head, for sheet metal, plastics, and other thin sheet material.
  • A self-drilling screw carries a small drill point ahead of its thread and makes its own hole in light-gauge steel, such as a steel stud.
  • A machine screw threads into a tapped hole or a nut. A plaster ring is held to its box by two machine screws that thread into tapped holes in the box.

On the mock-up panels, choose from the screws set out on site: clause 27 05 00 4.5 sets their head, and the work orders their length.

Drives and driver bits

The drive is the shape of the recess: Phillips (a cross), square (also called Robertson), star (often sold as Torx), and slotted. A driver bit fits one drive and one size: a #2 Phillips bit fits most #8 Phillips screws; a #1 fits smaller screws. A bit of the wrong type or size, or a worn one, rides up out of the recess under torque. That fault is called cam-out. It damages the head and the bit.

Drill bits and the holes they make

A pilot hole is drilled before a screw is driven, so that the screw goes in straight and does not split the material. It is smaller than the thread and about the size of the core, so the threads cut into the material around the hole. A drill/driver drills a pilot hole with a twist drill bit; an impact driver drills one with a hex-shank drill bit. A clearance hole is the size of the thread or larger, so the screw passes through freely. The mounting holes in a box or a bracket flange are clearance holes; the screw holds in the material behind them. The hole for an anchor is sized to the anchor, not the screw. The anchor’s packaging gives the size.

526.2.3 Hollow-wall anchors

A hollow-wall anchor holds in a thin board with empty space behind it. Each type is made for a stated range of board thickness and rated for a load in a named material.

Where there is no stud, blocking, or solid material behind a board, a screw holds only in the board. A hollow-wall anchor spreads the load over more of the board: it expands in the hole, cuts a coarse thread into the board, or opens out behind it. The types differ in the hole they need, the load they carry, and whether they can be taken out again from the room side.

Open each card.

Grip range and load rating

The grip range is the range of board thickness the anchor is made to set in. In a board thinner than the range, an anchor that clamps behind the board has too far to travel and does not tighten against it. In a board thicker than the range, it cannot reach through to open behind the board.

The load rating is the load the anchor carries in a named material, usually gypsum board of a stated thickness. A rating belongs to the material it was tested in and does not carry over to another one. Some packages print the load at which the anchor failed in testing, with a factor to divide it by; others print the working load. Check which figure the package gives before you compare it with the load the anchor must carry.

Anchors on the mock-up

The mock-up work orders allow only anchors that can be removed from the room side and leave nothing loose in the cavity behind the panels. Of the five types above, the plastic expansion anchor and the self-drilling threaded anchor meet that rule. A sleeve anchor and a strap toggle stay in the panel, and the wings of a spring-wing toggle bolt drop into the cavity. A part that drops behind a panel lies among the cable in the cavity, where it can be reached only by taking the panel off. To remove an anchor, back the screw out with a screwdriver, then unscrew or draw the anchor out of its hole. If an anchor will not come out, leave it in place and tell the site lead. Do not push it through the panel: that drops it loose in the cavity.

Anchor packaging rates an anchor in the materials it was tested in, usually gypsum board, and gives no rating to carry over to the hardboard panels. On the mock-up panels, clause 27 05 00 4.5 decides when an anchor is used, the work orders limit the choice to anchors removed from the room side, and the anchor’s grip range must include the panel thickness.

526.2.4 Selecting for a substrate and a load

Name the substrate and what lies behind it, weigh the load, apply the project’s rules, then read the product data.

The substrate is the material a fastener goes into. The same box needs a different fastener on a wood stud, on gypsum board between studs, on a concrete block wall, and on a mock-up panel. Selection runs in the same order every time:

  1. The substrate. Its material, its thickness, and what is behind it: a stud, blocking, an open cavity, or solid masonry.
  2. The load. The weight of the item and of anything it will carry, such as cable or a device fitted later. Note the direction as well. A load that pulls down along the face of the wall is a shear load. A load that pulls the fastener straight out of the wall is a tension load, often called pull-out. A device that stands off the wall puts both on its top fasteners.
  3. The project’s rules. The owner’s standard and the drawing notes may limit the fastener type, its length, or where it goes. On the mock-up panels, clause 27 05 00 4.5 decides whether a screw or an anchor is used.
  4. The product. From its packaging or data sheet: the type, the length, the grip range, the rating in this substrate, and the bit size.
  5. The source. Record the product name, and whether each figure came from the packaging or the data sheet.

Worked selection — an enclosure on a gypsum wall

The situation. A wall-mounted enclosure of about 12 lb goes on 1/2 in gypsum board. It has four mounting holes. A stud finder shows a wood stud behind one of them and an open cavity behind the other three.

The hole over the stud. A wood screw long enough to pass through the enclosure back and the board into the stud, driven in a pilot hole sized from the screw maker’s chart. No anchor.

The other three holes. A hollow-wall anchor whose grip range includes 1/2 in. Its working load in 1/2 in gypsum board, with the margin its maker states, must cover the share of the enclosure’s weight that each of the three anchors carries. The enclosure stands off the wall, so its top fasteners carry pull-out as well as shear. The bit is the size on the anchor’s packaging.

The record. Each fastener, its bit, and its source: the product name, and the packaging or data sheet it came from.

Match each situation to the selection that fits it.

526.2.5 Drill/driver, impact driver, and hole saw

Both cordless tools drive screws. The drill/driver has a clutch that stops it at a set torque; the impact driver has none.

Drill/driver

  • A keyless chuck holds round-shank drill bits, hex-shank bits, and hole-saw arbors.
  • A numbered clutch ring sets a torque. When the screw reaches that torque, the clutch slips with a clicking sound and the bit stops turning the screw.
  • A drill setting on the same ring bypasses the clutch, for drilling.
  • A speed switch gives a low range, with more torque, for driving and for large cutters, and a high range for small drill bits.

Impact driver

  • A quick-release collet takes 1/4 in hex-shank bits only.
  • When the screw resists, a hammer inside strikes rapid rotational blows. The tool gives more torque than a drill/driver of the same battery voltage.
  • No clutch: it keeps turning until the trigger is released.
  • Loud in use. The mock-up work orders require hearing protection for both installers at a wall while an impact driver is running.

Driving into a thin panel

In hardboard, a screw strips when its head has seated and the tool keeps turning it. With a drill/driver, start on a low clutch number and raise it one step at a time until the clutch slips just as the head seats, then finish the screw by hand with a screwdriver. With an impact driver, release the trigger as the head touches the panel. Set a powered tool down only after it has stopped turning.

To remove a screw, set the tool to reverse, or turn it out by hand with a screwdriver. Under clause 27 05 00 4.5 and the mock-up work orders, back out a screw that has stripped its hole and set a removable hollow-wall anchor in its place.

The hole saw

A hole saw is a toothed steel cup fitted to an arbor, the shank that goes in the chuck. A pilot bit through the center of the arbor stands out beyond the teeth. The size of the hole is stamped on the cup.

  • Fit the saw to the arbor and the arbor to the drill/driver on its drill setting, in the low speed range.
  • Set the pilot bit on the center mark. The pilot enters the panel first and holds the saw on center as the teeth start to cut.
  • Hold the saw square to the panel so that the whole rim of teeth cuts at once. A tilted saw cuts on one side and wanders.
  • Ease the pressure as the teeth break through, so they do not tear the back of the panel or leave a ragged edge.
  • The plug stays in the cup. Remove it with the drill/driver stopped.

526.2.6 Cutting an opening from a template

An opening is traced from the manufacturer’s template and cut to the line.

A flush outlet in a finished wall sits in an opening cut to fit its bracket. The bracket’s manufacturer prints a template, a full-size outline of the opening, on the packaging or on a sheet. The owner’s standard gives a nominal opening for the flush outlet in clause 27 05 00 4.8, and says the opening is cut to the bracket’s template. Where a product differs from that nominal opening, the product’s own data governs.

Tracing

Hold the template on the panel with its center lines on the center mark of the outlet’s position, square to the bay’s edges. Set a level against the template’s long side and turn the template until the bubble is centered, so that the outline is plumb. Trace the outline in pencil.

Cutting with a jab saw

A jab saw, also called a drywall saw or keyhole saw, has a narrow, pointed blade with coarse teeth. The point lets it start its own cut, a plunge cut: push it through the panel just inside the traced line, then saw along the line, holding the blade square to the face of the panel. Keep your free hand out of the saw’s path, including the stretch of line ahead of the blade.

Behind the panel is the cavity, with cable tied to the frame and the back of the other face’s panel beyond it. An outlet support on the other face may already stand in the cavity opposite yours. Insert the blade only as deep as the cut needs. The owner’s representative sets a maximum blade depth for each wall and marks it with tape on the blade of the saw used at that wall, measured from the face of the panel. Keep the tape outside the face of the panel.

Fitting

Hold the bracket in the opening to test the fit. Where it binds, trim the edge with the saw, a little at a time, and try it again. Stop trimming as soon as it seats. An opening cut larger than the bracket’s flange leaves a gap that the flange cannot cover. The only repair is a new panel.

Put these steps in order, from the first to the last.

526.2.7 Replacing a wall panel

A panel comes off the frame only when nothing is fastened in it and no cable passes through it.

Each panel is held to the frame by four panel screws, one near each corner. Each screw threads into a channel nut, a nut that slides in a channel of the aluminum frame. A panel that has been cut and fastened can be taken down and a fresh one fitted in its place.

Before the panel comes down

General note 11 on sheet TW-001 sets the condition: replace a panel only when no cable passes through it, and remove first any raceway that crosses a panel joint. Every box, screw, and anchor fastened in the panel comes out before the panel screws do.

Holding the panel

One person holds the panel flat against the frame with both hands for the whole swap. The other removes and fits the screws. Both wear gloves against the panel’s edges. The two top screws carry the panel’s weight while the two bottom screws come out, so the top screws come out last. The two lift the panel off the frame together.

Fitting the fresh panel

  • The holder sets the fresh panel against the frame with its screw holes in line with the screw positions.
  • Start every screw by hand. A screw started at an angle cross-threads in the nut and jams; turning it by hand for the first few turns shows that it is running true.
  • A channel nut can slide along its channel away from its screw hole while a panel is off. Slide it back into line before you start that screw.
  • Tighten every screw once all of them are started.
  • Check the result: the panel sits flush against the frame with no gap, and it does not move when pressed at each corner. If a corner moves, tighten its screw again.

The site lead inspects a fresh panel once it is seated and firm. Do not mark, cut, or fasten anything on it before that inspection.

526.2.8 The low-voltage retrofit bracket

A retrofit bracket mounts an outlet in a wall that is already finished. It goes in from the room side, and nothing is fixed behind the wall first.

A low-voltage retrofit bracket, also sold as an old-work or low-voltage mounting bracket, is an open frame with no back. Its flange lies on the room side of the wall and covers the cut edge of the opening. Behind the wall, two clamp tabs swing out and draw up against the back of the wall as their screws are turned. The faceplate screws to the bracket’s flange. A one-gang bracket takes one faceplate the size of a single device.

The cable comes out of the cavity through the bracket’s open back. The bracket encloses nothing. It is made for low-voltage cabling such as data and communications cable; power wiring is terminated in a box.

The rated thickness

Each bracket is made for a range of wall thickness, printed on its packaging. In a wall thinner than the range, the tabs draw up to the end of their travel without reaching the back of the wall, and the bracket stays loose. In a wall thicker than the range, the tabs cannot swing out behind it. Where the tabs do not grip a mock-up panel, the bracket is fixed instead with pan-head screws through its flange into the panel. The owner’s representative states which method each wall of the mock-up uses.

A sound installation

  • The opening. Cut to the template, with no gap or overcut showing beyond the flange.
  • Plumb. The side of the flange is plumb by a level.
  • Seated. The flange lies flush on the face of the panel all the way around, and the bracket does not move when pressed.
  • Tightened by hand. Clamp screws turned with a screwdriver until the tabs grip. A powered tool can drive them past the point of grip and crack the panel or the bracket.

526.2.9 The box with a plaster ring, and its projection

A plaster ring sets how far in front of the box the faceplate sits. In a building, that distance matches the wall finish. On the mock-up, the box depth and the raise together set how far the outlet stands out from the panel.

A 4 in square box is a steel box whose face measures about 4 in on each side. Common depths are 1 1/2 in and 2 1/8 in. Its sides and back carry knockouts, discs partly cut through so that one can be removed for a conduit connector or a cable clamp. Two corners carry tapped ears for the screws of a cover.

A plaster ring, also called a mud ring, is a cover for the front of the box. It reduces the box opening to the size of one device, for a one-gang ring, or two devices, for a two-gang ring. Its raised collar holds the faceplate a set distance in front of the box. That distance, from the face of the box to the face of the ring, is the ring’s raise. Rings are made in a series of raises, commonly from 1/4 in to 1 1/4 in. The ring fits the box with two machine screws.

In a building

Before the wall is closed, the installer fixes the box to a stud or to a box bracket, a metal bracket that spans between studs. The ring then goes on the box. The finish, such as 5/8 in gypsum board, is then cut around the ring. The raise is chosen to equal the finish thickness, so the face of the ring sits flush with the finished wall and the faceplate seats flat against it. Conduit can enter the box through a knockout. A box with a ring serves where the cable arrives in conduit, and where the outlet carries power wiring.

On the mock-up

The box is screwed to the face of the panel through its back, as details 2 and 5 on sheet TW-301 show, and the ring goes on the front of the box. Nothing surrounds the ring, so its face stands out from the panel. The projection is the distance from the face of the panel to the face of the ring, measured square to the panel. With the box flat on the panel, the projection is the box depth plus the ring raise. Both depend on the product: read the box depth and the ring raise from the product cards on site. Clause 27 05 00 4.8 gives the owner’s value for the mock-up, a one-gang ring with a 1/2 in raise on the second data outlet box. Where a product differs from 4.8, its own data governs the calculation.

Mounting the box

The box is fixed with two screws (clause 27 05 00 4.8) through its back (TW-301, detail 5). Hold the box on its mark with a level on its top edge, center the bubble, and mark the two screw holes through the box back. Drill the pilot holes and drive the two screws. If the bubble has moved, loosen one screw, turn the box until the bubble is centered, and tighten the screw again. Then fit the ring to the front of the box with its two screws.

Measuring the projection

Hold the end of the tape on the face of the ring and the blade square to the panel, then read the distance at the panel face. Box depth and ring raise are nominal figures, so a measured projection can differ slightly from the expected one. The tolerance is the one set for the job. Beyond it, look for a box or ring other than the one on the product cards, a box not flat on the panel, or a ring not seated on its box.

Lesson 526.2 · Section 3 Outlet Supports and Boxes

526.2.10 Bracket or box: telling them apart

Both supports take the same faceplate. They differ in when they go in, what they are fixed to, and what they enclose.

Both present a one-gang opening to the room. On site, installers may call either one a mud ring. The drawings and the owner’s standard keep the names apart. The legend on sheet TW-001 shows the retrofit bracket as its own symbol. The identification list on that sheet names the second data outlet as a 4 in square box with a one-gang plaster ring.

The choice follows from the wall and the cable. A wall already finished, with no access behind it, takes a bracket, or a surface box if the wall cannot be cut. A wall still open, or a cable arriving in conduit, takes a box with a ring set to the finish.

Lesson 526.2 · Section 3 Outlet Supports and Boxes

526.2.11 The surface box and its cable entry

Where a wall cannot be opened, the outlet sits on its surface and the cable arrives in raceway. The cable enters the surface box through one prepared opening.

A surface box mounts on the face of the wall and holds the outlet there. It is made as part of a raceway series. The raceway’s fittings are shaped to meet the entries of boxes in the same series. Detail 4 on sheet TW-301 calls for a surface box of the same series as the raceway, with a fitting matched to the box. On the mock-up, the surface raceway is non-metallic under clause 27 05 00 4.4, and sheet TW-001 calls for a plastic surface box.

Entries

A surface box carries several possible entries on its sides, top, and bottom. On a plastic box each is a breakout, a section molded thin at its edges so that it can be snapped or cut out. A metal box has knockouts instead. You open only the entry the raceway meets, and every other entry stays closed. An open entry with no raceway in it leaves a hole into the box, beside the outlet.

A protected entry

A cable is drawn across the edge of the entry as it is pulled, then rests on that edge. A breakout leaves a ragged edge and thin slivers of plastic, called flash or burrs, which can cut into the cable’s jacket. A protected entry is one whose edge has been made smooth. Cut the burrs away with a deburring tool or a utility knife, cutting away from your hand, until you feel no burr or sharp edge under your fingertip. The raceway fitting then covers the entry.

Fixing

Clause 27 05 00 4.8 sets two screws for each surface box, and the screws follow the same rules as any fastener in the panels: pan-head or washer-head, within the length limit, and 2 in clear of every panel edge and panel screw. Set the box level by a level on its top edge, with the opened entry facing the raceway.

Lesson 526.2 · Section 4 Wrapping Up

526.2.12 What you covered

Fasten into a thin panel with nothing behind it, cut to the line, replace a panel safely, and set each outlet support the way its type requires.

Clause 27 05 00 4.5 of the owner’s standard requires pan-head or washer-head screws in the hardboard panels, short enough that the point stays clear of cable in the cavity. The mock-up work orders set that limit at the panel thickness plus 1/2 in. An anchor or through-bolt is used only where a screw strips or the device is heavier than a box or a plate. Nothing goes into the frame, and no cut or fastener comes within 2 in of a panel edge or a panel screw.

A pilot hole is about the size of the screw’s core. A hollow-wall anchor is chosen by its grip range and its rating in the material it was tested in. On the mock-up, only the plastic expansion and self-drilling threaded types, which come out from the room side, are used.

The drill/driver has a clutch. With the impact driver, release the trigger as the screw head touches the panel. An opening is traced from the manufacturer’s template, started with a plunge cut inside the line, and trimmed only where the bracket binds. A panel comes off only with nothing fastened in it and no cable through it, and every screw of the fresh panel is started by hand.
