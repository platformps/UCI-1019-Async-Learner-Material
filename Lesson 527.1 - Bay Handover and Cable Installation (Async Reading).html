Lesson 527.1 - Bay Handover and Cable Installation

How an installer takes over a bay for cabling, works out the length and route of each cable, pulls a cable down a closed wall, and lays the bay’s cables along a cable ladder to the rack.

Kestrel Communications holds the telecommunications contract for the Harlow Street Operations Center, project HSOC-26-014. The owner, Calder Regional Services, has approved the rough-in of the installation mock-up: five free-standing training walls, each bay with its outlet boxes in place and two of its cables coiled above the wall. The cabling phase follows. Each data outlet in a bay becomes one end of a permanent link, the installed cable from the outlet jack to its patch panel port in the Mobile Infrastructure Rack (MIR), which stands in for the Level 2 telecommunications room.

D. Okafor, the Kestrel site lead, sets the order of the work. The installer who owns a bay accepts it in writing before pulling any cable into it. The installer then works out the new cable’s length and the route of the bay’s cables, pulls the new cable down the wall, lays the bay’s cables along the cable ladder to the MIR, and records each one. The owner’s representative inspects the cables before the installer terminates any of them.

The worked examples on this page use other jobs and other walls, not the values of any bay on the mock-up.

The cabling phase turns each data outlet box on the mock-up into one end of a permanent link.

D. Okafor briefs the teams at the start of the phase: “The owner’s rep walks the cabling before anything gets terminated. By then every cable on that ladder has a length, a route, and an installer who can account for it.”

Horizontal cabling and the permanent link

Horizontal cabling runs from an outlet in a work area to the patch panel that serves it in the telecommunications room. On the mock-up, each bay is one work area. The Mobile Infrastructure Rack (MIR) takes the place of the telecommunications room (27 05 00 4.4).

As the cabling addendum defines it, a permanent link includes the termination at each end. A permanent link excludes patch cords: the cord from a laptop to the outlet and the cord from the patch panel to a switch are not part of it.

Addendum A2 sets the cable for every permanent link on the mock-up: Category 5e Unscreened Unshielded Twisted-Pair (U/UTP), four-pair, Communications Riser (CMR) cable with solid conductors.

Where the work stops

The installer accepts the bay in writing before pulling any cable into it. Later, when the bay’s cables lie on the cable ladder with their spare cable at the rack end, the work reaches a hold point: the installer calls the owner’s representative, who inspects the cables and releases them. The installer terminates no cable at either end before that release.

527.1.2 Accepting a bay

Acceptance puts the condition of a work area on record before the installer who receives it changes anything.

A defect recorded at acceptance belongs to the work done before the handover. A defect found after you have pulled cable into a bay is yours to explain. The site lead therefore asks for acceptance in writing, check by check, before the first cable goes in.

The acceptance checks

The work order lists the acceptance checks. Each check names an element or a group of elements and states the conditions it must meet: what must be in place, what must not move, where a label must sit, and, for a measured item, a figure with its tolerance. For each check you record one of two results:

  • Accepted: every condition in the check is met.
  • Not accepted: at least one condition is not met. Under the result, name each element that is missing or defective and what is wrong with it, as found.

A measured condition is met only inside its tolerance. Where a check reads “18 in of cable, within 3 in” and you measure 14 in, the check is not accepted, and the entry gives the measurement: “14 in of cable, 4 in short of 18 in.”

Inspect, do not repair

Acceptance is an inspection. Press a box to see whether it moves; do not tighten it. Remove a cover only where the work order allows it, and refit it before you leave the bay. Read a coil’s label from the floor; do not take the coil down. Record a fault and leave it as found: the site lead decides whether to repair it, replace it, or leave it as an open item.

527.1.3 Panel ports and identifiers

The label at each end of a permanent link names the other end. The label on each cable end names both.

Production and mock-up identifiers

In production work, the owner’s standard identifies an outlet by the patch panel and port that serve it (27 05 53 2.1): the telecommunications room is “2A”, its first patch panel “2A-PP01”, and the outlet on port 14 of that panel “2A-PP01-014”.

The mock-up uses the wall scheme of sheet TW-001 instead (27 05 53 2.2). A bay identifier names the wall, the face, and the side. An element in the bay takes the bay identifier followed by its code, such as D1. The two together form the outlet identifier. Each element carries one element label with that identifier, fixed to its box or bracket. Until termination, each cable carries a machine-printed label with its outlet identifier at both ends. At termination, the contractor adds the patch panel and port.

Panel ports

Addendum A6 names the patch panel serving each wall and assigns its ports bay by bay, from a fixed table, with the order of D1, D2, and D3 inside each bay. Take each outlet’s port from that table. Do not number ports in the order you happen to terminate them. With a single table covering every panel, a port number tells an inspector which bay and which outlet the port serves without tracing the cable.

527.1.4 The cable length

A cable cut to a planned length covers its slack, its drop, and its service loop, with little surplus left on the cable ladder.

On the mock-up, owner’s standard 27 05 00 4.7 sets a cable’s cut length as the sum of six parts. You measure three of them on site. The owner’s documents set the other three.

The six parts of a cable length on the mock-up, how each is found, and what each provides.
PartHow you find itWhat it provides
Run on the wallMeasured on the face of the panel, from the top of the box (or of the opening, for a bracket) to the top of the panel fieldThe cable inside the wall
RiseMeasured from the top of the panel field straight up to the rung of the cable ladder the cable will lie onThe climb from the wall to the ladder
RouteMeasured along the cable ladder’s path to the MIRThe cable on the ladder
Slack12 in at the outlet (27 05 00 4.7; addendum A5)Cable to strip and terminate the jack, and to cut back and terminate it again
Drop6 ft at the MIR (27 05 00 4.8)The length from the cable ladder down to the patch panel
Service loop10 ft at the MIR end, stored on the cable ladder (27 15 00 3.1; addendum A5)Spare cable at the rack end, so the panel end can be terminated again, or the panel moved, without a new cable

Measuring the route

You can measure a route along a cable ladder without climbing to it. Walk the floor beneath the cable ladder, following its path from below your bay’s J-hook to below the point where it meets the rack, and measure along the floor. Where the route is longer than the tape, mark the tape’s end on the floor and measure on from the mark. Where the cable ladder runs level, the floor distance equals the length along the ladder. Where it climbs or drops on the way, the site lead tells you what to add.

From parts to a cut length

Add the parts in inches, then convert the total to feet and inches. The cut length is the total rounded up to the next whole foot: a cable cut long leaves a few inches of surplus, but a cable cut short cannot be lengthened. Check the total against the owner’s limit: a permanent link is not more than 295 ft (90 m), including slack (27 15 00 2.2).

527.1.5 Route and bend radius

A route plan states where a bundle runs, where it turns, and how tightly its cables may bend.

Walls passed and changes of direction

From the floor, follow the cable ladder from your wall to the MIR. List each wall it passes, in order. Count each change of direction: a corner where the cable ladder turns. Each change of direction is a place where the cables bend.

The minimum bend radius

The bend radius of a cable is the radius of the curve it follows through a bend. Addendum A4 sets the minimum at four times the cable’s outside diameter, the figure ANSI/TIA-568 gives. The outside diameter comes from the cable’s data sheet. Inside the jacket, each pair keeps its twist and its spacing from the other pairs. The cable’s rated performance depends on both. A bend tighter than the minimum can deform the pairs’ twist and spacing.

A bend gauge makes the limit visible on the job: a round object eight times the outside diameter across. Its radius is half that, four times the outside diameter. A cable laid around the gauge, or in a wider curve, is within the limit.

527.1.6 Ties, supports, and capacity

The route plan counts the ties a bundle needs and checks that each support can carry the cables that will lie on it.

The ties rule

The site lead’s rule for the cable ladder: each installer lays their cables together and ties them to the rungs with hook-and-loop ties no more than ~24 in apart, plus a tie within ~6 in of each side of each change of direction. For the plan, the work order sets the estimate of ties as the route in inches divided by 24, rounded up, plus two for each change of direction.

Worked tie estimate for the Linden Street Library route: 62 ft 6 in with two changes of direction.
StepResult
Route in inches: 62 × 12 + 6750 in
750 ÷ 24 = 31.25, rounded up32 ties
Two for each change of direction: 2 × 24 ties
Ties for the bundle36

Rounding down would leave a stretch of cable ladder longer than ~24 in without a tie.

Supports and their capacity

Cable rests only on a support built for it: the cable ladder, or a J-hook. Addendum A4 forbids supporting a cable from another service, such as a pipe, a conduit, or another trade’s hanger. A support’s rated capacity is the number of cables of a stated size that its manufacturer allows on it (27 05 28 2.2). To check a support, count the cables it will carry at its fullest and compare the count with the rating.

On a cable ladder, the fullest point is where the most bundles have joined: usually the last stretch before the rack. Count every cable that will lie there once every installer has laid their cables, not only the cables already in place. On the library job, six work areas of two cables each feed one ladder, and a second ladder joins it with four cables before the rack: 6 × 2 + 4 = 16 cables on the last stretch.

Lesson 527.1 · Section 3 Pulling and Laying Cable

527.1.7 Footage marks and the cut

The footage marks printed on a cable’s jacket measure the cable as it comes off the box.

The cable on the mock-up carries a footage mark, a printed length in feet, at every foot of its jacket. The length of a piece of cable is the difference between the marks at its two ends. On some boxes the marks count up along the cable; on others they count down. The difference gives the length either way.

Cutting to length

  1. Draw the free end from the box and cut it square at a footage mark. Note that mark as the start mark.
  2. Work out the end mark: the start mark plus the cut length on a box that counts up, or minus the cut length on a box that counts down.
  3. Draw cable from the box until that mark appears, and cut square at it. Note it as the end mark.
  4. Fix the cable’s label within ~6 in of the end that will hang in the coil, flat and facing out.
  5. Wind the cable in loose loops to carry it. A kink, or cable dragged through a walkway, can damage the jacket and the pairs inside it.

Cutting both ends at a mark leaves no inches to add.

Example, from another job. A box counts down. The installer cuts the free end at mark 2210. The cut length is 137 ft, so the end mark is 2210 − 137 = 2073. The marks at the two ends of the piece read 2210 and 2073.

527.1.8 Pulling a concealed cable

A concealed cable runs inside the wall from its outlet box to the open top of the frame. The installer draws it down from the box end.

The cavity is the space inside a wall between its two faces. Cable in the cavity is out of sight once the wall is closed, so the installer checks it during the pull: nothing in the cavity may press on it or wear through its jacket.

The cable entry

Where a box has no entry toward the cavity, the work order states how to make one. Before drilling, shine a flashlight down the cavity from the top of the frame. A cable already lying behind the box is a reason to stop and tell the site lead. A hole drilled through a steel box leaves a sharp edge, so the installer fits a snap-in insulating bushing, a plastic ring that clips into the hole and keeps the cable off the edge. On the training walls, where no bushing is stocked, a split loom offcut squeezed into the hole serves as a liner.

Fishing the cable

A fish tape is a flat steel tape on a reel, pushed through a closed space to draw a cable back through it. Push it in through the bushing and up the cavity until its head comes out of the open top of the frame. Tape the cable end to the head, wrapped smooth so that it does not catch on the way down. Then draw the tape back down while you feed the cable over the top of the frame, so that the cable does not rub on the frame’s edge or catch on another cable.

Slack, take-up, and the frame top

  • Slack. Draw the cable through until the slack the owner asks for stands out of the box, and measure it.
  • Take-up. At the top, take up the cable until it hangs in the cavity without sag. Do not pull it tight: a tight cable bears on the bushing and draws the slack back into the box.
  • Top tie. Tie the cable to the frame’s top horizontal member with a hook-and-loop tie, snug but not pressing into the jacket.
  • Edge protection. Where the cable crosses the top of the aluminum frame, lay a split loom offcut over the edge with the cable inside it.
  • Coil. Wind the cable beyond the top of the frame in loops, tape the coil and its end, leave the label on the outside, and hang the coil on the bay’s J-hook. Both ends stay unterminated.

The screw-point check

On both faces, the screws that fix boxes, straps, and raceway bases to the panels pass through into the cavity. Owner’s standard 27 05 00 4.5 limits each screw’s length so that its point stays clear of cable in the cavity. A cable left sagging can still drift against a point. In the screw-point check, you shine a flashlight down the cavity along your cable, from the top tie to the box, and report either “none” or each place where a screw point touches the cable.

Lesson 527.1 · Section 3 Pulling and Laying Cable

527.1.9 On the cable ladder

On the cable ladder, each installer lays, ties, and checks their own cables, in the order the site lead posts.

Laying order

Teams lay their cables one at a time. The team whose wall is farthest from the rack lays first, so its cables are already in place when the next team’s cables join them. No team then lifts its cables over another team’s.

Laying

  • Unwind the bay’s coils together, starting at the J-hook, so that the cables do not twist around each other.
  • Walk beneath the cable ladder toward the rack, lifting the cables into it as you go. Keep them inside the ladder’s side rails, beside the cables already laid.
  • At each change of direction, lay the cables in a curve no tighter than the bend gauge.
  • Lay cable on the cable ladder only: never on another service, and never across a walkway at floor level.

Hook-and-loop ties

Addendum A4 allows hook-and-loop ties only, tightened by hand. No tie may deform a cable jacket. A tie pulled tight presses into the jacket and the pairs under it. Where 27 05 00 4.6 calls for cable ties in the cavity, the addendum states that A4 governs. Tie the bundle to the rungs at the spacing the site lead sets: no more than ~24 in apart, and within ~6 in of each side of each change of direction.

Figure — Ties at a change of direction
Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled Ties at a change of direction (plan view). A cable ladder runs from the wall on the left, turns a corner, and runs down toward the rack. A cable bundle follows it, curving through the turn: curve no tighter than the bend gauge.

Pulling tension

Addendum A4 limits the pulling tension on any cable to 25 pounds-force (lbf), or 110 newtons (N), the figure ANSI/TIA-568 gives. Pulling harder can stretch the solid copper conductors and loosen the pair twist. A tension gauge reads the pull on a cable, in lbf or in N. To check a laid cable, tape a small loop at its rack end, hook the gauge to the loop, and draw the cable steadily toward the rack for a short distance. The reading during the draw is the pulling tension. Where the reading is over the limit, stop pulling and report it to the site lead. A high reading can come from a tie pulled too tight or a snag at a change of direction.

527.1.10 Service loop, drop, and the release

At the rack end, the installer leaves each cable a service loop on the cable ladder and a drop for the patch panel. The cable stays unterminated until the owner’s representative releases it.

The team mark is a tape flag on the last rung of the cable ladder before the MIR, lettered for the block of patch panel ports the team’s cables serve. Each installer stores their cables’ spare length there.

  • Service loop. 10 ft of each cable, coiled on the cable ladder at the MIR end (27 15 00 3.1; addendum A5). Coil the loops of your cables together, lay them flat on the ladder at the team mark, and tie them to a rung. Note the footage mark of each cable where its loop begins and where it ends.
  • Drop. 6 ft of each cable beyond the service loop (27 05 00 4.8). Until termination, wind the drops in one loose loop and hang it from the last rung, off the floor.
  • The ends. Tape each end, and check that its label is legible within ~6 in of the end. The outlet end of each cable stays in its box or bracket with its slack, taped and labeled.

The release

When the cables are tied, looped, and dropped, the installer calls the owner’s representative. This is the hold point. The representative inspects each cable and names any fault. The installer corrects each named fault, the representative checks it again, and the representative then releases the cable. Terminating a cable fixes its length. A fault in its route or its loop, found after termination, means cutting off the termination and making it again.

527.1.11 The cable record

The cable record gives each cable’s length from its own footage marks, the route its bundle takes to the rack, and the result of the owner’s inspection.

The footage marks give each cable a length that does not depend on a tester. Read the mark nearest each end: at the outlet, the mark nearest the end that stands out of the box or bracket; at the rack, the mark nearest the end of the drop. The difference is the cable’s length, whichever way the marks count.

What a cable record holds for each cable and for the route.
EntryWhat it carries
Each cableThe mark nearest the outlet end, the mark nearest the rack end, and the length they give; the marks where the service loop begins and ends
RouteThe walls the cable ladder passes, in order, and the number of changes of direction
Pull checkThe cable checked, the reading in lbf, and whether it is within 25 lbf
InspectionThe time of the call, each fault named or “none”, each correction, and the release time of each cable

Example, from another job. On the library job, a cable’s outlet-end mark reads 2073 and its rack-end mark 2210: 2210 − 2073 = 137 ft. Its service loop begins at mark 2191 and ends at mark 2203, a loop of 12 ft, the library standard’s figure. The 7 ft from 2203 to 2210 is the drop.

Check each length against the owner’s limit of 295 ft (27 15 00 2.2), and each loop against the owner’s figure.

Lesson 527.1 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

527.1.12 What you covered

An installer accepts a bay, plans each cable, pulls and lays the cables within the owner’s limits, and records them. The owner’s representative releases the cables for termination.

Accepting the bay

A permanent link runs from the outlet jack to the patch panel port, both terminations included, and excludes patch cords. Record each acceptance check as accepted or not accepted, naming each missing or defective element and what is wrong with it, as found; do not repair it. Take each outlet’s port from the addendum’s table. The patch panel port label carries the outlet identifier, the faceplate the panel and port, and each cable end both.

Planning the pull

A cable’s cut length is the sum of six parts: the run on the wall, the rise, and the route, which you measure, and the slack, the drop, and the service loop, which the owner’s documents set. Round the total up to the next whole foot. The minimum bend radius is four times the outside diameter; the bend gauge measures eight times the outside diameter across. Estimate ties as the route in inches ÷ 24, rounded up, plus two for each change of direction. Check each support’s rated capacity against the cables it will carry at its fullest.
