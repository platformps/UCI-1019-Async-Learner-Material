Lesson 533.3 - NVR Commissioning and Recording

How an installer sets up a Network Video Recorder (NVR) so that its recordings carry the right time and the right camera name, how long its drive keeps them, and how a recording comes back out as a clip somebody else can play.

At 7:40 on a Monday morning, the security manager of a distribution warehouse asks for the video of a loading-dock door from 2:10 on Saturday morning. The recorder holds three weeks of video. The search finds nothing at 2:10, because the recorder’s clock was set by hand at installation and never moved for daylight saving time. Its recording of the door is stamped an hour off, on a channel named “Camera 7”. The clip that is finally exported opens only in the recorder maker’s own player, which the insurer’s investigator does not have.

Each of those faults dates from the day the recorder was commissioned. On project HSOC-26-014, Kestrel Communications is bringing the cameras on the Level 2 installation mock-up onto their recorders. The owner, Calder Regional Services, sets the terms in its Security Addendum: every channel named for its camera’s location, the recorder’s time taken from a named time source, a retention estimate for each recorder, and a settings backup or a manual recovery record at hand-off.

Lesson 533.3 · Section 1 The Recorder, Its Drive, and Its Clock

533.3.1 What a network video recorder does

Lesson 533.3

NVR Commissioning and Recording

A camera that shows a live picture is not yet a surveillance system. The installer fits the recorder’s drive, sets its clock from the site’s time source, names each channel for its camera’s location, and applies the owner’s recording schedule. The installer then proves that a recording can be found and exported, states how many days of video the drive keeps, and leaves the settings in a form the next technician can rebuild from.

5 sections  ·  14 topics  ·  13 activities

By the end of Lesson 533.3 you will be able to
  • Confirm that the Network Video Recorder (NVR) detects its recording drive.
  • Set the NVR’s date, time and time zone.
  • Confirm a camera’s identity, firmware version and NVR port.
  • Name a camera channel to its installed location.
  • Apply a recording schedule to a camera channel.
  • Estimate recording retention from bitrate, schedule and drive capacity.
  • Retrieve a recorded event by channel and time.
  • Export a playable video clip.
  • Save a settings backup or a manual recovery record.

An NVR receives each camera’s video over the network, writes it to a drive, and gives it back on request.

On a small or mid-sized site, one recorder does all of this.

  • It powers the cameras, where it has Power over Ethernet (PoE) ports. Each camera then needs one cable, to its port on the recorder.
  • It receives each camera’s video stream. The recorder keeps one channel for each camera: the camera’s name, its recording schedule, and its recordings.
  • It writes the video to its drive, on each channel’s schedule, stamped with the recorder’s own date and time.
  • It shows live and recorded video, on a monitor connected to it or in a web browser on a computer that can reach it over the network.
  • It gives recordings back: it searches them by channel and time, plays them, and exports a clip to a file.

A recorder without a drive shows live video and keeps none of it. The camera’s picture on the screen proves the camera, its cable, and its port. The recording proves the drive, the schedule, and the clock.

Where the settings are made

Most recorders accept the same settings from a monitor and mouse connected to the recorder and from a web browser on the network. The two screens are organized alike but are not always identical: a maker may offer a function on one and not on the other. On the mock-up, the site lead posts which screen each step uses and the menu names on it.

533.3.2 The recording drive

The drive is where every recording lives. The recorder has to detect the drive. The drive has to be initialized before the recorder writes to it.

What kind of drive

A recorder writes to its drive around the clock, from several cameras at once. Drive makers sell lines rated for that load, labeled for surveillance or video recording. A drive made for a desktop computer is rated for fewer hours of use a day. The recorder maker’s documentation states the drive types, sizes, and number of bays the recorder takes. The recorders on the mock-up take one drive each.

Fitting it

The recorder’s quick start guide sets out the steps. Disconnect the recorder’s power before the cover comes off, and reconnect it only with the cover back on. Photograph the drive’s label before it goes in: the model and capacity on the label are the record of what was fitted. They cannot be read once the cover is closed.

Detected, and initialized

When the recorder starts, its disk page lists each drive it detects, with a status and a capacity. A drive missing from the list is not connected, not seated, or not working. Check the data and power cables inside, with the power disconnected, before anything else.

A new drive is usually listed as unformatted or uninitialized. Initializing the drive, which some recorders call formatting, prepares it for the recorder’s own file layout. Initializing erases everything on the drive. On a new drive nothing is lost. On a drive taken from another recorder, the erased recordings may be the only copy of an incident. The site lead authorizes each initialization, at the disk page, after confirming which recorder and which drive it is.

Once initialized, the drive shows a status such as normal. The recorder then begins writing to it. Record the status and capacity as the disk page shows them, with the unit the recorder uses.

When the drive is full

By default, most recorders overwrite the oldest recording once the drive is full. The drive then always holds a moving window of the most recent days. A recorder set to stop when full keeps the oldest video and records nothing new.

533.3.3 Date, time, and time zone

The recorder stamps every frame with its own clock. A search by time, and any account of what happened when, is only as good as that clock.

Video becomes evidence when somebody can say where and when it was recorded. The “when” is the timestamp the recorder wrote. The settings below make that timestamp right.

  • The time zone of the site, and its daylight saving rule, so the recorder’s clock reads local time through the year. Some recorders apply daylight saving with the zone; others have a separate daylight saving setting.
  • The time source. A recorder can take its time from a server using the Network Time Protocol (NTP). It then corrects its clock against that server instead of drifting. The site names the server; the recorder has to be able to reach it from the network it sits on.
  • The date and time, set by hand only where no time source can be reached. A clock set by hand drifts, and its error grows until somebody corrects it.

Why the clock matters beyond one camera

An incident is often followed across several cameras: a car enters the gate, then crosses the yard, then stops at the dock. Where two recorders keep different times, the sequence cannot be read across them. A report that cites those times becomes open to challenge. Recorders that take their time from the same source agree with each other.

Checking it

After you save the settings, compare the recorder’s displayed time with a reference clock the site names, in one screenshot. Record both times and the difference in seconds. A difference of a few seconds is the time the screens took to refresh; a difference of an hour is a time zone or daylight saving error.

The owner’s Security Addendum, clause E4, requires the recorder to keep its time from the posted time source, in the site’s time zone.

533.3.4 Confirming the camera on its port

Before a channel gets a name, confirm which camera is on it. A cable crossed at the recorder puts the right name on the wrong picture.

A recorder lists the cameras it has added, by port or channel. Some recorders add a camera as soon as it is connected to a PoE port. Others need a device search and an add step, as the maker’s guide describes. Either way, the recorder’s camera pages usually give these facts about each camera.

  • The model, as the camera reports it.
  • The firmware version: the software inside the camera. The hand-off record carries it, so the next technician knows what the camera runs before updating it or reporting a fault.
  • The Media Access Control (MAC) address: the hardware address set in the camera at manufacture, usually printed on the camera’s own label.

The port number says where the cable lands. The MAC address says which camera is on the other end. Compare the MAC address the recorder shows with the one on the label of the camera at the location the drawing gives for that port. A match ties the channel to the camera on the wall. A mismatch means the cables are crossed or the labels are wrong. Naming the channel before that is resolved puts the error into the record.

Where the recorder shows no firmware version or no MAC address, record “not shown” and the pages you looked at. Do not copy a value from another camera of the same model.

Lesson 533.3 · Section 2 Cameras, Channels, and Recording Settings

533.3.5 Naming the channel for its location

A channel name tells the person searching the recordings where the camera looks. It names the location, not the camera.

A recorder gives each channel a default name, such as a number or the camera’s model. Neither tells a security officer which door or corridor the channel covers. The owner’s standard sets the name: on most sites, the camera’s location identifier, the same identifier printed on its cable label and shown on the camera schedule. On a parking structure, for example, the third camera on the level 2 ramp might be P2-RAMP-C3 on the drawing, on both ends of its cable, and on its channel.

The identifier belongs to the location. When a failed camera is replaced with another model on the same cable, the channel keeps its name; the hand-off record takes the new camera’s model, firmware version, and MAC address.

Enter the name character for character, as the label shows it, with its hyphens and capitals. A search screen, a report, and an exported file name all carry it.

The owner’s Security Addendum, clause E4, requires each channel to carry its camera’s identifier.

533.3.6 Recording schedules

A schedule sets, for each channel, the hours of each day the recorder writes, and what makes it write.

A schedule is a grid of days and hours for one channel. Each block of the grid takes a recording type.

Recording types a schedule commonly assigns, and what each one keeps.
TypeThe recorder writesWhat it keeps
ContinuousAll the time in the blockEverything, at the highest use of the drive
EventOnly when an event is detected, such as motion in the pictureThe detected events; nothing between them
NoneNothingLive video only

Many recorders record each channel continuously, every day, until a schedule changes it. Where the owner’s schedule is continuous recording, open the channel’s schedule, check each day, and save it: the saved schedule is the evidence that you checked it. On a playback timeline, many recorders draw continuous and event recordings in different colors.

Apply the schedule to the channel the work order names. Most recorders offer to copy a schedule to other channels. Copy it only where the work order covers those channels too.

The owner’s Security Addendum, clause E4, sets the schedule: as posted on the Security Plan Sheet, or continuous, 24 hours a day, seven days a week where none is posted.

Lesson 533.3 · Section 2 Cameras, Channels, and Recording Settings

533.3.7 Resolution, codec, and bitrate

The stream settings decide how much video data a channel sends. The recorder writes what it receives, so they decide how fast the drive fills.

A camera usually sends two streams. The main stream, at the camera’s full resolution, is the one most recorders write to the drive. A smaller sub stream serves views where many cameras share one screen. The settings below are those of the main stream.

  • Resolution: the width and height of each frame, in pixels, such as 1920 × 1080. More pixels show more detail and need more data.
  • Codec: the method that compresses the video, such as H.264 or H.265. H.265 typically needs a lower bitrate than H.264 for a similar picture. A computer that plays the exported file has to support the same codec.
  • Bitrate: the amount of data the stream sends each second, shown in kilobits per second (kbit/s) or megabits per second (Mbit/s). 1 Mbit/s is 1,000 kbit/s.
  • Bitrate type: a Constant Bitrate (CBR) stream sends close to the set figure all the time. A Variable Bitrate (VBR) stream sends less when the scene is still and more when it moves; on most cameras the set figure is its ceiling.

Read these settings on the channel’s video or encoding page, and record them as the recorder shows them. They belong to the camera. On a commissioning job, you read and record them without changing them: a change alters the picture the owner approved and the retention the drive gives.

Match each stream setting to what it sets.

Lesson 533.3 · Section 3 Retention

533.3.8 Estimating retention

Retention is how far back the recordings reach once the drive is full. It is estimated from the bitrate, the hours recorded each day, and the drive’s capacity.

A recorder that overwrites the oldest recording when the drive is full keeps a moving window of days. The owner wants to know how wide that window is, because an incident reported after it has passed can no longer be retrieved.

A bracket under the whole bar reads Retention: about 21.7 days, and under it: 6,000 GB divided by 276.5 GB written a day, all channels together.

The method

  1. Bitrate in Mbit/s. Divide a figure in kbit/s by 1,000.
  2. Gigabytes (GB) a camera a day. Multiply the bitrate in Mbit/s by the seconds recorded in a day (3,600 × the hours), divide by 8 to turn bits into bytes, and divide by 1,000 to turn megabytes (MB) into GB.
  3. GB a day for the recorder. Add the figures of every channel. Where every channel has the same bitrate and schedule, multiply one channel’s figure by the number of channels.
  4. Retention in days. Divide the capacity on the drive’s label, in GB, by the GB a day for the recorder.

For a channel recording 24 hours a day, step 2 comes to the bitrate in Mbit/s × 10.8.

A recorder on another job (hypothetical): a 6 terabyte (TB) drive, ten cameras each at 2,560 kbit/s, continuous recording 24 hours a day.
LineResult
Capacity: 6 TB × 1,0006,000 GB
Bitrate: 2,560 ÷ 1,0002.56 Mbit/s
GB a camera a day: 2.56 × 3,600 × 24 ÷ 8 ÷ 1,00027.6 GB

Lesson 533.3 · Section 3 Retention

533.3.9 Units, and what the estimate leaves out

The figure is an estimate of what the settings allow. The owner’s Security Addendum, clause E5, asks for it by that name: an estimate, not an observed retention.

Two ways of counting a gigabyte

Drive makers count 1 GB as 1,000 MB and 1 TB as 1,000 GB. The method above counts the same way. Some recorders and computers count in powers of two, where 1 TB is 1,099,511,627,776 bytes. A drive labeled 8 TB then shows as about 7.3 TB. Work the estimate on the capacity printed on the drive’s label, in decimal GB. Record the figure the recorder displays beside it, with its unit. A figure in binary units would shorten only an estimate worked from it; the drive holds the same video whatever unit the recorder’s screen shows. A recorder also reserves part of a drive when it initializes it, so the space left for video is slightly less than either figure.

What moves the real figure

  • Variable bitrate. A VBR stream sends less than its set figure when the scene is still, so over a quiet scene the drive can keep recordings for longer. Where the set figure is the stream’s ceiling, an estimate at the set figure gives the shortest retention. An audio track, or a camera’s smart codec, also changes the real figure.
  • Event recording. A channel that records on events writes only when something happens. Its hours a day are an assumption about activity, so state the assumption beside the estimate.
  • Mixed channels. Channels with different bitrates or schedules each get their own line; the recorder’s figure is their sum.
  • Changed settings. A higher resolution, a different codec, or a new camera changes the figure. The estimate holds only for the settings it was worked from, so record them with it.

The observed retention is read later from the recorder: the date of the oldest recording still on a full drive. Where it differs from the estimate, the settings above usually explain why.

533.3.10 Searching and playing back by channel and time

A request for video names a place and a time. The search uses the same two keys: the channel, and a window of time.

Every retrieval starts the same way.

  1. Choose the channel. The channel name is the location identifier, so the location in the request gives the channel.
  2. Set the date and a window of time. A reported time is rarely exact. Start a few minutes before it and end a few minutes after it, then widen the window if the search finds nothing.
  3. Run the search, and read the result. The recorder lists the recordings in the window, or draws them on a timeline. A window with no recording in it points to the schedule, the drive, or the camera, not the search.
  4. Play from the start of the window and pause on the moment requested. The frame carries the recorder’s timestamp. Record it beside the time in the request, and state any difference.

A search on the wrong channel, or a window around the wrong time, returns recordings that look like an answer and are not. Confirm the channel name and the recorder’s time before you report that nothing was recorded.

533.3.11 Exporting a clip that plays

An export copies part of a recording into a file that leaves the recorder. The file has to open on the computer of whoever receives it.

Making the export

  • Range. Export from a little before the event to a little after it, so the clip shows what led up to it and what followed.
  • Destination. Recorders export to a USB flash drive in one of their ports; many also let the web interface download a clip to the computer running the browser. A recorder may need the flash drive in a particular format: the recorders on the mock-up use FAT32.
  • File type. Some recorders write a format of their own that opens only in the maker’s player. Where the recorder offers a standard format such as MP4, choose it.

Proving that it plays

Open the file on a computer that does not have the recorder maker’s software, in its ordinary video player, and watch the event in it. A clip recorded in H.265 needs a player or computer that supports H.265; on Windows 11, the built-in player needs the HEVC Video Extensions. Record the file name, its type, its size, the length of the clip, and whether it played, or what the player showed if it did not.

Who receives it

A clip of an incident can become evidence. The owner decides who receives a copy. The work order or the site lead names that person. Hand the file to the person named, keep the original export unaltered, and record when and to whom it went.

533.3.12 Settings backup, or a manual recovery record

A recorder that fails, or is reset, loses its settings. A backup, or a written record of the settings, lets the next technician rebuild the settings.

The settings can be kept in either of these forms.

  • A settings backup. Many recorders export their configuration to a file, on a USB flash drive or through the web interface, and import it into the same model later. Record the file’s name and date, and store it where the owner keeps equipment records.
  • A manual recovery record. Where the recorder offers no export, write the settings down from the screens.
    • For the recorder: its identifier and Internet Protocol (IP) address, its time zone, and its time source.
    • For each channel: its number or port, its name, the camera’s model and firmware version, the stream settings, and the recording schedule.

Neither form carries a password, a passphrase, or an account name. The owner holds the accounts and issues access to whoever needs it. The owner’s Security Addendum, clause E9, keeps credentials out of every record, photograph, screenshot, and prompt to an Artificial Intelligence (AI) tool. Clause E10 asks for a settings backup or a manual recovery record for each device at hand-off.

533.3.13 The camera network and remote viewing

On many sites, cameras and recorders sit on a network of their own, behind a firewall. The firewall decides what can open a connection to the recorder, and what the recorder can reach.

The camera network is a zone of its own, with a firewall between it and the office network. The network team writes the firewall’s rules. A common set, and the one the network team set on the mock-up, reads like this.

  • The office network may start a session into the camera network: a laptop opens the recorder’s web page.
  • The camera network may not start a session into the office network. The firewall lets the recorder’s replies through, and nothing the recorder starts.

Under these rules, video stays on the camera network unless somebody on the office network asks for it. A camera or recorder cannot open a connection to an office computer. The rules also explain what an installer meets on site. A laptop on the office network reaches the recorder’s web interface. A recorder cannot reach a time server, a mail server, or anything else on the office network unless the network team adds a rule, so the site names a time source the camera network can reach.

Remote viewing

Owners often ask to watch their cameras from outside the site. Recorders offer several routes: a phone app that reaches the recorder through the maker’s cloud service, a port opened through the site’s firewall, or a connection through the owner’s own remote-access system. Each route opens a way into the camera network from outside. The owner and the network team decide whether any route is allowed, and which. An installer sets up only a method the owner has approved, and never enables a cloud or remote feature on a recorder without that approval. Some recorders offer the maker’s cloud service during first start-up: leave it off unless the owner has approved it, and check its state at hand-off. Whether the camera network can reach the internet, as a phone-app route needs, is a separate firewall rule, written by the network team.

533.3.14 What you covered

Fit and check the drive, set the clock from the site’s time source, confirm and name each channel, apply the schedule, work out how many days of video the drive keeps, and prove that a recording comes back out as a clip that plays.

The recorder, its drive, and its clock

A recorder powers its cameras where it has PoE ports, writes each channel to its drive, and gives recordings back by channel and time. Without a drive it shows live video and keeps none. Initializing a drive erases it, so the site lead authorizes each initialization. The recorder takes its time from the site’s named time source, in the site’s time zone. A screenshot against a reference clock proves the setting.

Cameras, channels, and recording settings

The MAC address the recorder shows, matched to the camera’s label, confirms which camera is on a port. The channel takes the location identifier, character for character, and keeps it when the camera is replaced. A schedule sets continuous, event, or no recording for each block of each day. Resolution, codec, bitrate, and bitrate type set how much data the main stream sends; the installer reads and records them without changing them.
