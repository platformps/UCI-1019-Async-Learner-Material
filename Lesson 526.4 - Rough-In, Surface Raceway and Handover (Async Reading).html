Rough-in is the stage at which cable and its supports go into a building before the walls are finished, the devices are fitted, or any cable is terminated.

The rough-in installer leaves every outlet cable in a known state for the crew that terminates it: run to its pathway, cut to the length the termination needs, labeled at both ends, and protected wherever it bends or crosses an edge. The installer replaces a cable left short or damaged with a new pull. An unlabeled cable has to be traced before it can be matched to its outlet at the rack.

On the Harlow Street mock-up, each bay holds four elements, each with a code. C1 is the camera box. D1, the flush data outlet, is a low-voltage bracket in an opening cut in the panel. D2, the surface data outlet, is a plastic box on the panel’s face. D3, the second data outlet, is a box left empty at this stage. Two network cables serve the bay. The cable to D1 runs concealed inside the wall, and the cable to D2 runs in raceway on the panel’s face.

Both cables leave the wall at the open top of its frame. They wait there, coiled and labeled, for the crew that carries them along the cable ladder, the open rack of rungs above the walls, to the mobile infrastructure rack (MIR) and terminates them. The MIR holds the patch panels where the cables are terminated.

526.4.2 Cutting a cable to length

A rough-in cable is cut from the cable carton, the 1,000 ft box the cable is supplied in, before it goes into the wall, so its length is calculated from the route it will take.

The length is the sum of six parts. On the mock-up, each face of a wall carries a panel field: the area the panels cover, whose top is the top edge of the upper panels. The run on the wall ends at the top of the panel field, and the rise starts there. Positions on the mock-up are given as x and y in inches: x from the left edge of the bay, y from the bottom of the panel field.

Round the length you cut up to the next whole foot: a cable a few inches too long is usable; a cable a few inches short is not. An outlet cable is never spliced, so the installer replaces a short cable with a new length from the carton.

The site lead measures a 14 in rise from the top of the wall to the J-hook pathway and a 58 ft route along that pathway to the room. Cutting by the footage marks

Cable is cut at a pull point, the place where the cable cartons stand.

  1. Draw the free end from the carton and cut it square at a footage mark. Note that mark as the start mark.
  2. Draw cable from the carton, counting the marks, until the difference from the start mark equals your rounded length.
  3. Cut at that mark and note it as the end mark.
  4. Before you leave the pull point, fix one label within ~6 in of one end, flat and facing out. On a concealed run, that end stays at the top as the coil end. On a surface drop, label the first end you cut: it goes into the outlet box.

At the clinic, the footage marks on the cable in use count down. The installer cuts the free end at the 1,240 ft mark and the other end at 1,240 − 91 = 1,149 ft. The two marks are the evidence of the length installed: an inspector reads them at the two ends of the cable without uncoiling it.

526.4.3 Drawing a cable through a closed wall

The fish tape goes in at the outlet opening and comes out at the open top of the wall. You then draw the cable back down behind it.

A tape pushed up from the outlet opening can come out anywhere along the open top and still be reached. A cable dropped from the top has to find an opening a few inches across.

The method

  1. Push the fish tape in through the outlet opening and straight up the cavity until its head comes out at the top of the frame.
  2. Fasten the cable to the head. Use the end without a label. The end labeled at the pull point stays at the top as the coil end. Tape the cable end to the head of the fish tape, wrapping the adhesive tape smooth so that the joint cannot catch on a frame member or on another cable.
  3. Draw the fish tape back through the opening, feeding the cable in at the top as it goes. Keep the cable off the frame’s edge where it passes over.
  4. Free the end. Draw the cable end out through the opening and take the tape off it.

If the tape or the cable stops, it has met something: a frame member, another cable, the back of a box. Draw it back a few inches and try again on a slightly different line. Pulling harder against an obstruction can scrape the jacket or drag another bay’s cable with it.

Dressing the cable in the cavity

Once the end is out of the opening, take up the cable from the top until it has no sag between the outlet and the top of the frame. Leave a gentle curve behind the outlet bracket instead of pulling the cable tight against it. Keep the slack at the outlet: do not draw it back through the opening. Then tie it to a frame member with a hook-and-loop tie. Owner’s standard 27 05 00 4.6 requires cable in the cavity to be bundled by route and tied to the frame’s horizontal members. Clause 4.5 allows no fastener to be driven into the frame; a tie around a frame member is not a fastener.

The cavity holds the work of more than one bay. Cables, brackets, and box backs from the other face sit within inches of your cable. Do not touch, move, or pull a cable that serves another outlet. If you disturb one, report it.

Screw points

The boxes and brackets on both faces are screwed into the panels. Each screw point ends in the cavity. Owner’s standard 27 05 00 4.5 limits screw length so that the point stays clear of cable in the cavity. A slack cable can bow across to a point. A point resting on a jacket can cut through it when the cable is drawn again, or when a panel is taken off. Taking up the sag holds the cable on its line. A flashlight check shows whether any point touches it: shine the light down the cavity along the cable, from the tie to the outlet, and note every place where a screw point touches the jacket. The remedy for a point that touches is a shorter screw. The cable installer reports the contact and where it is; the trade that fixed the screw replaces it.

526.4.4 Protecting entries, edges, and the jacket

A network cable is damaged where it bends too tightly, where it bears on an edge, and where something presses into its jacket.

A four-pair cable carries its signal on pairs twisted at set rates and held at set spacing inside the jacket. Its transmission performance depends on that geometry. Damage that leaves the copper intact can still change that geometry.

Bend radius

ANSI/TIA-568 sets the minimum inside bend radius of an installed four-pair Unshielded Twisted-Pair (U/UTP) cable at four times the cable’s outside diameter. The diameter is on the cable maker’s data sheet. For a cable with a 0.30 in outside diameter, the radius is 4 × 0.30 = 1.2 in, so the tightest bend allowed is a curve about 2.4 in across. A concealed run bends in two places: where it turns out of the outlet opening, and where it turns over the top of the frame.

A kink is a fold sharp enough to crease the jacket. It is damage even after the cable is straightened.

Pulling tension

ANSI/TIA-568 limits the pulling tension on a four-pair cable to 25 lbf (110 N). Pulling a cable through a snag can exceed that limit. A cable stretched that way does not recover the original twist of its pairs. Free a snag where it happens.

Edges

A cable that bears on an edge can wear through when it moves. On a concealed run, keep the cable off two edges: the cut edge of the panel around the outlet opening, and the top edge of the frame where the cable crosses it. At the outlet, the cable passes through the middle of the bracket opening without touching the cut panel edge. At the top, fit a short length of split loom, a flexible tube slit along its length, around the cable where it crosses the frame. In a building, a bushing or grommet does the same job where a cable passes through a metal opening.

Ties

A tie pulled tight enough to press into the jacket deforms the pairs under it. Pull a hook-and-loop tie snug and no tighter.

Match each source of damage to the practice that prevents it.

526.4.5 Slack and identification at both ends

The outlet end of a rough-in cable has working slack. Both ends carry the same machine-printed identifier.

Slack at the outlet

The slack is measured from the outlet opening, or from the box entry, to the cable end. Slack that is too short cannot be drawn out far enough to strip and terminate. Slack that is too long has to be folded into the box behind the jack. The owner’s standard sets the length: on the mock-up, clause 27 05 00 4.7. Tape the end so that the conductors stay clean and the jacket cannot slide back.

The coil at the far end

The far end waits as a coil: even loops, taped at several points, its end taped and unterminated. The coil stays where the work order puts it until the run is inspected. Sheet TW-301, detail 6, of the drawing set shows the cable end at the ladder.

Identifiers

Owner’s standard 27 05 53 2.1 requires a machine-printed label at both ends of every cable. On the mock-up, clause 2.2 sets the identifier: the bay followed by the element the cable serves. W5-1R-D2, for example, identifies the cable to the surface data outlet in the right bay of Face 1 of wall W5. The terminating crew uses the identifier to find the right coil at the ladder and the right port at the rack. A label at one end only leaves the other end unidentified.

Place each label so that it can be read without handling the cable:

  • within ~6 in of its end, as the work orders require;
  • flat, wrapped so that the identifier faces out;
  • clear of the tape that seals the end;
  • on the outside of the coil, at the coil end.

526.4.6 Cable on the face of a wall

Surface raceway carries cable on the face of a finished wall, where the wall cannot be opened or a concealed run cannot reach the outlet.

A block wall or a concrete wall has no cavity. A finished wall in an occupied building may have a cavity that the owner will not allow to be opened. In either case the outlet becomes a surface box, fed by raceway fixed to the wall.

Surface raceway is a channel in two parts that snap together over the cable, with fittings and boxes made to match it. On the Harlow Street project it is non-metallic: owner’s standard 27 05 00 4.4 names plastic surface raceway for the mock-up’s surface drop.

Open each card.

Fill

The maker publishes a fill table for each raceway size: the number of cables of a given diameter it holds. Beyond that number, the cables press against the cover, which may then spring off.

526.4.7 Cutting the raceway and planning its screws

The base and the cover are cut to the length of the run, the base is set plumb on its line, and its screws go at positions planned before the drill comes out.

Cut length

The raceway cut length is the distance from the top of the box to the end of the run, plus the entry allowance. The cover is cut to the same length as the base. Cut both at the bench, square, against a stop, with the part clamped. Then deburr the cut ends. A burr catches the cover as it snaps on and can nick a cable laid over it.

The factory end of each part, the end you did not cut, goes into the box, where it seats cleanly in the entry. The cut end goes to the open end of the run, under the end fitting.

Line and plumb

Seat the base in the box, then set it plumb with a level held along it. Check the line where the run ends, against the position on the drawing. A base that leans from the box drifts off its line as it rises.

The screw rule

On the mock-up, the base is fixed by screws on its center line that meet three conditions. Clause 27 05 00 4.8 of the owner’s standard sets the spacing and clause 4.5 the clearance; the work order adds the 4 in end limit.

  • One screw within 4 in of each end of the base. At the bottom, measure from the top of the box, where the base leaves it. An unfixed end lifts and lets the cover spring off.
  • No more than 16 in between neighboring screws. A longer span bows away from the panel.
  • Every screw 2 in or more from a panel edge or a panel screw: one of the four screws, one near each corner, that hold a panel to the frame. A screw near a panel edge can break out of the panel. A screw near a panel screw can strike that screw or the frame behind it. Owner’s standard 27 05 00 4.5 allows no fastener in the frame.

Work the plan before you drill. List the bands where no screw can go, place a screw near each end outside them, then fill the gaps so that no span exceeds 16 in. Drill a pilot hole at each position with the bit named on the raceway product card. That card gives the raceway series, its pilot bit, its screws, and its entry allowance. Drive each screw on a low clutch setting. An over-driven screw can crack a plastic base or strip its hole in a hardboard panel.

A worked screw plan

The example wall is not the mock-up. Its panel field is 96 in high, made of two panels that meet at y 48. Its panel screws lie off the raceway line. The box top is at y 14, and the raceway product card gives an entry allowance of 1/2 in.

526.4.8 Laying the cable and closing the raceway

The cable goes into the box first, then into the base, and the cover goes on only when the cable lies flat and clear of the lips the cover snaps onto.

The cable and its length

A surface drop’s cable is cut by the same six-part method. The run on the wall becomes the raceway run, from the top of the box to the top of the wall (on the mock-up, the panel field), and the slack lies in the box.

Closing the raceway

  1. Slack into the box. Pass the labeled end into the box through its top entry until the slack the owner’s standard requires lies between the entry and the end. Tape the end clear of the label, and fold the slack into the box.
  2. Lay the cable flat in the base from the box to the top, in the middle of the channel, clear of the lips at both edges.
  3. Photograph it. Once the cover is on, a photograph is the only record of how the cable lies in the base.
  4. Snap the cover on from the bottom up, pressing it home along its full length. Check that both ends of the cover sit flush with the ends of the base.
  5. Fit the end fitting at the top, with the cable passing out through it.

Above the raceway, the cable is finished as a concealed cable is: edge protection where it crosses the top of the frame, then a coil, taped, labeled, and left unterminated.

A cable on the lip is a crushed cable

The cover snaps onto the base along both edges. A cable lying across a lip when the cover snaps on is pinched where it crosses the lip. The cover hides the damage until the cable fails a test.

Lesson 526.4 · Section 3 The Camera Mounting Plate

526.4.9 Drilling a plate from a manufacturer’s template

A camera maker supplies a full-size paper template of the camera’s mounting base. The holes are drilled where the template marks them.

On the mock-up, the camera box is a 4 in round weatherproof box at the end of the camera’s pathway. The camera mounting plate is a 4 in round weatherproof blank cover for that box, drilled to the camera maker’s template and fixed to the box with the cover’s own screws. Sheet TW-401, note 5, of the drawing set calls for the plate on the camera box, drilled from the manufacturer’s template, and leaves the camera to a later stage.

The template marks the camera’s screw holes, a cable hole where the camera needs one, and an orientation mark, which fixes which way the camera faces. Most templates also carry a printed dimension or a scale bar for checking the print.

Check the print before you drill

A template is only correct at full size. A printer set to fit the page shrinks it, and every hole moves toward the center. The person who prints the template prints it at 100% and measures its printed dimension or scale bar with a tape measure. On the mock-up, the instructor prints and checks each copy. A print that does not measure true is not used.

The blank-cover card at the drilling place gives two sizes. The first is the drill size for the screw holes: a clearance size, which lets the camera’s screws pass through without threading into the holes. The second is the step-bit size for the cable hole, or “no cable hole”.

Text description of this diagram

A note reads Example only. The camera maker’s template governs.

Drilling the plate

Wear eye and hearing protection while you drill.

  1. Note the camera box’s cover-screw holes. Before drilling, look at the camera box and note where its cover-screw holes sit. The cover fits its box only with its screw holes over those holes.
  2. Hold the work. Turn the cover front face up, with its screw holes at the same positions as the box’s cover-screw holes. Keep it turned that way and screw it to a scrap backer board through its screw holes, so that it cannot spin on the bit.
  3. Place the template. Tape it centered on the front face, with its orientation mark at 12 o’clock, the point of the rim farthest from you.
  4. Center-punch each hole. The dimple keeps the bit from wandering on the smooth metal face.
  5. Drill the screw holes at the size on the blank-cover card.
  6. Cut the cable hole, where the template marks one, with a step bit: a cone-shaped bit that cuts a round hole in thin sheet metal one diameter step at a time. Stop at the step for the size on the blank-cover card.
  7. Write TOP. Before the template comes off, write TOP with a fine marker on the front face at the edge where the orientation mark sits.
  8. Free the plate. Take the template off and unscrew the cover from the backer board.
  9. Deburr every hole on both faces. A burr on the back keeps the plate from sitting flat on the box. A burr in the cable hole can cut the camera cable that passes through it.

Fixing the plate

Hold the plate on the box with TOP up, line its screw holes up with the box’s cover-screw holes, and drive the cover screws until the plate sits flat with no gap. Owner’s standard 27 05 53 2.2 puts the camera box’s label on the box itself. The plate carries no second label.

526.4.10 Inspecting the alignment and fixing of a mount

An inspector checks a mount on two counts: whether it sits where and how the drawing puts it, and whether it is held so that it stays there.

Alignment

  • Its position against the drawing, on both axes, within the tolerance the work order sets.
  • Its orientation: the plate’s TOP at the top.
  • Level, where the work order asks for it.

Fixing

  • Every screw present.
  • Every screw tight, with none stripped or spinning.
  • The plate flat on the box, with no gap and no rock when pressed.
  • The box itself firm on the panel.

A mount can pass one count and fail the other. A plate level and on its position with one screw missing is aligned and not fixed. A plate fixed tight and flat with TOP at the bottom is fixed and not aligned: the camera mounted on it will sit half a turn from its intended position.

Measuring a position

Positions on the drawing set are measured to the center of a box or bracket, in the x and y set out under “Cutting a cable to length”. A tape measure reaches an edge, not a center. Measure to both edges on the axis you are reading, the left and right edges for x and the bottom and top edges for y, and take the point halfway between them. For a 4 in square box whose left edge measures x 3 7/8 and right edge x 7 7/8, the center is at x 5 7/8. The same method finds the center of a bracket or a surface box, and the center line of a tube or a raceway. Read each edge to the nearest 1/8 in.

Deviation and tolerance

The deviation on each axis is the measured position minus the drawing’s position, with its sign: plus is right or up, minus is left or down. A tolerance of 3/8 in accepts any deviation from −3/8 to +3/8 in. A position with a deviation over the tolerance on either axis is a position outside the tolerance. Report it.

526.4.11 As built, open items, and the handoff note

An as-built record gives each installed element’s measured position beside its position on the drawing.

Each position is entered as measured, with its deviation and its sign. A position outside the tolerance is reported as it stands, not rounded onto the drawing.

The handover hold point

Owner’s standard 27 05 00 4.3 makes the completed bay a hold point. At handover the installer presents the bay to the owner’s representative. The presentation is not an inspection. After presenting, the installer makes no change to the bay. The representative then inspects the bay against the acceptance checks the work order lists. The representative records each change named at the presentation. The representative releases a bay when every check is met, or when each check not met has been corrected or entered as an open item.

Before presenting, the installer makes the same checks as a self-inspection, marking each one met, not met, or not applicable. “Not applicable” belongs only on a check that names bays other than yours. An acceptance check not met and not corrected is an open item. At the presentation, the installer names each position outside the tolerance and each open item.

The handoff note

The handoff note tells the next trades what they find at each item of the work area: what is installed, what is labeled, and what is left for them. 526.4.12 A daily report drafted with an AI assistant

A daily report is the contractor’s dated record of one day’s work on site. Every statement in it comes from what the writer saw and noted that day.

The site lead, the project manager, and the owner read daily reports. When a dispute arises over a delay, a quantity, or an inspection, the parties read the reports again as evidence. Kestrel’s daily report carries eight headings, set in the work order: date; project and location; work completed; materials used; inspections and hold points; problems and delays; safety; and work planned for the next day.

Field notes come first

The report is written from field notes: short entries made during the day, as things happen, with times, quantities, results, and problems. Notes written from memory at the end of the day often lose the times and the figures.

What an AI assistant does with the notes

An AI assistant such as Microsoft Copilot turns notes into sentences under the headings it is given. It writes the likeliest text for each heading. Likely text is not always text the notes support. Check its draft for four faults:

  • A fact added that is not in the notes: a quantity, a time, a test result, an inspection passed.
  • A heading filled with a general statement where the notes say nothing, such as “No safety incidents occurred.”
  • A fact dropped, often a problem or a delay.
  • A meaning changed: an approximate figure stated as exact, a bay held stated as released.

The method

  1. In one prompt, give the assistant your field notes and the headings, and ask for a daily report under those headings.
  2. Keep your prompt as you sent it, and the assistant’s whole draft as it was produced.
  3. Read the draft one sentence at a time against your notes. Remove every fact the notes do not support. Add every fact from the notes that the draft left out.
  4. Record each change you made and the reason for it.
