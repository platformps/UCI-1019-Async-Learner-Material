A work order tells an installer what to do, where, under which rules, and at what point to stop and wait for approval.

On a Kestrel job, a work order is the site lead’s written instruction for one piece of work. Its number goes on every record made under it.

The parts of a Kestrel work order and what each one tells the installer.
PartWhat it gives you
TaskThe job in one or two sentences
ScopeA numbered list of what you do. Work outside the scope needs another work order
LimitsThe rules that apply while you work: protective equipment, access, power, tools, and who may do what
Hold pointThe stage at which work stops until a named person inspects the work and releases you
DocumentsEach drawing set, standard, and record the work depends on, with the revision or the clauses that apply
Project factsValues that belong to this job only: a numbering rule, a position, a measured height

The revision

Each reissue of a sheet carries a new revision, shown in the sheet’s title block. A work order names the revision it was written against. Before you work from a sheet, compare the revision in its title block with the revision the work order names. If they differ, stop and ask the site lead (in class, your instructor acts as site lead) which governs: a layout marked from a superseded sheet is rework.

Limits and hold points

A limit applies for as long as you work under the order. A hold point is a single stage: you stop there, call the named person, and wait. That person inspects the work, then releases you to go on or holds the work and tells you what to correct.

On the mock-up, the owner’s standard sets three hold points for every bay (27 05 00 4.3): the marked layout, before any panel is cut; the electrical metallic tubing (EMT) pathway, before cable is pulled; and the completed bay, at handover. The first one comes before the cut because a pencil mark can be erased and a hole in a panel cannot.

Each work order also states its own hold point. Read it and record it, in the work order’s own words, before you start.

526.1.2 Inspecting before work

You inspect every item before you use it and record its condition as found.

A pre-work inspection covers your personal protective equipment (PPE), your tools, and the work area. You check each item at its check points: the places where a fault makes it unsafe or unreliable. You record the condition you found and whether the item is serviceable or reported.

As found means what you saw, not what the item should be. On a step ladder, “rails straight, feet and rivets intact” and “one rail split below the second step” are conditions. “OK” is not: it records no check point.

Set an unserviceable item apart, label it with the fault, and report it. Do not repair it. The site lead decides whether it is repaired, replaced, or tagged out of service.

Each item has its own check points. On a multimeter they are the lead insulation, the probe tips, the case and input jacks, and the category (CAT) rating printed on the meter.

Checking a level

Place the level on a flat surface and note where the bubble sits, then turn the level end for end in the same place. On a true level the bubble does not move. If it moves, the vial is out of true. Every mark drawn with that level is out by the same error.

526.1.3 The job hazard analysis

A job hazard analysis lists each task, what in it can hurt someone, what prevents that, and what makes the installer stop.

You write a job hazard analysis (JHA), also called a task hazard analysis, before the work starts. It takes the work one task at a time. For each task it gives a hazard, a control, and, where the analysis asks for one, a stop condition.

  • Hazard. The cause and the harm it does: “a rack tips when a caster catches a floor box: the rack falls on a person.” A single word such as “tipping” names neither.
  • Control. An action, a setup, or a piece of equipment that removes the hazard or stops it reaching anyone. Each control answers the hazard beside it. “Be careful” and “work safely” change nothing about the task, so they are not controls.
  • Stop condition. Something you can see, hear, or feel that makes you stop the task and call the site lead. “Stop if it is unsafe” names no sign, so it is not a stop condition.

The analysis draws on the work order’s tasks and tools, the manufacturers’ instructions, and the owner’s standard. On the mock-up, clauses 27 05 00 4.5 and 4.6 set the rules for screws in the panels and for cable in the cavity between the wall’s two faces.

526.1.4 Safety data sheets and installation instructions

A chemical product comes with a safety data sheet. A hardware product comes with installation instructions. You find either one from the name on the product.

A safety data sheet (SDS) is the manufacturer’s document on the hazards and safe handling of a chemical product: a cable-pulling lubricant, a cleaning solvent, a firestop sealant. The Hazard Communication Standard of the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) sets the same 16 numbered sections, in the same order, for every SDS.

SDS sections an installer opens most often.
SectionTitleWhat you look up there
1IdentificationProduct name, manufacturer, and emergency phone number. The product name confirms that the sheet belongs to the product in your hand
2Hazard identificationThe product’s hazards, signal word, and pictograms
4First-aid measuresWhat to do after contact with the eyes or skin, after breathing it in, or after swallowing it
8Exposure controls and personal protectionThe protective equipment the manufacturer specifies
16Other informationThe date the sheet was prepared or last revised

Read the manufacturer’s name and the product name from the container or the package. Then find the SDS in the site’s SDS binder or library, on the manufacturer’s website, or on the distributor’s product page. Check that the name in Section 1 matches the container: two products from one manufacturer can have different hazards. Record where you found the SDS: a web address, the binder, or the package insert.

Installation instructions come with hardware such as a bracket, a box, or a raceway fitting. They give the opening size or a cut-out template, the wall thickness the product is rated for, the fasteners, and the steps. They come as a package insert, a template on the package, or a document on the manufacturer’s website.

On the mock-up, the owner’s standard gives material data: a table of product sizes for layout and for the material takeoff, the list of material a piece of work needs (27 05 00 4.8). Where a product on site differs from that table, the product’s own data governs. The clause also requires the contractor to report the difference by request for information (RFI): a written question to the owner’s representative, answered in writing. The product’s data comes from the manufacturer’s instructions.

Lesson 526.1 · Section 2 Power and the Meter

526.1.5 Power sources near a work area

Before work starts at a wall, the installer lists every power source near it and decides which ones can reach the work.

The mock-up walls carry no wiring of their own, but a cord can run along the floor beside a wall or into its cavity. A screw point or a saw blade that goes through a hardboard panel enters that cavity, where the installer cannot see.

What counts as a power source

Receptacles, floor boxes, power strips, extension cords, and equipment with a power cord all count. So does Power over Ethernet (PoE) equipment: a switch or an injector that puts direct current (DC) voltage on a data cable to power a camera, an access point, or a phone. A data cable can carry power.

Identify by looking

Identify each source without touching, testing, or moving it. For each source, record what it is, where it is, and what you see: a load plugged in, an indicator lit, or nothing plugged in. Only the site lead plugs a source in, unplugs it, or switches it.

Which sources affect the work area

A source affects a bay when the source or its cord lies in the floor space where the installer stands to work at that bay, or when a cord from it runs onto the wall or into the wall’s cavity. A screw point or a blade can reach a cord in the cavity; a ladder or a dropped tool can damage one on the floor. A receptacle across the aisle with nothing plugged into it is a source near the wall, but it affects no bay.

526.1.6 Setting up the meter

A meter reads voltage correctly only with its leads in the right jacks and the function set for the kind of voltage. It is safe to use only on a circuit within its rating.

Leads and jacks

For a voltage measurement, the black lead goes in the jack marked COM (common) and the red lead in the jack marked V. With a lead in the current jack, marked A or mA, the meter has almost no resistance between its probes. Touching the probes across a live circuit can blow the meter’s fuse or damage the meter.

The function

The voltage positions on the rotary switch are marked with a V and a symbol:

  • AC volts — a V with a wavy line (∿). A building receptacle supplies alternating current (AC).
  • DC volts — a V with a solid line over a dashed line (⎓). A battery and a PoE source deliver direct current (DC). A low-voltage power supply can deliver either: 24 V AC transformers are common. Set the function from the voltage and the symbol on the source’s label.

On the AC volts function, many meters read a DC source as 0 V or close to it. A wrong function can make a live circuit look dead.

An auto-ranging meter chooses its own range once the function is set. On a manual-ranging meter, choose a range above the voltage you expect. On DC volts, a minus sign before the reading means the red probe is on the negative side. The value without the sign is the voltage.

The category rating

Every meter and lead set carries a measurement category (CAT) rating and a voltage, for example CAT II 300 V. The category states how close to the building’s electrical service the meter can be used.

  • CAT II — equipment plugged into a receptacle, and the receptacle.
  • CAT III — the building’s distribution: panels, feeders, and fixed wiring.
  • CAT IV — the origin of the installation: the service entrance and the utility meter.

A meter often carries more than one rating, such as CAT III 600 V and CAT II 1000 V; each rating is a separate limit. Where the meter and its leads differ, the lower rating applies. When you inspect a meter, record every category and voltage printed on it.

Where a PoE source puts its voltage

An Ethernet cable has eight conductors in four twisted pairs, ending on pins 1 to 8 of its connector. A PoE source puts DC voltage between two pairs of pins: pins 1 and 2 against pins 3 and 6 (Alternative A), pins 4 and 5 against pins 7 and 8 (Alternative B), or both on a four-pair source. A breakout is a small board with an Ethernet jack and a screw terminal for each of its eight pins, so that a meter probe can reach each conductor.

A PoE source built to the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 802.3 standard applies only a small detection voltage to the cable, to test for a powered device on the far end. It applies full power only once it detects one. With no device connected, a meter on a working source reads about 0 V, or a small value that flickers while the source tests for a device. A passive injector applies its voltage whether or not a device is connected. The source’s label gives its rated output.

526.1.7 Prove, test, prove again

A reading of 0 V shows that a circuit is dead only if the same meter reads a live source correctly immediately before and immediately after it.

A broken lead, a wrong function, a probe on the plastic of a terminal, or a failing meter battery can each make a live circuit read 0 V. The sequence called prove, test, prove again — also called test before touch — rules out each of these failures.

  1. Prepare. Inspect the leads and probes, put them in COM and V, and select the function.
  2. Prove. Place the probes on a known live source: the circuit itself before it is isolated, or a separate source of the same kind of voltage, such as a tool battery for a DC circuit. The meter must show the source’s voltage; the site lead gives you anything you need to judge the reading. On a PoE source, probe every combination the source can use — pin 1 against pin 3, then pin 4 against pin 7 — and read aloud each one that shows voltage. Where more than one does, use the first one you found for the rest of the sequence.
  3. Isolate. The site lead switches off or unplugs the circuit and tags it. You do not change the power state.
  4. Test. Place the probes on the isolated circuit and read the value aloud. Where you proved on the circuit itself, use the same terminals.
  5. Prove again. Read the same known source again with the same function: the restored circuit on the same terminals, or the same separate source. If the meter reads the live voltage again, it was working during the test. The test reading then describes the circuit.

Read each value aloud as the display shows it. Write the values down after the second proving, once you have stepped back with the probes clear of the circuit.

When a reading is wrong

The meter reads 0 V on a source that should be live. Stop. Do not go on to the test: an unproved meter cannot show that anything is dead. Check the leads, the jacks, the function, and the probe contact, then prove again. If it still reads 0 V, tell the site lead.

The meter reads a voltage on the isolated circuit. Stop. Keep your hands and the probes away from the circuit and report the reading to the site lead exactly as the display shows it, even a few millivolts. The site lead decides what the reading means. Treat the circuit as live until the site lead has found and cleared the cause.

On a building power circuit

A building power circuit, such as a 120 V receptacle circuit, is confirmed dead in the same way, with one change of order: the circuit is locked out first, and the meter is proved on a separate live source. On this project, only a qualified electrician carries it out. The electrician:

  • switches off the circuit’s breaker, then locks and tags it under the site’s lockout/tagout procedure;
  • selects a multimeter and leads rated CAT II or higher, at a voltage no lower than the circuit’s, and sets the meter to AC volts;
  • proves the meter on a known live source of the same voltage, such as a receptacle on another circuit;
  • tests the locked-out circuit at the point of work between each pair of conductors: hot to neutral, hot to ground, and neutral to ground;
  • proves the meter again on the same known source.

A non-contact voltage detector cannot prove a circuit dead: it can stay silent beside a live conductor.

Before you work near the circuit, confirm with the site lead that the lock and tag are in place and that the electrician has completed the sequence.

A site lead is about to switch off a 24 V DC power supply so that an installer can work on the cable it feeds. The supply, while it is on, is the known live source. Put the installer’s steps in order.

526.1.8 Reading a tape measure

A tape divides each inch into sixteenths. The length of each mark tells you which fraction it is.

On most construction tapes, each inch carries 15 marks between its numbered ends. The longest mark between two numbers is the half inch. The next longest are the quarters, then the eighths, and the shortest are the sixteenths. Some tapes also mark thirty-seconds along the first foot.

To read a length, take the last whole inch the end of the object has passed, then count sixteenths from it to the end of the object, and reduce the fraction. Eleven sixteenths past 13 reads 13 11/16 in. Six sixteenths past 14 reads 14 6/16, which reduces to 14 3/8 in.

Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled Reading a tape to the sixteenth of an inch. It shows a length of tape blade with the inch numbers 13, 14 and 15. Between each pair of inch numbers there are 15 marks of four lengths.

Using the tape

  • Hook or push. Hook the end over an edge to measure from that edge, or push it against a surface for an inside measurement. The hook slides by about its own thickness, out when hooked and in when pushed, so the zero is correct both ways. The movement is deliberate. A bent hook, or one that moves farther than its own thickness on loose rivets, gives a false zero.
  • Start from the 1 in mark. Where the hook cannot catch, hold the 1 in mark on the reference edge and subtract 1 in from the reading. Note the 1 in start beside the reading, so the extra inch does not stay in the figure.
  • Read square. A blade held at an angle to the edge reads long.
  • Return it slowly. A blade that snaps back can bend the hook.

526.1.9 Fractions, decimals, and units

Do layout arithmetic in sixteenths, or in thirty-seconds where halving an odd number of sixteenths calls for them, and write each result in lowest terms.

Lowest terms

A fraction is in lowest terms when the top and bottom numbers have no common factor other than 1. Divide both by 2 until the top number is odd: 10/16 is 5/8, and 12/16 is 3/4.

Adding, subtracting, and halving

Put every fraction over the same bottom number: 16, or 32 where halving has produced thirty-seconds. Add or subtract the whole inches and the fractions separately. Carry or borrow one inch in the same unit as the fractions — 16/16 in sixteenths, 32/32 in thirty-seconds — then reduce. To halve a dimension, turn it into sixteenths and divide by 2. Where the number of sixteenths is odd, the half comes out in thirty-seconds.

A position on a drawing is usually the center of a box. Its top edge is the center plus half the box’s height; its bottom edge is the center minus half the height.

Feet and inches, decimals, and metric

To convert inches to feet and inches, divide by 12: the whole number is the feet, and the remainder is the inches. 82 1/8 in is 6 ft 10 1/8 in, because 6 ft is 72 in.

To convert a decimal to sixteenths, multiply the decimal part by 16 and round to the nearest whole number: 7.3 in gives 0.3 × 16 = 4.8, so about 7 5/16 in. Say “about” when you round.

One inch is exactly 25.4 mm. A product 400 mm wide is 400 ÷ 25.4 = about 15.75 in; 0.75 × 16 = 12, so about 15 12/16, or 15 3/4 in. The owner’s standard gives the 90 m limit on a permanent link, the installed cable from the outlet to the patch panel, as 295 ft (27 15 00 2.2).

526.1.10 Measuring against the drawings

Before a layout is marked, the installer measures the work area and compares each measurement with the dimension the drawings give.

The owner’s standard requires the contractor to verify dimensions and conditions on site before installation and to report differences from the drawings in writing (27 05 00 1.2). On the mock-up, sheet TW-001, general note 2, marks the drawing set as a provisional issue: it takes the panels and the screws that hold them to the frame from a site measurement, and the overall wall from the wall maker’s quote. The note requires you to verify every dimension on your wall before you cut, and to report a difference by RFI before you work around it.

The reference edges

Every position on a layout is measured from fixed reference edges, called the datum. On the mock-up, sheet TW-001, note 3, sets it. The panel field is the area the panels cover on a face. x is measured from the left edge of the bay, and y from the bottom of the panel field, as seen by a person standing in front of the face. A position written “x, y” on the drawings is the center of the box or plate.

Measure every position from the datum, not from the last mark. Three marks chained one from another, each 1/16 in off in the same direction, put the third mark 3/16 in out. Measured from the datum, each carries only its own error.

Tolerance and the statement

A tolerance is the band on either side of a drawing dimension within which a measured value is accepted. A width drawn as 48 in with a tolerance of ±1/4 in accepts any measured value from 47 3/4 to 48 1/4 in. Compare each measurement with its drawing dimension and state one of these:

  • Agrees — the measured value lies within the tolerance of the drawing value.
  • Differs — it lies outside. A difference between the drawings and the site is a subject for an RFI.
  • Not given — the drawings give no value for it. Record what you measured. A dimension the work depends on and the drawings leave out is also a subject for an RFI.

Write each measured value beside the dimension it checks, at the time you take it.

526.1.11 Marking a layout

A layout is a set of pencil marks that puts each position from the drawings on the panel, where the installer will later cut and fasten.

The center cross

Each box or bracket is marked at its center with a center cross: a horizontal arm and a vertical arm crossing at the position. Draw the horizontal arm along the top edge of the level with the bubble centered in the middle vial, so the arm is level. Draw the vertical arm along the level’s edge with the bubble centered in the end vial, so the arm is plumb. The installer later squares the box to these arms.

Mark in pencil only. Erase a wrong mark completely before drawing the correct one: an installer can mistake a faint old mark for the cut line. Mark a pathway, such as a raceway, as a line between two points, each measured from the datum.

A reserved zone is an area kept clear for later work. The drawings show it hatched or outlined and labeled. Mark nothing in it.

From the center to an edge

The drawings give centers; the product data gives sizes. Find an edge by adding or subtracting half the size. The clearance between a box and a fixed line, such as a panel edge, is the distance from the center to that line minus half the box’s size across it: half its width for a plumb line, half its height for a level line.

A panel screw, one of the four screws that hold a panel to the frame, is a point, not a line. Take two measurements from the center of the mark: across, with the tape held level, to the point directly above or below the screw; and up or down, with the tape held plumb, to the height of the screw. Subtract half the box’s width from the measurement across, and half its height from the measurement up or down. The screw may lie beside the box, above or below it, or off a corner. Take the larger of the two results as the clearance; off a corner, the screw lies slightly farther from the box than that figure.

Why the owner’s standard keeps cuts away from edges and screws

On the mock-up, clause 27 05 00 4.5 of the owner’s standard keeps every cut and fastener at least 2 in from a panel edge, a panel joint, or a panel screw. The EMT and the raceway may cross a panel joint or the top edge of the panel field where sheet TW-201 shows them; their strap and base screws stay 2 in clear. A screw close to the edge of a hardboard panel can split the edge out. A box fastened over a panel screw covers it, so the panel cannot come off the frame until the box comes off. Where a drawn position leaves a clearance under 2 in, the drawing conflicts with the standard. Raise the conflict by RFI.

526.1.12 Material takeoff and cable length

A takeoff lists the material a piece of work needs, line by line, with the quantity, the unit, and the document that gives each figure.

You work a material takeoff from the documents. Each line names one item, gives its quantity and unit, and cites its reference: the sheet and note, the schedule row, or the clause that sets the figure. A line without a reference cannot be checked.

  • Counted items (each) come from the schedule (the drawing set’s table of elements and their positions), the elevations, and the fittings each typical detail calls for.
  • Rule-based items come from a clause that sets a number per element, such as screws per box.
  • Lengths (inches or feet) come from your own layout arithmetic along the pathway.

Along a pathway with bends, add the straight lengths between the points the documents give. A tube that runs 5 in from a box to a first bend, 7 in to a second bend, then 9 in to its end is 5 + 7 + 9 = 21 in long.

Keep the unit the takeoff asks for: a length in feet on a line that asks for inches is a wrong line.

Fasteners at a maximum spacing

A rule such as “one fastener at each end and at no more than 20 in centers” is counted in two steps. Divide the length by the maximum spacing and round up to get the number of spaces. Add one to get the number of fasteners. A raceway 52 in long gives 52 ÷ 20 = 2.6, so 3 spaces and 4 fasteners, about 17 5/16 in apart. Rounding down would leave a space longer than the rule allows.

Cable length

On the mock-up, owner’s standard 27 05 00 4.7 sets a cable’s cut length as the sum of six parts. Three carry the cable from the box to the rack: the run on the wall from the top of the box to the top of the panel field, the rise to the ladder, and the run along the ladder to the rack. The other three are the slack, the drop at the rack, and the service loop. The slack is spare cable left at the box. The drop is the length from the ladder down to the patch panel. The service loop is spare cable coiled on the ladder at the rack end. The figure shows the six parts on a mock-up wall built for another project, with that project’s allowances.

526.1.13 Requests for information

A request for information puts a question about the documents in writing, to the person who can answer it, before the affected work goes ahead.

An RFI goes from the contractor to the owner’s representative or the designer. The owner’s standard requires one where two documents conflict (27 05 00 1.1) and where a product differs from the standard’s material data (4.8). Clause 1.2 requires differences between the drawings and the site to be reported in writing; on the mock-up, sheet TW-001, note 2, names the RFI as the way to report them. For a conflict, clause 1.1 also stops the affected work until the answer arrives. Raise a missing dimension that the work depends on the same way.

Lesson 526.1 · Section 4 Layout, Takeoff, and RFIs

526.1.14 Checking an AI takeoff

An AI tool can produce a takeoff in seconds. The installer who submits a takeoff answers for every figure in it.

A general-purpose AI assistant, such as Microsoft Copilot, has not read the project’s drawings or the owner’s standard unless the prompt includes them. Its reply reads the same whether a figure is right or wrong.

A takeoff from an AI tool can use a common product size instead of the specified one, round a length or give it in another unit, apply a per-element rule to the wrong elements, or add an item the drawings do not show.

The check

  1. Finish your own takeoff first, with a reference on every line.
  2. Put the project’s own data in the prompt — the schedule rows, the material data, the line names — and ask for each line’s quantity and unit. Keep the prompt and the whole reply as they were.
  3. Compare the reply with your takeoff line by line. List every line where the quantity or the unit differs.
  4. For each difference, open the reference and decide which figure is right: yours, the tool’s, or neither. Where neither is right, record the correct figure. Where your own figure is wrong, correct it and record the original.

The reference decides each difference. A figure is not right because the tool produced it, or because you did.

Lesson 526.1 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

526.1.15 What you covered

The site lead releases an installer for wall work after an inspection, a hazard analysis, and a proved meter. The owner’s representative releases a layout after the installer measures and marks it and the representative inspects it.

Before work starts

Check the revision a work order names against each sheet’s title block, and record the hold point the work order states. Record each item’s condition as found, then “serviceable” or “reported”. Give each task in a job hazard analysis a hazard with its harm and a control that addresses it. An SDS gives first aid in Section 4.

Power and the meter

A source affects a bay when it or its cord lies where the installer stands, or a cord runs onto the wall or into its cavity. Set the function from the source’s label: DC volts for a battery, PoE, or a DC supply. Prove, test, prove again with the same function: a test reading counts only between two good provings on the same known source. On a building power circuit, a qualified electrician applies lockout/tagout, then proves the meter, tests, and proves again with the meter set to AC volts.

Measuring and converting

The length of a tape mark shows whether it is a half, a quarter, an eighth, or a sixteenth. Work in sixteenths or thirty-seconds, borrow in the same unit, and reduce to lowest terms.
