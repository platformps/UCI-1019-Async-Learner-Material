How a technician mounts and wires the devices of an access-controlled door, what the codes require of a door people must exit through, how the door is commissioned and tested, and what the record handed to the owner contains.

An access-controlled door is a lock, the devices that report on the door, and a controller that decides when the lock releases.

A card reader opens nothing by itself. A controller decides whether the lock releases; Ubiquiti calls it an access hub. The hub holds the credentials and rules the access-control application sends it. It reads the devices at the door and switches the power that holds the lock. The equipment in the rack that the door devices connect to is the head end.

The secure side of a door is the side the lock protects; the public side is the side a visitor approaches from. The reader faces the public side. The device that releases the door for someone leaving sits on the secure side.

The diagram below shows how each part connects. On the mock-up, the hub is the UniFi Access Door Hub from the G3 Starter Kit Pro, and one hub serves one door. The hub takes Power over Ethernet (PoE) from a 60 W PoE++ injector, since the kit includes no injector. The hub in turn powers the reader and the lock. The console is a CloudKey+ running the UniFi Access application. The hub keeps a copy of the credentials the console has sent it. If the console goes offline, the door still opens for those credentials, but a new credential needs the console.

533.4.2 Fail-safe and fail-secure locks

When its power fails, a lock either releases or stays locked; its type decides which. On the Door Hub, the wiring must match that type.

A fail-safe lock releases when it loses power. A fail-secure lock stays locked when it loses power, and needs power to release. A magnetic lock holds only while current flows through it, so it is fail-safe. An electric strike is made in either form, and its specification sheet states which.

The lock output is a relay

The hub switches lock power with a relay, an electrically operated switch whose contact has three terminals: Common (COM), Normally Open (NO), and Normally Closed (NC). The names describe the relay at rest.

The hub’s lock relay: which terminal pair is connected at rest and when the hub grants access.
Relay stateNO to COMNC to COM
At rest (relay off)OpenClosed
Triggered (access granted)ClosedOpen

A lock wired to NC and COM has current at rest and loses it when the hub grants access: the wiring for a fail-safe lock. A lock wired to NO and COM has no current at rest and receives it when the hub grants access: the wiring for a fail-secure lock. Ubiquiti’s instructions put a fail-safe lock, a magnetic lock included, on NC and COM, and a fail-secure lock on NO and COM.

No lock-type setting on the Door Hub

The Door Hub’s settings in UniFi Access offer no fail-safe or fail-secure choice for its lock. The lock setting the application offers is the trigger duration: how long the lock stays released after the hub grants access. A lock landed on the wrong pair of terminals behaves the opposite way. The wiring diagram names the pair.

Powered and dry outputs

The Door Hub has two lock relays. The output labeled LOCK – POWERED supplies 12 V direct current (DC), up to 1 A, from the hub itself, and must not be connected to another power source. The output labeled LOCK – NO POWER (DRY) switches a circuit that an external supply powers, up to 30 V DC and 1 A, for a lock that does not run at 12 V. Neither output takes a lock that needs alternating current (AC). If the hub loses its own power, the powered output loses its 12 V, and a fail-safe lock on it releases.

On another job, a 24 V DC fail-secure strike lands on the dry output, on NO and COM, with its own 24 V DC supply switched through the relay.

533.4.3 Magnetic locks and electric strikes

A magnetic lock holds the door with current through a coil. An electric strike lets a latched door open. Each draws a current that the hub output must carry.

The magnetic lock

A magnetic lock is an electromagnet fixed to the door frame’s header, and a steel armature plate fixed to the door. With current in the coil, the magnet holds the armature. With the current cut, it lets go. The Seco-Larm E-941SA-600 on the mock-up holds 600 lb. It runs at 12 V or 24 V DC, selected by jumpers inside the magnet, and draws 500 mA at 12 V or 250 mA at 24 V. Its manual warns that a wrong voltage setting may damage the lock.

When the current in a coil is cut, the coil produces a voltage spike. A diode across the coil is one way to suppress it. The E-941SA-600 has a metal oxide varistor (MOV) built in for that purpose. Its manual says to remove any diode fitted across the lock, because a diode can delay the release and leave residual magnetism.

The electric strike

An electric strike replaces the strike plate in the door frame. When it releases, the door’s latch passes through it, and the door opens without anyone withdrawing the latch. Ubiquiti’s fail-safe strike, the UACC-Lock-Strike-Safe-15mm, runs at 12 V DC and draws 400 mA, ±10 %.

Check the current against the output

Before you wire a lock, compare its current with the rating of the output it will land on. The Door Hub’s lock outputs carry 1 A. Its powered siren output, AUX – SIREN/CHIME, carries 12 V DC at 0.33 A. The hub drives that output from its door alarm and chime settings, not from access grants.

533.4.4 The contacts the hub reads

The push-to-exit button and the door position switch are contacts. The hub powers neither; it reads whether each is open or closed.

A dry contact is a switch with no power of its own on it. A contact is normally open (NO) if it is open at rest and closes when operated, or normally closed (NC) if it is closed at rest and opens when operated. In UniFi Access, each input’s trigger level tells the hub which change to act on: close active (NO) or open active (NC).

Where a motion sensor serves as the request to exit, Ubiquiti recommends a push-to-exit button as a manual override, with clear labeling such as PUSH TO EXIT.

The EMERGENCY input switches the door into the one mode set for it in the application: Evacuation, which unlocks doors, or Lockdown, which locks them. A further option applies the mode to every location at the site. Ubiquiti states that a mode the fire alarm panel triggers cannot be ended from the Access application, the Access mobile app, or Admin Mode on a reader. Which doors release, on which fire alarm signal, belongs to the fire alarm system’s design: the technician lands the interface only where the fire alarm drawings show it, and sets the mode the designer specifies. On the mock-up, nothing connects to a fire alarm system, so the input stays unwired and the emergency modes stay off.

533.4.5 Mounting and routing the door devices

Each device goes where the drawing places it, fixed as its manufacturer’s instructions show, with its own cable back to the head end.

The access-control drawings give each device’s position. The manufacturer’s instructions and template show how it is fixed. Where a door or frame arrives prepared for a device, use the preparation as it is.

The magnetic lock

The E-941SA-600’s manual places the magnet on the frame header, near the upper corner opposite the hinges, on the inside of the door it secures. The armature plate goes on the door with a rubber washer between two steel washers, so that it pivots flat against the magnet face. Brackets adapt the lock where the door or the header differs from the manual’s.

The reader and the push-to-exit button

A reader goes on its mounting plate, level, with its cable entering from the wall behind it. The SD-7103GC-PEQ push-to-exit plate screws into a back box. The plate measures 4½ in high, 2¾ in wide, and 1¾ in deep.

The door position switch

Door position switches come in recessed, plunger, surface, and overhead styles. Each switch’s instructions give the largest gap allowed between switch and magnet with the door shut. Beyond that gap, the switch reads the door as open.

One cable for each device

  • Run one cable from each device to the head end. The owner’s Security Addendum names the cable for each: Category 5e for the reader, stranded 22 American Wire Gauge (AWG) four-conductor for the push-to-exit button and the door position switch, and stranded 18 AWG two-conductor for the lock, each rated for riser use.
  • Fit a grommet where a cable passes through a hole cut in a panel.
  • Fix the cable inside the wall with hook-and-loop ties.
  • Label each cable at both ends, to the label scheme on the drawings.
  • Leave at the hub the slack that the owner’s addendum asks for.

Ubiquiti’s access devices need all four pairs of the cable connected for PoE. The manufacturer warns that a connection on four wires only may cause the device to malfunction. A reader may be up to 100 m of cable from its hub. Where a PoE switch powers the hub, the cable from the switch to the hub and on to the reader may total no more than 100 m.

533.4.6 Power-limited circuits and conductor size

The circuits at an access-controlled door are low-voltage, power-limited circuits. The lock’s current and the length of its run set the size of its conductors.

Class 2 circuits

The National Electrical Code (NEC) covers Class 2 power-limited circuits. In the 2023 edition, Article 725 covers Class 2 and Class 3 circuits, and the requirements for their cables sit in a new Article 722, Cables for Power-Limited Circuits and Fault-Managed Power Circuits. Earlier editions arranged these requirements differently: the Door Hub’s specification sheet cites NEC Article 725.121, a section number from the earlier arrangement. The 2023 article numbers here come from published summaries of that edition, not from the code text. The 2026 edition renumbers these requirements again. Read the requirement in the edition the jurisdiction has adopted.

A Class 2 circuit takes its power from a power source the code permits, such as a listed Class 2 power supply. The E-941SA-600’s manual calls for a listed, regulated, power-limited Class 2 supply. The Door Hub accepts PoE++, a power-limited Class 2 supply listed to Underwriters Laboratories (UL) 294, or a limited power source listed to UL 60950-1 or UL 62368-1. UL 294 is the safety standard for access-control equipment; the Door Hub’s specification sheet states a UL 294 Level I rating for the hub.

Conductor size

The longer the run and the larger the lock’s current, the heavier the conductor the lock needs. Ubiquiti publishes the longest recommended lock cable for each current and conductor size.

533.4.7 What the codes require of an exit door

Where people must be able to leave through an electrically locked door, the life-safety codes set how the lock releases. The designer applies them.

Two model codes carry the requirements: National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 101, the Life Safety Code, and the International Building Code (IBC). A jurisdiction adopts an edition of each, and the adopted edition governs the door. The wording summarized below comes from two sources, and section numbers can differ between editions:

  • NFPA 101, section 7.2.1.6.2, Sensor-Release of Electrical Locking Systems. Taken from the NFPA committee’s First Draft Report for the next edition, which reproduces the 2024 text with proposed changes. The adopted wording may differ in detail.
  • IBC 2021, section 1010.2.12, Sensor release of electrically locked egress doors. Taken from the 2021 code text. In the 2024 IBC the same requirements are section 1010.2.11, by a published summary of that edition.

What a sensor-release door must do

  1. A sensor on the egress side unlocks the door in the direction of egress when it detects an approaching occupant.
  2. Loss of power to the sensor, or to the part of the system that locks the door, unlocks the door.
  3. A manual release device on the egress side, 40 to 48 in above the floor and signed PUSH TO EXIT, sits within 60 in of the door in the NFPA 101 text and within 5 ft in the IBC 2021 text. Operating it interrupts the power to the lock directly, independent of the locking system’s electronics, and the lock stays unlocked for at least 30 seconds.
  4. Activation of the fire alarm system, where one is provided, unlocks the door until the system is reset. Activation of the sprinkler or fire detection system, where provided, unlocks it too.
  5. Emergency lighting serves the egress side of the door; NFPA 101 applies this to new installations.

The IBC 2021 text also requires the locking system to be listed to UL 294.

What the codes mean for the wiring

A push-to-exit button that only signals the hub does not meet the third requirement: the hub’s electronics, not the button, then release the lock. On a sensor-release door, the button’s own contact sits in the lock’s power circuit. A button with two independent contacts, such as the SD-7103GC-PEQ, can both cut the power and signal the hub. Its normally closed contact, in series with the lock’s positive lead, cuts the power directly. Its normally open contact signals the hub, which logs the exit. Seco-Larm’s lock manual says to connect such switching devices between the power source and the lock’s positive terminal, because on the negative side they may delay the release.

The 30-second hold needs a timer. The SD-7103GC-PEQ has none. Its variant, the SD-7103GC-PTQ, adds a timer adjustable from 1 to 180 seconds, whose own relay usually switches the lock. Whether a timer built into the button meets “independent of the electronics” is a question for the authority having jurisdiction.

Who decides

Choosing a door’s release method, its devices, and its interface with the fire alarm system is design work. The designer does it, under the code the jurisdiction enforces. The technician installs what the drawings show. Where the drawings and the code text appear to disagree, the technician raises a request for information (RFI) before wiring.

The mock-up door is a training door

The owner’s Security Addendum states that the mock-up door is a training door, not an egress door, and that nothing on it connects to a fire alarm system. Its push-to-exit button signals the hub only: the normally open contact goes to the hub’s exit-button input, and the normally closed contact stays unconnected. The reader and the push-to-exit button also share the door’s public face, for access to the wall. In a building, the button sits on the secure side. The mock-up door is not a pattern for an occupied building.

533.4.8 Isolate, prove, land, inspect

The installer lands conductors on a hub with its power off and proves each cable first. The site lead inspects every termination before the power returns.

Isolate at the source

Where the lock is on the hub’s powered output, as on the mock-up, and the hub takes PoE, one source feeds the whole door: the injector or switch port that powers the hub also powers the reader and the lock through it. Removing that source isolates the door only when every device is powered from the hub. A lock on the dry output has its own supply, which you isolate as well. On the mock-up, the site lead unplugs each injector at its AC end and hangs a tag on the plug, with a name and the time. Only the person named on the tag removes it. Before you touch a hub, look at its injector: the plug is out, and the tag is on it.

Prove the cable before you land it

Set a meter to resistance or continuity and read across the two conductors at the head end, with the device connected at the far end. The reading proves the cable and the device together. A coil, such as a lock’s, reads a resistance. A closed contact reads near zero. An open contact or a broken conductor reads open, which many meters show as OL. Operate the device and read again: a contact changes state when its device operates. A reading that does not follow the device points to a splice, a terminal, or a conductor.

On a warehouse job, a door position switch on a roll-up door reads OL at the head end with the door shut. Read across the switch’s own leads at the door. If it reads closed there, the fault is in the cable or a splice. If it reads open there, the switch and its magnet are out of line or farther apart than the switch allows, or the switch has failed.

Land each conductor

  • Read the terminal on the wiring diagram, and find the label printed beside it on the hub.
  • Strip each conductor to the length the hub’s instructions give. Twist the strands, insert the conductor fully, and tighten the terminal.
  • Tug each conductor. It holds, and no bare copper shows outside the terminal.
  • Fold back and tape each conductor the diagram does not use.
  • Dress the slack, and keep the cable label readable.

Inspect, then power

The site lead checks every termination on the door against the wiring diagram before removing the tag. A termination that fails goes back to its installer for correction and a second check. Once the hub has power, a fail-safe lock on NC and COM holds the door at rest.

Open each card. Each names one thing the site lead checks at every termination.

533.4.9 Adopting the hub

A hub joins the access-control system when the application adopts it. The application can adopt only a hub its console can reach.

A new hub on the network waits for adoption. In UniFi Access, the hub appears under Devices; select it to adopt it. The Access mobile app and Ubiquiti’s Site Manager can also adopt it. Once adopted, the hub takes its credentials and rules from the console. The installer names the hub and its door with the identifiers on the drawings.

Readers connect to the hub’s PoE ports, on the hub’s own network segment. A reader and its hub cannot communicate across a Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN) boundary. A new G3 Reader Pro needs a direct connection to a hub’s PoE port for its first adoption and for the firmware update that may come with it.

The console’s network

The CloudKey+ that runs UniFi Access on the mock-up adopts devices on a single VLAN only. It lacks the All Networks setting that lets other consoles adopt across VLANs. A hub on another VLAN does not appear for it to adopt. Where the hubs sit on the network is the network team’s decision. On the mock-up, each hub’s switch port is on the console’s network.

Where a hub does not appear, check its power and its switch port against the network plan. The manufacturer’s troubleshooting steps include a factory reset: hold RESET for ~10 seconds; the LED turns steady white when the reset is done. The site lead decides whether to reset a hub.

Record what you adopted

For the hub and its reader, record the model, the firmware version, the name, and the status, as the application shows them.

533.4.10 People, credentials, policies, and schedules

A credential opens a door only when a person holds it and a policy gives that person the door at that time.

UniFi Access keeps four things apart:

  • A person is a user record.
  • A credential is what the person presents. On the mock-up, it is a 13.56 MHz Near Field Communication (NFC) keyfob. Ubiquiti’s own cards and keyfobs are encrypted for the site they serve.
  • A schedule is a set of time windows, with holidays where they apply.
  • An access policy ties people to locations, such as doors, and to a schedule.

A person with no policy cannot unlock any door, whatever credential the person holds. Where a person has several policies, their time windows combine.

Enrolling a keyfob

A keyfob is enrolled at a reader. In the application’s card inventory, the installer adds a new NFC card, selects a reader, and holds the keyfob to it for ~5 seconds. The installer then assigns the card to a person. The menu names change between versions, and the site lead posts the ones in use.

Test credentials

A test credential proves a door without belonging to a member of staff. The owner’s Security Addendum allows test credentials only, one per technician, revoked before hand-off. Name each test person with a code, not a personal name.

Lesson 533.4 · Section 3 Commissioning the Door

533.4.11 Testing, revoking, and reading the event log

Each test of the door has a time, an action, what the door did, and what the log recorded. The record holds all four, even where they disagree.

The tests

The tests that prove an access-controlled door, and what you do for each.
TestWhat you do
Allowed entryPresent an enrolled credential inside its schedule window, then open and close the door
Denied entryPresent a credential that is not enrolled, or one outside its window
Request to exitPress the push-to-exit button, then open and close the door
Door positionNote when the door opens and when it closes
Revoked credentialRemove the credential from the person, then present it

Note each test’s time to the minute, from a clock you can read later. Present a credential and watch the door. Do not pull on a locked door.

Reading the event log

The hub writes an event for what happens at its door, and the application lists the events of every door in one log. Find your door’s entries by the door’s name and the time. Record each entry word for word as the log shows it; a paraphrase loses the application’s wording. Where the log shows no entry for a test, record “no entry”. A test whose result differs from what you expected goes into the record as it happened.

Revoking

To revoke a credential, remove it from the person. Prove the revoke at the door, not on the screen: present the credential, watch the lock, and read the log at that time.

Restoring the baseline

Before you leave a door, delete what you created for the tests: the policy, the schedule, the test person, and the credential in the card inventory. Then read each list again and confirm that none of your entries remains.

Lesson 533.4 · Section 4 The Hand-Off

533.4.12 What the hand-off record carries

The hand-off record lets a technician who has never seen the site find each device, its connection, its settings, and the proof that it worked.

The owner’s Security Addendum, clause E10, lists what the contractor delivers for each device:

  • Identity: the model and the firmware version.
  • Location identifier: the device’s identifier on the drawings.
  • Port or terminal: where the device connects at the head end.
  • Cable label: the label on its cable.
  • Test evidence: how the device was proved, and the result.
  • Settings backup or manual recovery record: an exported settings file, or the settings a technician would enter again to restore the device.

Test evidence claims what the test showed

State the test, the tool or method, and the result as recorded. Claim no more than the test showed. A wiremap pass shows the conductors’ order and continuity, not the cable’s performance. An allowed entry shows that one credential opened one door at one time.

Where each value comes from

Read each value from the installed system where you can: the device’s label, the hub’s terminal labels, the application. Where a value cannot be read without disturbing the system, take it from the records made during the installation, and mark its source. Where no value exists, list it as an open item, with what would fill it.

Credentials stay out

No password, passphrase, credential number, or account name appears in a record, a photograph, a screenshot, or a prompt to an AI tool (Security Addendum, clause E9). Crop the sign-in name out of each screenshot. The record states that a test credential or an account was used and removed, never what it was.

Match each question a technician might bring to the record with the entry that answers it.

Lesson 533.4 · Section 4 The Hand-Off

533.4.13 Checking an AI-drafted record

An AI tool can draft a hand-off table from field notes. The technician checks every value in it against the installed system.

Ask for a draft you can check

  • Give the tool your field notes unedited, and the column names of the hand-off table.
  • Tell it to fill each cell from the notes only, and to leave empty any cell the notes do not fill.
  • Ask it to list every empty cell.
  • Read the prompt before you send it. It holds no password, account name, or credential number.

What a draft can get wrong

A draft can fill a gap with a value that looks right, carry a value into the wrong row, or change a port or terminal while rewording. Nothing in the draft marks these errors.

Verify, then correct

Before you look at a device, write what the draft says you will find. Then read the device, its label, its terminal, or the application, and write what you found. Correct each value that differs. Delete each value that is in neither your notes nor the system. List every correction with what the draft said and what you found. Keep your prompt and the tool’s reply with the record.

Lesson 533.4 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

533.4.14 What you covered

Mount and wire the door’s devices with the power isolated, prove the door with a test credential, read the log against the tests, and hand over a record that a stranger can work from.

The access-controlled door

The access hub decides: it holds credentials and rules, reads its inputs, and switches the lock’s power. The lock’s type decides what it does when its power fails. On the Door Hub, the wiring must match the lock: a fail-safe lock on NC and COM, a fail-secure lock on NO and COM. Check every lock’s current against its output. Without a door position switch, the hub cannot see a forced door. The emergency input and its mode follow the fire alarm design.

Mounting, wiring, and the codes

Mount each device as the drawing and its instructions show, with one labeled cable to the head end, sized for the lock’s current and run. On a sensor-release exit door, the push-to-exit button’s own contact cuts the lock’s power, and a timer holds the release; the designer designs that circuit. Isolate the hub at its source, prove and land each cable, and leave the terminations for the site lead to inspect before the power returns.

Commissioning the door

Adopt the hub from a console that can reach it. A credential opens a door only through a policy that gives its person that door at that time. Record each test’s time, action, door behavior, and log entry as they were, then delete every record you created for the tests.

The hand-off

For each device, hand over its identity, location identifier, port or terminal, cable label, test evidence, and a settings backup or manual recovery record, with each value’s source and every open item. Check an AI draft cell by cell against the system, and keep every password, account name, and credential number out of the record and the prompt.
