EMT Pathway

A camera on a corridor wall needs a protected route for its cable. On this project that route is Electrical Metallic Tubing (EMT). This lesson covers working out the bend marks from the bender’s chart; cutting, reaming, and bending 3/4 in EMT; and the fittings, support, checks, frame label, and photographs of an installed camera pathway.

5 sections  ·  12 topics  ·  12 activities

By the end of Lesson 526.3 you will be able to
  • Calculate cut length, take-up, offset and saddle marks from the bender’s own chart.
  • Cut, ream and bend 3/4 in EMT.
  • Assemble a compression connector to the box and the EMT.
  • Install the camera pathway from the top of the wall to the camera box.
  • Check fit, support and bend geometry against the drawing.
  • Photograph the accepted pathway.

EMT is thin-wall metal tubing, usually steel. Fittings, not threads, join it to boxes and to other pieces of EMT.

EMT is sold in 10 ft lengths called sticks. Its sizes are trade sizes: 1/2 in, 3/4 in, 1 in, and up. A trade size is a name, not a measured diameter. The NEC does not permit EMT to be threaded. A connector joins the tubing to a box; a coupling joins two pieces end to end. Installers bend EMT cold, by hand in the small trade sizes. It holds the shape it is bent to.

A metal pathway protects a cable where it runs exposed on a wall. It also lets an installer pull the cable in after the pathway is accepted, or pull it out later to replace it.

The owner’s standard specifies 3/4 in EMT for the camera pathway on the mock-up (27 05 00 4.4). Clause 27 05 00 4.8 gives the route. Sheet TW-301, detail 3, of the drawing set shows it. The pathway has five parts. Open each card.

The owner’s standard makes the EMT pathway a hold point: the owner’s representative inspects it before the contractor pulls any cable into it (27 05 00 4.3).

Lesson 526.3 · Section 1 EMT and the Bender’s Chart

526.3.2 The hand bender and its chart

A hand bender is a curved shoe on a long handle. Its chart gives the figures that turn a dimension on a drawing into a mark on the tubing.

The shoe is the curved head of the bender. The tubing lies in a groove around its curved edge. The radius of that curve sets the radius of every bend the bender makes. A hook at the front of the shoe holds the tubing as the bend starts. A foot pedal at the back of the shoe, often called the heel, takes your foot pressure. The long handle multiplies the force of your pull. A shoe is made for one size of tubing: a 3/4 in EMT bender does not bend 1/2 in EMT.

The shoe carries two kinds of marks. Reference marks — an arrow, and often a star or a notch — are the points you line up with a mark on the tubing. The chart says which reference mark each kind of bend uses. Degree marks on the side of the shoe show the angle of the bend as you pull it, commonly at 10°, 22.5°, 30°, 45°, and 60°. The chart, or the bender’s instructions, says how to read them.

What the chart gives

The chart is printed on the bender or in its instructions. It gives three kinds of figure.

  • Take-up, for a 90° stub: the length to subtract from the stub height to place the mark.
  • Multiplier, for each offset angle: the distance between the two marks of an offset is the offset height times the multiplier. A three-point saddle has its own multiplier for the side marks.
  • Shrink, for each offset angle and for a saddle: the length the bends take out of the run, per inch of offset or saddle height.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 2 Bend Arithmetic

526.3.3 The stub and its take-up

A stub is a single 90° bend that turns the tubing up from the floor. Its mark goes at the stub height less the take-up, measured from the end.

The stub height is the distance from the floor to the end of the stub, with the long leg flat on the floor. A bend is an arc around the shoe, not a sharp corner. With the mark set at the bender’s stub reference mark, the finished stub stands taller than the mark’s distance from the end. The take-up is that extra height. It depends on the bender’s shoe.

The method

  1. Take the stub height from the drawing.
  2. Subtract the chart’s take-up.
  3. Measure the result from the end of the piece and mark it all around the tubing.
  4. Set the mark at the reference mark the chart names for a stub, with the end facing the way the chart shows.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 2 Bend Arithmetic

526.3.4 Offsets: multiplier and shrink

An offset moves a run sideways with two equal bends in opposite directions. The straight lengths before and after the bends end up parallel, a set distance apart.

The straights are the straight lengths of tubing before the first bend and after the second. The offset height is the distance between them, measured square to the run from one side of the first straight to the same side of the second. Both bends are made to the same angle.

Between the two bends, the tubing crosses the offset height at the bend angle. The length of tubing it takes to cross is the offset height divided by the sine of the angle. The multiplier is one divided by that sine, worked out in advance. At 30°, the sine is 0.5, so the multiplier is 2. A shallow angle needs a long length of tubing between the bends but gives the cable an easier pull. A steep angle fits a short space but makes the pull harder and the shrink larger.

Because the tubing between the bends runs on a diagonal, the run advances less than the length of tubing used. The difference is the shrink. The chart gives it per inch of offset height.

Every mark is measured from one end of the piece, the end where bending starts. Mark that end so that you do not confuse it with the other.

The method

  1. Take the offset height from the drawing. Take the angle from the drawing, or choose one.
  2. Place the first mark at the drawing’s distance to the first bend, measured from the end where bending starts.
  3. Place the second mark beyond the first. The distance between them is the offset height times the chart’s multiplier.
  4. Work out the shrink: the offset height times the chart’s shrink per inch.

Working with fractions

Heights and chart figures mix whole numbers, fractions, and decimals. Work every mark to 1/32 in. To multiply by a fraction, turn a mixed number into a fraction first: 3 1/2 × 3/8 in = 7/2 × 3/8 in = 21/16 in = 1 5/16 in. To add or subtract, bring the fractions to one denominator, borrowing a whole inch where needed: 26 1/4 in − 1 7/8 in = 25 10/8 in − 1 7/8 in = 24 3/8 in. To turn a decimal into thirty-seconds, multiply the decimal part by 32 and round: 3 1/2 × 1.4 = 4.9 in, and 0.9 × 32 = 28.8, which rounds to 29, so 4.9 in is 4 29/32 in.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 2 Bend Arithmetic

526.3.5 The three-point saddle

A three-point saddle carries a run over an obstruction, such as a pipe across its path, and returns it to its original line.

The saddle has a center bend of 45° over the obstruction and a 22.5° bend on each side of it, in the opposite direction. All three bends lie in one plane. The saddle height is the height of the obstruction: the distance the run has to rise to clear it.

The bends shorten the run, so a mark at the center of the obstruction would put the center bend short of it. The chart’s method adds the saddle’s shrink to the distance to the center of the obstruction. The side marks are then measured from the center mark, one on each side, at the saddle height times the side-mark multiplier. The chart names the reference mark for the center bend, which on many benders is not the arrow.

The method

  1. Measure the saddle height and the distance from the end where bending starts to the center of the obstruction.
  2. Work out the shrink: the saddle height times the chart’s shrink per inch.
  3. Place the center mark at the distance to the center of the obstruction plus the shrink.
  4. Place one side mark on each side of the center mark, at the saddle height times the side-mark multiplier.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 2 Bend Arithmetic

526.3.6 Cut length of a bent piece

A bent piece covers less distance along its run than its own length. Its cut length is the run it must reach plus the shrink of its bends.

The run length is the distance along the run, from the end where bending starts to the point the piece must reach. Measure every mark and the run length from that same end. A piece cut to the run length and then bent comes up short by the shrink.

Worked cut length: a piece carries the 45° offset, 5 in high, from the offset example and must reach 40 in along its run.

Where a drawing dimensions every part of a route along the tubing, the developed length gives the tubing it needs: the straights plus the length of tubing between each pair of bends, with each bend taken as a corner. Treat a saddle the same way: add the saddle’s shrink to the run length. Where a piece carries an offset and a saddle, add both shrinks.

Where one end of a pathway must finish on a fixed line, such as the top of the panel field, an installer often works out the cut length to know how long a piece to start with. The installer then bends the piece, fits it, and marks that end in place. Last, the installer cuts it at the mark and reams the cut end.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 3 Cutting, Reaming, and Bending

526.3.7 Cutting and reaming

A cut end must be square to the tubing and free of burrs before any cable is pulled past it.

Mark a cut line all around the tubing, so that it shows from every side. Clamp the tubing, or hold it flat on a bench, before you cut.

Tubing cutter

A sharp wheel and two rollers. Wear gloves to cut and to ream. Close the cutter on the mark, turn it around the tubing, and tighten it a little on each turn until the piece separates. The cut comes out square. The wheel rolls a burr, a thin raised edge of steel, into the bore, the inside of the tubing. Reaming removes it.

Hacksaw

A fine-tooth blade, with the tubing held in a clamp. Keep the blade square to the tubing; a hacksaw cut can wander off square. The cut leaves a ragged edge inside and outside the end.

Reaming removes the burr and the sharp edge from the inside of the cut end. A reamer is a cone of cutting edges turned in the end of the tubing. A fresh burr is sharp. Ream both ends of a piece until no burr shows when you look into the bore against the light.

The NEC requires every cut end of EMT to be reamed or otherwise finished to remove rough edges. A cable pulled into the tubing is drawn across the end. An edge left sharp can cut the cable’s jacket.

Check each cut end three ways. For squareness, lay a rule along the tubing and look at the cut in profile: a square cut stands at a right angle to the rule. For the cut face, look at the end: it is even all around, with no lip standing up from it. If a lip remains, ream the end again. For reaming, look into the bore against the light: no burr shows.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 3 Cutting, Reaming, and Bending

526.3.8 Making and checking the bends

A hand bender for 3/4 in EMT works on the floor: one foot holds the tubing down, the other presses on the heel, and both hands pull the handle.

A stub

Lay the piece on the floor and set the bender on it with the mark at the stub reference mark. Hold the tubing down with one foot and keep steady pressure on the heel with the other. Pull the handle in one smooth movement until the stub stands nearly upright. Steady foot pressure keeps the tubing seated in the groove of the shoe. Without it the tubing can flatten or kink.

EMT springs back a little when you release the handle. Lay the long leg flat on the floor and hold a level against the stub. If the stub falls short of plumb, set the bender back on it and pull a little further. Stop just short of plumb and check: a stub can be pulled further, but backing a bend off risks kinking it.

An offset

Make the first bend at the first mark, to the angle on the degree marks. Then rotate the piece half a turn, 180°, about its own length, and make the second bend at the second mark to the same angle. The half turn puts the second bend in the opposite direction and in the same plane as the first. The chart says which reference mark the second bend uses and which way the piece faces.

Two bends that are not in one plane form a dogleg. It shows when the piece is laid on the floor: one straight rests on the floor and the other lifts.

Checking a bent piece on the floor

  • Stub angle: with the long leg flat on the floor, the level held against the stub reads plumb.
  • Stub height: a rule held square to the floor beside the stub, read at the end of the stub.
  • One plane: with the offset laid on the floor, both straights rest on it.
  • Offset height: a level laid along one side of the first straight, and a rule held square to the level, read to the same side of the second straight.

The NEC requires bends in EMT to be made so that the tubing is not damaged; a bend must not effectively reduce the internal diameter of the tubing. The NEC also sets a minimum radius for a bend. A piece with a kinked or flattened bend goes to scrap.

A pull point is a place where an installer can reach the cable to pull it, such as a pull box or the open end of a conduit run. The owner’s standard and TIA-569 govern what counts as one. The NEC allows no more than 360° of bends between pull points, the equivalent of four 90° bends. For telecommunications conduit, TIA-569, which the owner’s standard names among its governing documents, sets a stricter limit: no more than two 90° bends, 180° in total, between pull points. The NEC figure is the minimum requirement; a pathway within 180° meets both, so the project works to 180°. Every bend counts toward the total, including the bends of an offset or a saddle: a 30° offset adds 60°. Count from one pull point to the next, or to the open end of the run.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 4 Installing and Accepting the Pathway

526.3.9 Connectors and couplings

A connector joins EMT to a box. The stop inside the connector sets how far the first bend mark lies from the box end of the EMT.

Assembling a compression connector

  1. Screw the threaded end of the connector into the box’s top hub and tighten it with pliers until it does not turn in the hub.
  2. Push the EMT into the connector until it meets the connector’s stop.
  3. Tighten the compression nut with pliers until the EMT does not turn in the connector.

Couplings

A set-screw coupling takes one piece of EMT in each end. Push each piece in to the stop at the center of the coupling, then tighten the set screw on each piece. A compression coupling grips each piece with a ring that a nut squeezes onto the tubing. Set-screw fittings are not raintight. The NEC requires a fitting in a wet location to be listed for wet locations. A raintight compression fitting is the usual choice. A run shorter than one stick needs no coupling. Where a run needs a coupling, the joint goes in a straight, clear of every bend.

The connector stop

The drawings on this project dimension the pathway from the end face of the box’s top hub. The drawn straight is the straight length the drawing gives from that face to the first bend. The EMT does not end at that face: it ends at the stop inside the connector. The distance from the box end of the EMT to the first bend mark therefore differs from the drawn straight by the position of the stop.

On this project, the connector’s product card gives the connector stop: the distance from the hub face to the end of a fully seated EMT, measured toward the box. The figure is positive where the EMT ends inside the hub, past its face, and negative where it ends outside the hub face. The distance from the box end of the EMT to the first bend mark is the drawn straight plus the connector stop. A negative stop makes that distance shorter than the drawn straight; a positive stop makes it longer.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 4 Installing and Accepting the Pathway

526.3.10 Support, fit, and geometry

An installed pathway sits where the drawing puts it, holds firm, and runs in one plane without being forced there.

Sheet TW-301, detail 3, shows how the camera pathway is built. Its position in each bay is on sheet TW-401 and in clause 27 05 00 4.8 of the owner’s standard.

Support

A one-hole strap holds the EMT to the panel with one screw. At the box, the top hub holds the EMT a set distance off the panel. A strap screwed down with nothing behind it pulls the EMT toward the panel, out of line with the hub, and springs the run. A spacer behind the strap holds the EMT at the same distance off the panel as at the box, so that the straights run parallel to the panel.

Clause 27 05 00 4.5 of the owner’s standard sets the fastening rules for the mock-up. Screws are pan-head or washer-head, short enough that the point stays clear of cable in the cavity behind the panel. No fastener goes into the aluminum frame. No cut or fastener falls within 2 in of a panel edge, a panel joint, or a panel screw. EMT and raceway may cross a panel joint or the top edge of the panel field where sheet TW-201 shows them; their strap and base screws stay 2 in clear. Clause 27 05 00 4.8 sets the strap at 2 in wide with one screw. Where a product on site differs, its own data governs. The contractor reports the difference by request for information.

In production work, the NEC requires EMT to be fastened within 3 ft of each box or other termination and at intervals of no more than 10 ft.

Fit and geometry, checked against the drawing

  • Fit: the connector does not turn in the hub; under a tight compression nut, the EMT neither turns nor pulls out.
  • Box: at its drawn position, with the center of its top hub on a plumb line drawn through that position.
  • Plumb: a level held against each vertical straight.
  • Offset: the distance between the two straights, measured square to them, against the drawn offset.
  • One plane: sighted down the run, both straights parallel to the panel, with no bow at the strap.
  • Top: the top end on the line the drawing gives, with its bushing fitted.

Lesson 526.3 · Section 4 Installing and Accepting the Pathway

526.3.11 Acceptance, the frame label, and the photographs

The owner’s representative accepts the pathway before cable goes in. The photographs record what was accepted. The frame label in them identifies the bay.

The hold point

Clause 27 05 00 4.3 of the owner’s standard sets three hold points for each bay of the mock-up: the marked layout, before any panel is cut; the EMT pathway, before any cable is pulled; and the completed bay, at handover. The contractor does not work past a hold point until the owner’s representative releases the bay.

The installer calls for pathway acceptance once the EMT is strapped and its bushing fitted. The owner’s representative checks the pathway against the drawing, then accepts it or holds it and gives the reason. The installer corrects a held pathway before doing any further work on it.

The frame label

A frame label, fixed to the frame above each bay, carries the bay identifier. The identifier names the wall, the face, and the side, as sheet TW-001 sets out: W4-1R is Wall 4, Face 1, right bay.

The photographs

Each photograph in an acceptance record shows one thing legibly:

  • the whole run from the box to the top of the panel field, with the frame label above the bay in view;
  • a level on the run, with the bubble readable, where the record states that a straight is plumb;
  • the plumb line drawn through the box position, in view above and below the box, where the record states that the box is plumb;
  • each reamed end close up, looking into the bore;
  • a rule beside a length, with the reading at the mark legible, where the record states a measured length.

526.3.12 What you covered

Measure every mark from the end where bending starts, and check every piece before it goes on the wall.

EMT is thin-wall metal tubing, usually steel, joined to boxes by connectors and to other pieces by couplings.
