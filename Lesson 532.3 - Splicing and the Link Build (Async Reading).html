How an installer prepares, cleaves, and fusion splices single-mode fiber, protects and stores each splice, and builds a backbone run from a rack enclosure to a wall box.

Kestrel Communications holds the telecommunications contract for the Harlow Street Operations Center, project HSOC-26-014. With the copper mock-up accepted, the owner, Calder Regional Services, wants the fiber backbone for Level 2: single-mode cable from the telecommunications room to two floor enclosures in each section of Level 2, spliced onto factory pigtails at both ends. D. Okafor briefs the teams: “Every fiber we light in this backbone ends in a splice at each end. The owner’s rep sees every splice screen before the lid opens. The loss test at the end of the job finds any bad splice the screen missed.”

Pigtails, splices, and the run

A pigtail is a short length of fiber with a factory-fitted connector at one end and bare fiber at the other. Each rack enclosure in the Mobile Infrastructure Rack (MIR) and each wall box arrives with a pigtail fitted behind each of its Subscriber Connector (SC) adapters. A run is one length of backbone cable from a rack enclosure to a wall box. A splice is a permanent joint between two fibers. The installer splices each cable fiber that will carry light to the pigtail behind its port. A run therefore has a splice at each end of every fiber in use.

Two ways to join fiber

In a fusion splice, the splicer melts the two fiber ends together in an electric arc. The result is one continuous path of glass. A mechanical splice holds two cleaved ends against each other in a precision alignment element, with an index-matching gel between them, and needs no power. Kestrel’s crews carry mechanical splices to restore a cut fiber until a fusion splicer can be brought to it. Clause F2 of the owner’s fiber addendum accepts only fusion splices in this backbone. No mechanical splice is left in the plant.

The evidence for a spliced run

Evidence for a splice comes from each stage of the job:

  • At the splicer: the splicer’s estimated loss and its image of the joint, which the installer judges against clause F2 before the lid opens.
  • After the splices at both ends are stored: a VFL, a handheld red laser source, sends visible light into one end. Its light must appear at the right port or coupler at the other end.
  • At acceptance: the insertion loss of each lit fiber, measured with a light source and a power meter at 1310 nm and 1550 nm, against the run’s loss budget, the most loss a fiber may show and still pass (clause F6).

The splicer’s estimate is a calculation from its image. It is never recorded as a measured loss.

532.3.2 Preparing fiber for a splice

A splice starts with two lengths of bare glass, each stripped to length, clean, and untouched since cleaning.

The layers you strip

A single-mode fiber carries light in its core, ~9 µm across. The cladding around it is glass of a lower refractive index, 125 µm across. Single-mode and multimode fiber share the 125 µm cladding, so a splicer’s V-grooves, the V-shaped channels that hold each bare fiber in line, take either. Outside the glass is a 250 µm acrylate coating. A tight-buffered fiber adds a 900 µm plastic buffer over the coating. In a cable, a layer of aramid yarn under the jacket carries any pull on the cable, so the fibers do not.

Stripping and cleaning

  1. Sleeve first. Slide the heat-shrink splice sleeve onto one of the two fibers and push it well back before you strip anything. Once the fibers are joined, the sleeve cannot go on.
  2. Strip. A three-hole stripper has one hole sized for each layer it removes. Strip the buffer, where the fiber has one, then the coating, each in its own hole, to the strip length on the splicer’s product card. Hold the stripper square to the fiber.
  3. Clean. Draw the bare glass once through a lint-free wipe wetted with isopropyl alcohol, from the coating toward the end. Let the alcohol dry. Keep the alcohol bottle closed and away from the splicer: the splicer’s arc can ignite alcohol vapor.
  4. Hold it clear. Hold a prepared fiber by its buffer or coating, never by the glass. Never set a stripped fiber down. If the glass touches anything, cut it back and prepare it again.
Glass offcuts

A fiber offcut is a sliver of glass that pierces skin and is hard to see or remove. Put every offcut into a sealed fiber trash container as soon as it leaves the stripper or the cleaver. Safety glasses stay on while anyone near you strips, cleaves, or splices. Take them off only at your team’s inspection station, to look through the inspection microscope.

532.3.3 The cleave and the cleave check

A cleave breaks the glass along a flat face at a right angle to the fiber’s axis. The splicer measures each end’s departure from that angle before it fuses the fibers.

How a cleaver works

A cleaver clamps the prepared fiber on each side of a blade. The blade scores the surface of the glass, and the cleaver then stresses the fiber, by tension or by a bend, so that the score runs across it as a crack. A good cleave leaves an end face that is flat, smooth, and square to the fiber’s axis. The cleave length is the distance from the edge of the coating to the end face. The installer sets it to the figure on the splicer’s product card by laying the fiber at that mark on the cleaver’s scale. A cleaver cuts prepared glass only. Cut a buffered fiber back with scissors, never with the cleaver.

The cleave angle

The cleave angle is how far an end face departs from a right angle to the fiber’s axis, in degrees. Once both fibers are loaded, the splicer images each end and measures its cleave angle. Two angled faces meet with a wedge-shaped gap between them. The splicer’s program sets a cleave-angle limit. The splicer accepts an end at or under the limit and rejects one over it. Cut a rejected end back, then strip, clean, and cleave it again.

Figure — Four cleaved ends, seen from the side
Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled Four cleaved ends, seen from the side. It has four panels, each showing the end of a fiber.

Good: flat, smooth, and at a right angle. Accepted. Angled: end face tilted from a right angle. Over the limit: rejected. Lip: a ridge of glass left at one edge. Can trap a bubble. Hackle: a chipped or broken end face. Prepare again.

A note reads: The splicer measures the angle; a lip or a hackle shows in its image of the end.

When cleaves keep failing

One rejected cleave is part of the work. A string of rejects from the same cleaver points at the cleaver or at the preparation:

  • debris on the cleaver’s clamp pads, or a blade worn at its current position;
  • coating residue left on the glass;
  • the fiber not lying straight in the cleaver’s guide, or set to the wrong cleave length.

Clean the cleaver’s pads as its manual sets out, and check the stripped glass for coating residue before you prepare again. Report a cleaver that keeps failing after cleaning: its blade may need turning to a new position.

Lesson 532.3 · Section 2 Fusion and Mechanical Splices

532.3.4 The fusion splice cycle

The splicer aligns the two fibers, fuses them in an arc, and calculates an estimated loss from its image of the joint.

Before the first splice

The splicer’s program sets the arc, and the limits it checks each splice against, for one type of fiber. Single-mode fiber is spliced on a single-mode program. Check the program, the sleeve heater’s setting for your sleeve length, and the battery or power supply before you prepare any fiber.

Loading the fibers

Lay each prepared fiber on its own side of the splicer, in the fiber holder for its coating diameter, with the bare glass in the V-groove and the end short of the electrodes, where the splicer’s manual shows. Neither end touches anything. Close the lid before you start the cycle. A splicer set to start on its own starts the cycle when the lid closes. The electrodes carry a high voltage during the arc: keep your fingers clear of them.

Two ways a splicer aligns

A cladding-alignment splicer lines up the outsides of the two claddings in fixed V-grooves. The cores line up only as well as each core is centered in its cladding. Dirt in a V-groove lifts a fiber out of line. A core-alignment splicer images the two cores and moves the fibers to line them up. Both types show the joint on screen and give an estimated loss. The splicer’s manual states which type it is.

The cycle

Figure — What the splicer does in one splice
Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled What the splicer does in one splice. It has three panels.

Panel 1, Image and align: two fiber ends face each other with a small gap, between an upper and a lower electrode. Cleave angles measured. Panel 2, Arc and fuse: an arc runs between the electrodes across the gap. Ends softened, pushed together. Panel 3, Image the joint: one continuous fiber, with a screen reading Est. loss.

Notes beneath read: The estimate is calculated from the splicer’s image of the joint. No light passes through the joint to produce it.

The splicer fires a short cleaning arc and sets a small gap between the ends. It then images each end, measures its cleave angle, and rejects the load if either end is over the limit. Next it fires the arc that fuses the fibers. The arc softens the glass, and the splicer pushes the two ends together into one fiber. Last, it images the joint, shows it on screen, and calculates the estimated loss from what the image shows, such as an offset between the two fibers.

What the estimate is, and is not

The estimate is the splicer’s calculation. The installer uses it to decide whether to keep the splice. It is not a measurement: no light from a source passes through the joint to a meter. The splicer can estimate a joint low while the joint still loses light for a reason the image does not show. A light source and a power meter measure the loss of the whole fiber, both splices and both connectors included: the loss the acceptance test counts. An Optical Time-Domain Reflectometer (OTDR) locates each splice or connector along the fiber and estimates its loss from one direction.

532.3.5 Accepting a splice

The installer decides whether to keep each splice at the splicer, before the lid opens, on the estimate and the joint image together.

The accept rule

A splice must meet both conditions of clause F2:

  • the splicer’s estimated loss is 0.30 decibels (dB) or less;
  • the splicer’s image of the joint shows no bubble, gap, or offset.

A splice that fails either condition is a re-do. A low estimate does not excuse a visible defect. Clause F6 allows the same 0.3 dB for each splice in the run’s loss budget.

What the splicer’s image of the joint can show, and the decision under clause F2.
Joint imageWhat it showsDecision
Clean jointOne straight fiber, the same width through the joint, no mark at the jointAccept if the estimate is 0.30 dB or less

GapThe joint is narrower than the fiber, or the ends have not closedRe-do

Making a re-do

Cut out the joint. The sleeve stays on the fiber it was slid onto. Prepare both ends again and splice again. Each re-do shortens the cable fiber and the pigtail it joins, so the slack left in the enclosure shrinks with every attempt.

The hold point and the record

A hold point is a step at which work stops until a named person has seen it. On this backbone, the installer shows each result screen to the owner’s representative before opening the lid. The installer then decides whether to keep the splice. Record every attempt, the re-dos included: the estimate, what the joint image shows, and the decision.

532.3.6 The mechanical splice

A mechanical splice holds two cleaved fibers end to end in an alignment element, with a gel between them, and needs no power.

Figure — A mechanical splice, cut away
Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled A mechanical splice, cut away. A long body holds a groove along its length. A fiber enters from each end, labeled Fiber in, and the two meet in the middle of the groove inside a drop of gel.

Labels: Alignment groove: holds both claddings in line. Index-matching gel where the ends meet. Body. Above the meeting point is a cap, labeled Cap: closed by the assembly tool.

How it holds the fibers

The splice element’s groove holds the two claddings in line. The index-matching gel has a refractive index close to that of the glass. It fills the space between the two end faces, so less light reflects or scatters where they meet. Closing the cap with the assembly tool grips both fibers in the element.

Making one

  1. Strip and clean each fiber to the lengths on the mechanical splice’s product card, not the splicer’s.
  2. Cleave each fiber. Nothing measures the cleave here: no screen shows the angle, and no estimate follows.
  3. Load the element in its assembly tool. Insert one fiber as far as the product card shows, then the other from the far end until the two ends meet.
  4. Close the cap with the tool.

Checking it with a VFL

A VFL shows whether light passes through the joint. Plug the fiber’s connector into the VFL, point the far end of the fiber at a card, say “light on,” and switch the VFL on. Then read two places:

  • The element. A bright red glow means light is leaving the fiber at the joint: the ends are angled, apart, or dirty. A faint glow can appear at a usable joint.
  • The card. A red spot from the far end shows that light crosses the joint and reaches the end of the fiber.

Switch the VFL off and say “light off.” The VFL measures no loss. A light source and a power meter measure the loss of the whole fiber, the joint included.

Lesson 532.3 · Section 3 Protecting and Storing the Splice

532.3.7 Sleeve and heater

A heat-shrink sleeve replaces the coating stripped for the splice and stiffens the joint.

What the sleeve is

A heat-shrink splice sleeve is an outer heat-shrink tube with a tube of hot-melt adhesive inside it and a strength rod, usually steel, beside the adhesive tube. When the sleeve is heated, the adhesive melts around the bare glass and the outer tube shrinks onto it. The sleeve’s length must match the heater’s setting and the slot in the tray. The manufacturer’s instructions govern both.

Sleeving a splice

  1. Open the lid and lift the spliced fiber out by its buffer, keeping it straight.
  2. Slide the sleeve along until the joint sits at its center. Both ends of the sleeve sit over coating or buffer, so no bare glass is left outside it.
  3. Lay the sleeved joint in the heater, straight, and run the heater cycle.
  4. When the cycle ends, lift the fiber out by the buffer and let the sleeve cool before you touch it or seat it. A warm sleeve is soft. If it bends while warm, it sets with the joint under strain.
  5. Check it: fully shrunk along its length, the fiber straight through it, and no bare glass beyond either end.

Lesson 532.3 · Section 3 Protecting and Storing the Splice

532.3.8 The splice tray

A splice tray holds each sleeve in its own slot and keeps the stored fiber in curves no tighter than the tray’s guides.

Each rack enclosure and each wall box comes with a splice tray: a shallow tray with a sleeve holder (a row of slots that grip the sleeves) and routing guides around its edge. The holder’s type and slot count vary by product; the manufacturer’s instructions govern. On this backbone each slot carries a tag with the rack port or coupler number its fiber serves. The strand record lists each fiber of a run with its color, its rack port, and its coupler.

Storing a splice

  • The slot. Seat each sleeve flat in the slot tagged for its port or coupler. A sleeve in another fiber’s slot leaves the tray and the strand record disagreeing.
  • The guides. Route both fibers of the splice, the cable fiber and the pigtail, along the guides. The guides keep every curve at or above the tray’s minimum bend radius.
  • The slack. Coil the spare fiber in even loops, all in one direction. The stored slack lets a later installer cut the splice out and make it again.
  • Clear of the lid. Keep every fiber off the sleeve holder and away from where the lid closes. A fiber across the holder is pinched when the lid comes down.
  • Other runs. Keep your fibers clear of every other run’s fibers. Move no other installer’s fiber or sleeve.

Fibers not spliced

A 12-fiber cable may have only two fibers spliced. The rest stay whole and protected: coiled in the storage area of the box or enclosure, clear of the tray lid and the cover, for later work. Close the tray lid, then the drawer or the cover, checking that no fiber is pinched at either.

532.3.9 Route and bend radius

A fiber cable must follow a wider bend while it is pulled than once it is installed.

The minimum bend radius

The bend radius of a cable is the radius of the curve the cable follows where it changes direction. A bend tighter than the cable’s minimum lets light escape from the fibers inside and can crack the glass. Clause F3 sets the rule for this backbone: no tighter than the cable manufacturer’s minimum bend radius, and in no case less than

  • 20 times the cable’s outside diameter while the cable is pulled;
  • 10 times its outside diameter once it is installed.

The pulling limit is the larger because a cable under tension presses against whatever it bends around, which loads the fibers inside the bend. Where the manufacturer’s data sheet gives a larger minimum, the data sheet governs.

The bend gauge

A bend gauge is a round object 20 times the cable’s outside diameter across. Its radius is half that, 10 times the outside diameter: the installed limit. A bend laid around the gauge, or in a wider curve, meets the installed limit. During the pull, keep every bend at least twice as wide as the gauge.

532.3.10 The cut length and the pull

The team plans each run’s cut length before the cable leaves the spool, then pulls the cable by hand along its route.

The parts of a cut length

A fiber run is cut once, at the spool. The cut length covers the route and everything stored or worked at each end:

  • an allowance at each end: cable inside the enclosure or box, from its cable entry to the end the installer prepares and splices;
  • the drop at the rack: from the cable ladder down to the enclosure’s cable entry;
  • a service loop at each end: spare cable coiled and tied on the cable ladder, so that an end can be prepared again, or an enclosure moved, without a new cable;
  • the route: along the cable ladder from the drop to the point where the run leaves the ladder;
  • the drop at the far end: from the ladder down to the far cable entry.

Add the parts and round the total up to the next whole foot. A run cut long leaves a little more slack. A run cut short cannot be lengthened without a splice in the middle of it.

Footage marks

Cable jackets carry footage marks, printed lengths at regular intervals, in feet or in meters, counting up or down along the spool. Read the mark nearest the free end before the pull. Pay out until the marks show the cut length from that reading: add the cut length where the marks count up, subtract it where they count down. Where the marks are in meters, convert the cut length first: feet × 0.3048 = meters.

Pulling by hand

  • Mark the free end with the run identifier on tape before it leaves the spool.
  • Pull the cable, never a fiber. Pull steadily, without jerks, and keep every bend within the pulling limit: at each change of direction, guide the cable around in a wide curve.
  • Keep the cable off the floor and out of walkways.
  • Coil and tie each service loop no tighter than the bend gauge. Cut the cable square at the spool only once the far end is in place.
  • Do not stand on a chair, a table, or a tool chest to reach a cable ladder. Use a step ladder, with a second person at its foot.

532.3.11 The wall box and the rack end

At each end of a run, the cable’s aramid yarn is anchored where the cable enters. Only the buffered fibers go on to the tray.

Mounting the wall box

The drawing set shows a fiber wall box zone on each Face 2 bay. The work order gives the box’s position inside that zone, measured from the bay’s left edge and from the bottom of the lower panel. Measure the position, hold the box there level, and mark its mounting holes and its cable entry hole. The cable entry hole sits behind the box’s own cable entry, so the cable passes from the wall’s cavity straight into the box. Fit a liner in the drilled hole so that the panel’s raw edge cannot damage the jacket. Fix the box so that it does not move when pressed.

Preparing an end

  1. With a jacket ringer, ring the jacket at the allowance measured from the cable end, then slide the jacket off. Cut the jacket only.
  2. Keep the aramid yarn. Secure the cable and its yarn at the enclosure’s strain-relief point, just inside its cable entry. On a wall box with only a tie-down point, use that point, as the box’s instructions show. A pull on the cable then reaches the enclosure through the yarn and never reaches the fibers. Cut off the yarn beyond that point with Kevlar cutters: aramid yarn dulls ordinary scissors.
  3. Inside the enclosure, route only the buffered fibers. Separate the fibers to be spliced. Coil the others in the storage area, clear of the cover.
  4. Uncoil the pigtails to be spliced. Keep every connector’s dust cap on.

The strand record

The strand record gives each fiber’s color, rack port, and coupler. Fiber colors follow the 12-color sequence of ANSI/TIA-598: fiber 1 blue, fiber 2 orange, then green, brown, slate, white, red, black, yellow, violet, rose, and aqua. The owner’s addendum assigns the ports and couplers. The installer copies them into the record before the cable is cut. Every splice follows the record.

The rack end

At the MIR, lay the drop from the cable ladder to the enclosure along the route posted for it and tie it at the posted points. Several teams share one enclosure. Work only on your own run’s cable and fibers, and leave the other teams’ fibers where they lie.

Lesson 532.3 · Section 4 Building the Run

532.3.12 Continuity and identity, both ways

Once both ends are spliced and stored, a red light sent into one end of a fiber must appear at that fiber’s own port or coupler at the other end.

What the check proves

Red light at the far end shows continuity: the fiber is whole from end to end, through both splices. Red light at the port or coupler the strand record gives shows identity: the fiber lands where the record says it does. The check runs in each direction, so each installer has evidence from their own end: the light arriving at their own port or coupler.

Running it

  1. Each installer inspects and cleans the end faces of a test cord at the inspection microscope, and cleans the port or coupler with a One-Click cleaner before mating into it (clause F8).
  2. The installer at one end plugs the cord into the fiber’s port and connects the VFL, says “light on,” and switches it on.
  3. The installer at the other end plugs a cord into the coupler the record gives and points its free end at a card. A red spot on the card shows that the light has arrived.
  4. The installer with the VFL switches it off and says “light off.” The two installers swap roles and repeat, then move to the next fiber.

Never look into a VFL, a connector, or a cord end to see whether it is lit. Read the card.

What a VFL can and cannot show

Red light escapes wherever a fiber is broken, bent too tightly, or poorly spliced. It shows as a glow through a light-colored buffer or through some jackets. It does not show through a black jacket. A VFL shows that light reaches a point and where it escapes on the way. It measures no loss: the loss of the fiber takes a light source and a power meter.

Lesson 532.3 · Section 5 Wrapping Up

532.3.13 What you covered

An installer prepares and splices each fiber, protects and stores each splice, and builds the run between its two enclosures within the owner’s limits.

Preparing fiber for a splice

The sleeve goes on before stripping. Strip the buffer and the coating to the product card’s lengths, clean the glass once with alcohol, and let nothing touch it. The splicer measures each end’s cleave angle and rejects one over its program’s limit. A string of rejects points at the cleaver or the preparation.

Fusion and mechanical splices

The splicer aligns the fibers, fuses them in an arc, images the joint, then calculates an estimated loss. The estimate is never a measured loss. Clause F2 accepts a splice at 0.30 dB or less with a joint image showing no bubble, gap, or offset; otherwise it is a re-do. A mechanical splice aligns two cleaved ends in gel and is checked with a VFL: a bright glow at the element means light is lost at the joint, and a spot at the far end means light crosses it. No mechanical splice stays in this plant.
