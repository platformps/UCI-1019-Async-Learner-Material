A fiber link carries information as pulses of light through a strand of glass 125 micrometers (µm) across.

D. Okafor briefs the teams before the first kit is opened: “Fiber does not shock anyone. It cuts, it hides in skin, and the light in it can hurt an eye that never saw it. Set up the bench before you touch the glass.”

How a fiber link works

At one end of a link, a transmitter turns an electrical signal into pulses of light from a laser or a light-emitting diode. The fiber guides the light to a receiver, which turns it back into an electrical signal. Most equipment combines a transmitter and a receiver in one module, a transceiver. A link between two transceivers of the kind used on this job uses two fibers, one for each direction.

The glass carries no current, so a fiber link is immune to electrical interference along its route. The light used for data is infrared, at wavelengths of 850, 1300, 1310, or 1550 nanometers (nm). The eye sees little or none of it: 850 nm is near-infrared and hard to see; the longer wavelengths are invisible.

532.1.2 Glass fragments

A fiber offcut is a sliver of glass sharp enough to pierce skin and small enough to lose on a bench.

Stripping exposes bare glass, and cleaving and scribing leave offcuts. Each offcut is clear and about 125 µm across. In a fingertip it is hard to see and hard to remove. On a sleeve or a mat it can injure someone long after the cut that made it.

The controls at the table

  • Safety glasses. Glasses go on whenever anyone at your table strips, cleaves, scribes, polishes, or splices. While that work goes on, you take them off only at your team’s inspection station: a surface, named by the site lead, away from that work. Where the site lead sets the station at the end of the table instead, all cutting at that table stops while anyone there has glasses off. Where the microscope is a video probe with a screen rather than an eyepiece, glasses stay on at the station too.
  • A black mat. A clear fragment shows against a dark, smooth surface. Work over the mat, not over the edge of the table.
  • A sealed trash container. Every offcut goes into your container as soon as it comes off, and nowhere else. Close the lid when you leave your seat. When your container is full, tape its lid shut and hand it to the site lead. A wipe used on bare glass can carry glass, so it goes in the sealed container too. A wipe or cleaning stick used only on end faces goes in the room’s waste bin.
  • Hands off loose glass. Pick up a loose fragment with tweezers. Never brush a mat with a hand or a sleeve, and never blow debris off it.
  • Nothing to eat or drink. No food, drink, or chewing gum at the table. A fragment on a cup or on food can be swallowed.

Opening and closing the bench

Each installer starts the day by setting up a work position that meets these conditions:

  • the mat squared to the table edge, with the trash container open beside it on the side of the working hand;
  • glasses on, and no food or drink at the seat;
  • the team’s alcohol and wipes capped in the middle of the table, clear of every edge;
  • light falling on the work, not from behind it.

The team counts its shared kit against its issue list, and each installer counts their personal items. A count and check at the start and at the close finds a missing blade or a damaged microscope before the next person relies on it.

At the close of the day, the team puts any loose offcuts into the containers, runs a lint roller over each mat, and vacuums the floor around the table. The stick vacuum takes dry debris only.

A fragment in the eye

Do not rub the eye, and do not try to remove the fragment. Go to the eye wash and tell the site lead at once. A suspected fragment in an eye needs medical evaluation.

532.1.3 Light you cannot see

The light in a working fiber link is infrared, so a fiber that looks dark can still be carrying it.

Transmitters and test sources launch infrared light. A laser beam that enters the eye can damage it. Infrared light gives little or no warning, because the eye sees it faintly or not at all. An eyepiece microscope on a lit connector gathers the light and sends it into the eye. The bright red light of a visual fault locator (VFL) can harm the eye too. Safety glasses are made to stop fragments; they are not a control for light.

The looking rule

Clause F7 of the owner’s fiber addendum and the bench rules apply to every fiber task:

  • Never look into a connector, an adapter, or an open port that may carry light, with a microscope or without one.
  • Inspect an end face only on a connector that has no source connected to it.
  • Before you inspect a connector on a link that has been in service, confirm with a power meter that the fiber is dark.
  • To see whether a connector is lit by a VFL, look at it from the side, never into its end.

A power meter detects light only within its detector’s range, and reads it accurately only at the wavelength it is set to. Where you cannot tell what equipment a fiber connects to, ask the site lead before you inspect it.

Light on, light off

Before you connect a light source, a transceiver, or a VFL to a fiber, say “light on” to everyone working on that fiber or at your table. When you disconnect it, say “light off.” The calls warn the people at the far end and across the table. They do not make a fiber safe to look into.

532.1.4 Cleaning chemicals and adhesives

Each chemical at a fiber bench is controlled by its label and its Safety Data Sheet (SDS).

Isopropyl alcohol cleans bare glass and connector end faces. An anaerobic adhesive bonds a fiber into a connector. A primer applied to the glass starts the adhesive’s cure. The alcohol and the primer are flammable. All three can irritate the eyes. The adhesive can also cause an allergic skin reaction.

The controls

  • Read the SDS. The SDS for each product sets its protective equipment, ventilation, storage, first aid, spill response, and disposal. Where a course document and the SDS differ, the SDS governs.
  • Capped until use. Keep the alcohol in its dispensing bottle and the adhesive and primer capped, in the middle of the table and clear of every edge. Use only the amount the task needs.
  • No ignition source. Keep the room ventilated and any flame away. The electric arc of a fusion splicer is an ignition source: keep open alcohol and primer away from a running splicer.
  • Point the primer away. Aim the primer at the fiber, away from yourself and the people beside you.
  • Labeled containers only. An unlabeled container carries no hazard information and no first-aid instructions. Keep each chemical in its labeled container.
  • Nothing wet near the vacuum. Clean up a spill as the product’s SDS directs, and tell the site lead. The stick vacuum takes dry debris only.

Lesson 532.1 · Section 2 Fiber and Its Grades

532.1.5 Core, cladding, and coating

Every fiber is a glass core inside a glass cladding, protected by layers that carry no light.

The core carries the light. The cladding around it is glass with a slightly lower refractive index. Light that meets the boundary between the two at a shallow angle reflects back into the core. This effect, total internal reflection, keeps the light in the core along the length of the fiber, through gentle curves as well as straight runs.

The fibers used in premises and backbone cabling, single-mode and multimode, have a cladding 125 µm in diameter. Every grade therefore fits a ferrule bored for 125 µm glass, though single-mode connectors are made to tighter tolerances: use the connector rated for the fiber. The same cleavers and splicer holders take fiber of every grade. The grades differ in the core.

Layers outside the glass protect it. The maker applies a 250 µm coating when the fiber is drawn. A tight-buffered fiber carries a 900 µm buffer over its coating. Aramid yarn, often called by the trade name Kevlar, takes the tension when a cable is pulled. The jacket outside everything carries the maker’s printed markings, the legend.

532.1.6 Single-mode and multimode

The size of the core decides how light travels in a fiber, how far a link reaches, and which equipment the fiber serves.

A multimode fiber has a core of 50 or 62.5 µm. Light travels down it along many paths, or modes, which arrive at the far end at slightly different times. The pulse spreads as a result. That spreading, modal dispersion, limits how fast a multimode link can signal over a given distance. Multimode fiber serves links inside buildings and data centers, with sources at 850 nm and 1300 nm.

A single-mode fiber has a core of about 9 µm, which carries light along one path. With no modal dispersion, single-mode links reach far greater distances, with lasers at 1310 nm and 1550 nm. The owner’s backbone is single-mode (addendum F1).

The grades

The OS (optical single-mode) and OM (optical multimode) designations name standard grades of single-mode and multimode fiber. A higher number does not make one grade a substitute for another with a different core: the core sizes must match end to end.

Fiber grades, their core and cladding diameters in micrometers, and what sets each apart.
GradeCore/claddingWhat sets it apart
OS29/125Single-mode, for long links at 1310 and 1550 nm
OM162.5/125Multimode; the only grade with a 62.5 µm core
OM250/125Multimode, not optimized for laser sources

OM450/125Multimode, optimized for laser sources, with more bandwidth than OM3

Light passing from a 62.5 µm core into a 50 µm core loses the part that does not fit. Light leaving a multimode core loses most of its power entering a single-mode core. Match a patch cord or a test cord to the grade of the fiber it connects to.

Jacket color is a convention

Indoor cable and patch cords commonly follow a color convention: yellow for single-mode, orange for OM1 and OM2, and aqua for OM3 and OM4. Outdoor cable is usually black whatever fiber it carries. A maker may choose another color. Read the jacket color as a clue to confirm against the legend, not as evidence of the fiber type.

532.1.7 Loss, wavelength, and bends

Light loses power along the fiber itself and at every joint and bend in its path.

Optical loss is stated in decibels (dB), a scale of power ratios: a loss of 3 dB leaves about half the light, and a loss of 10 dB a tenth. The loss of a fiber along its length, its attenuation, comes from scattering and absorption in the glass and is stated in decibels per kilometer (dB/km). Multimode fiber loses more per kilometer than single-mode fiber. In both, a longer wavelength loses less in a straight run: 1300 nm less than 850 nm, and 1550 nm less than 1310 nm.

Every connector pair and every splice adds loss of its own. Dirt or damage on a connector end face adds loss. It also reflects part of the light back toward the source, which can disturb the laser that sent it.

Macrobends and microbends

A macrobend is a curve tighter than the fiber or cable is made to take. At the bend, some light meets the core boundary too steeply to reflect and escapes into the cladding. A microbend is a small, local distortion of the fiber caused by pressure: a tie pulled tight, a crushed jacket, or a fiber pinched in a tray. Both add loss, and the loss is greater at longer wavelengths: a bend shows more loss at 1550 nm than at 1310 nm.

The cable maker’s data sheet gives the minimum bend radius for each cable, during the pull and once installed. Clause F3 of the owner’s fiber addendum sets the limits for the backbone.

532.1.8 The jacket legend

The legend printed along a cable’s jacket is the first evidence of what the cable is.

A cable maker prints a legend along the jacket and repeats it at intervals. A legend normally gives the maker, the fiber count, the fiber type, and the fire rating, in no fixed order. Most also carry a length mark, a running count of feet or meters. The difference between the marks at two points is the length between them.

Fiber type and grade

A legend names the fiber type in words or abbreviations, such as SINGLEMODE, SM, MULTIMODE, or MM. Many add the grade (such as OS2 or OM3), the core and cladding size (such as 9/125 or 62.5/125), or both. A 9/125 size identifies single-mode fiber, and 62.5/125 identifies OM1. OM2, OM3, and OM4 share a 50/125 core, so only a printed grade tells them apart.

Fire ratings

The 2023 edition of the National Electrical Code (NEC) sets listing types for indoor optical fiber cable by where the cable may run. The legend gives the listing type. The letter N means nonconductive: the cable holds no metal. The letter C means conductive: it holds metal, such as armor or a metal strength member, though it carries no current.

The construction under the jacket decides how the fibers are protected and how the cable is prepared at each end.

532.1.10 Inside plant and outside plant

Inside Plant (ISP) cable is built for buildings. Outside Plant (OSP) cable is built for weather, water, and the ground.

Inside a building, the concern is fire. ISP cable is usually tight-buffered, with an NEC fire rating printed on its jacket. Outside, the hazards are water, sunlight, frost, and crushing. OSP cable keeps water out of its fibers with gel or dry water-blocking tape and yarn. Its polyethylene jacket, usually black, resists sunlight. Many OSP cables add armor.

The marks of outside plant cable

  • Gel, water-blocking tape, or water-swellable yarn at the cut-back
  • Loose tubes rather than tight-buffered fibers
  • Armor under the jacket
  • An outdoor or outside-plant marking in the legend, or a direct-burial rating

Weigh the marks together. Jacket color alone is not a mark of outside plant. Some cables, rated indoor/outdoor, carry both a water-blocked construction and an NEC fire rating.

Where outside plant cable may enter a building

Most OSP cable carries no NEC listing. Section 770.48 of the 2023 NEC limits unlisted outside plant cable inside a building. In general, it may run no more than 15 m (50 ft) from its point of entrance, and it must end in an enclosure. Only nonconductive cable may run farther, in one of the raceways that section names. Conductive cable, such as armored cable, has no such exception. Unlisted cable may not run in a riser or an air-handling space. From that enclosure, commonly a splice enclosure near the entrance, listed ISP cable continues the run.

532.1.11 The fiber color code

Inside a cable, each fiber is numbered by the color of its coating or buffer, in a fixed order.

ANSI/TIA-598 sets the color sequence for fibers 1 to 12. A fiber’s number comes from its color, not from where it lies in the cable: the blue fiber is fiber 1 wherever it sits at the cut-back. In a loose-tube cable the tubes follow the same sequence, so the first tube is blue, the second orange, and so on. Above 12 fibers, many cables repeat the 12 colors with a stripe or a dash added. The maker’s data sheet gives the scheme for a particular cable.

532.1.12 SC, ST, and LC

A connector’s body and coupling identify its type. Its ferrule holds the fiber on center.

Every fiber connector holds the fiber in a ferrule, commonly a ceramic cylinder bored to fit 125 µm glass. Its polished front is the end face. When two connectors meet in an adapter, a sleeve inside the adapter lines the two ferrules up so that the two cores meet. The body around the ferrule couples the connector to the adapter. A boot at the back protects the fiber where it leaves the connector.

The three common connector types, how each couples, its ferrule diameter, and where it is commonly found.
TypeBody and couplingFerruleCommon applications

LC, commonly expanded as Lucent ConnectorSmall square body, about half the size of an SC, with a latch tab1.25 mmTransceivers in switches, and high-density panels

A simplex connector holds one fiber. A duplex connector is two connectors clipped together for the two fibers of a link, most often SC or LC. A hybrid patch cord carries a different connector type at each end, such as LC at one end and SC at the other.

SC and ST share a 2.5 mm ferrule, so their bodies and couplings tell them apart. SC and LC both push in and latch, so their size tells them apart. A connector type says nothing about the fiber inside it: an SC body can terminate single-mode or multimode fiber.

532.1.13 UPC and APC

The polish on a connector’s end face decides how much light it reflects back toward the source.

In a physical contact connector, springs press two slightly domed end faces together so that the cores touch, with no air gap. An Ultra Physical Contact (UPC) end face is domed and square to the fiber: some of the light that reaches it reflects straight back along the core. An Angled Physical Contact (APC) end face is polished at an angle, commonly 8 degrees. The light it reflects leaves the core at an angle and is lost in the cladding. APC connectors serve systems sensitive to reflected light, such as video carried over fiber and passive optical networks.

Telling them apart

By convention, APC connectors and adapters are green. Single-mode UPC connectors are commonly blue, and multimode connectors beige, black, or aqua. Many connectors also print “APC” or “UPC” on the body or the boot. The color is a convention; the print is evidence. Where the two disagree, or neither is present, hold the connector back and ask the site lead.

Never mate APC with UPC

An angled end face against a domed one leaves an air gap at the cores. The joint loses far more light than a matched pair, reflects more of it, and can damage both end faces. The owner’s backbone is SC/UPC throughout, and no APC connector or adapter belongs in it (addendum F1).

532.1.14 Transceivers

A transceiver’s label states the wavelength it sends and the fiber it is made for.

A Small Form-factor Pluggable (SFP) transceiver plugs into a port on a switch. On most SFPs, the optical end takes a duplex LC connector: one port transmits and the other receives, often marked with arrows. Two modules of identical shape can serve different fibers at different wavelengths, so the label, not the shape, identifies the module. The Ethernet standard of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), IEEE 802.3, names each type by its speed and its optics.

Lesson 532.1 · Section 5 Inspecting and Cleaning an End Face

532.1.15 The end face and its zones

Contamination on an end face adds loss and reflection at a connection. Only a microscope shows it.

A single-mode core is about 9 µm across, small enough for a speck of dust or a smear of skin oil to cover part of it. Mating presses contamination between the two end faces, where it can transfer from one to the other. A hard particle can scratch or pit the glass. Contamination can be cleaned off. Damage cannot: a scratch, a pit, or a chip stays however often the end face is cleaned.

The inspection zones

IEC 61300-3-35, a standard of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), sets how end faces are inspected. It divides an end face into zones, each a circle or ring centered on the fiber and sized by its diameter:

  • Zone A, the core zone: 0–25 µm across on single-mode fiber, 0–65 µm on multimode. It takes in the core, which carries the light.
  • Zone B, the cladding zone: from Zone A out to ~115 µm across, ~110 µm in the 2022 edition.
  • Zone C, the adhesive zone: the ring outside the cladding zone, where the glass is bonded into the ferrule.
  • Zone D, the contact zone: out to ~250 µm across, where the ferrule meets the mating ferrule. The rest of the ferrule face lies outside the zones.

The 2022 edition of the standard grades Zones A and B, the core zone and the cladding zone, by counting and sizing scratches and defects. It sets no defect limits for Zones C and D. Mating can push loose contamination there across the end face into the core.

Clause F8 of the owner’s fiber addendum requires the installer to inspect and clean every end face before every mating, and to judge it by these zones. On the mock-up, an end face is clean when nothing is visible in Zones A or B. A decision states what you saw, the zone it sat in, and what you compared the end face against.

Lesson 532.1 · Section 5 Inspecting and Cleaning an End Face

532.1.16 Inspect, clean, inspect

Inspect a connector before you clean it, clean it only as far as it needs, and inspect it again before you mate it.

The sequence

  1. Confirm that no source is connected to the connector, by the looking rule.
  2. Inspect. Seat the connector in the microscope, bring the end face into focus, and find the core and the cladding. Where Zones A and B are already clear, the connector is ready to mate without cleaning.
  3. Clean dry. A click cleaner, such as the One-Click, carries a dry cleaning tape that wipes the end face each time you push the cleaner until it clicks. A 2.5 mm cleaner fits 2.5 mm ferrules, such as SC and ST; an LC ferrule takes a 1.25 mm cleaner. A click cleaner can also reach an end face seated inside an adapter.
  4. Inspect again. A mark that has moved or gone was contamination. Where a mark remains in Zone A or B, clean dry a second time and inspect again. A mark unchanged after the second dry clean may need a wet clean, or may be damage.
  5. Clean wet, then dry, where dry cleaning has not cleared Zones A and B. Wet a fresh lint-free wipe with isopropyl alcohol: the wipe, not the connector. Draw the ferrule across the wet part of the wipe, then across a dry part of the same wipe. The dry pass removes the alcohol before it dries on the end face and leaves a residue. Skin oil, from a fingertip, seldom comes off dry and usually needs this step.
  6. Inspect again, then decide. Mate the connector or cap it. Where the end face still does not clear after the attempts the site lead allows, the end face may be scratched or pitted: hand the connector to the site lead and use another.

A wet ferrule wets the tape of a click cleaner, which then carries alcohol onto the next end face it cleans. Make the dry pass on the wipe, not with the click cleaner.

Both sides of every connection

Mating joins two end faces, so inspect both: the connector in your hand and the one it will meet. Clean an adapter or an equipment port only with a tool made for it. A cap that has touched a dirty surface can put contamination back on a clean end face.

Lesson 532.1 · Section 6 Wrapping Up

532.1.17 What you covered

A fiber installer works at a controlled bench, identifies each component from evidence, and mates a connector only after inspecting it and finding it clean.

Working safely with fiber

Glasses go on whenever anyone at the table strips, cleaves, scribes, polishes, or splices, and come off only at the team’s inspection station. Offcuts go straight into the sealed container. Never look into a connector, an adapter, or an open port that may carry light. Inspect only a connector with no source connected, after a power-meter check where the fiber has been in service. Call “light on” and “light off.” The Safety Data Sheet governs each chemical.

Fiber and its grades

The cladding is 125 µm on every grade; the grades differ in the core. Single-mode OS2 has a ~9 µm core and runs at 1310 and 1550 nm. OM1 has a 62.5 µm core, OM2 to OM4 a 50 µm core, and multimode runs at 850 and 1300 nm. Bends cause more loss at longer wavelengths.
