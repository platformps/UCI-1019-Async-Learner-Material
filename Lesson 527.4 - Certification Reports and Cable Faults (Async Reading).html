Lesson 527.4 - Certification Reports and Cable Faults

What a wiremap test proves about a copper link, how to read a certification report, and how to find and repair a fault that the wiremap test reports.

Kestrel Communications holds the telecommunications contract for the Harlow Street Operations Center, project HSOC-26-014. Its installation mock-up of the typical Level 2 work area stands on five training walls, each laid out in bays. The three data outlets in each bay are cabled to a team patch panel in the Mobile Infrastructure Rack (MIR). Before the owner, Calder Regional Services, accepts the mock-up as the standard for Level 2, the owner’s representative walks the cabling.

The owner’s cabling addendum sets two tests. Each team tests every link, from outlet jack to patch panel port, for wiremap and length with its own tester. At closeout, Kestrel’s lead technician tests every link again with a certification tester and sends the reports to the owner. A link can pass the first test and fail the second. Where a wiremap test fails, the installer finds the fault, repairs the termination, and proves the repair with the same test.

527.4.1 What a wiremap test proves

A wiremap tester shows whether a link is wired correctly. A certification tester measures how the link carries a signal.

A wiremap and length test shows where each conductor of a link goes, and how long the link is.

A permanent link is the installed cable from the outlet jack to the patch panel port, with the termination at each end. Patch cords are not part of it (owner’s cabling addendum, Terms). On the mock-up, each permanent link runs from a data outlet in a bay to a port on the team’s patch panel in the Mobile Infrastructure Rack (MIR). On this page, “link” means a permanent link.

The tester

A wiremap tester has two parts. The main unit runs the test and shows the result. The remote unit plugs into the other end of the link. Through it, the main unit tells which pin each conductor reaches. Each unit connects to the link through a short patch cord: one at the patch panel port, one at the outlet.

What the test checks

  • Continuity. Each of the eight conductors is connected from end to end.
  • Pin mapping. Each conductor reaches the same pin at the far end: pin 1 to pin 1, and so on to pin 8.
  • Shorts. No two conductors are connected to each other.
  • Length. The tester times a pulse along a pair and converts the time to a length with the Nominal Velocity of Propagation (NVP) set for the cable. NVP is the speed of a signal in the cable, as a percentage of the speed of light. A wrong NVP setting gives a wrong length.

The owner’s cabling addendum sets the pass (A7): all eight conductors continuous, each reaching the same pin at the far end and connected to no other conductor, with a length not over 295 ft (90 m). Each team runs this test on every link. It does not measure how the link carries a signal at the frequencies a network uses. A certification tester measures that.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 1 Three Kinds of Test

527.4.2 Verification, qualification, and certification

Verification, qualification, and certification each answer a different question about a link, with a different instrument.

The three kinds of test, the question each answers, and what its instrument does.
TestThe question it answersWhat the instrument does
VerificationIs the link wired correctly?A wiremap tester checks continuity, pin mapping, shorts, and length.
QualificationCan the link carry one named network application?A qualification tester measures the link against what that application needs, and reports whether the link supports it.
CertificationDoes the link meet every limit its cabling standard sets for its category?A certification tester measures each parameter the standard sets, across the category’s frequency range, and reports a pass or a fail for each. For a Category 5e permanent link, the limits are those of ANSI/TIA-568, from 1 to 100 MHz.

A qualification pass covers the application it names, not another one. A certification report names the standard, the category, and the test limit it applied.

The owner’s cabling addendum asks for two of the three. Under A7, each team verifies every link with its own wiremap tester as the link is finished. Under A8, the lead technician certifies every link at closeout and submits the reports to the owner. The owner accepts the links on the certification reports.

527.4.3 The parts of a certification report

A certification report records one link: where the tester was connected, what it measured, and whether each result met its limit.

A certification tester also has a main unit and a remote unit. Its report for one link has the parts below.

The parts of a certification report and what each one gives.
PartWhat it gives
HeaderThe link; the test limit, such as a Category 5e permanent link; the cable type; the NVP set in the tester; where the main unit and the remote unit were connected; and the overall result
WiremapFor each pin at the main unit, the pin it reaches at the remote unit, then the wiremap result
Length and delayFor each pair, its length, its propagation delay (the time a signal takes to travel the pair), and its delay skew (the difference in delay between that pair and the fastest pair); then each against its limit
Transmission resultsOne row for each parameter, at each end where the tester measures it: the pair or pairs, the frequency, the measured value, the limit, the margin, and the result
DiagnosticsWhere the tester finds the largest disturbance on the link, as a distance from the main unit, when it reports one

Permanent link and channel

ANSI/TIA-568 gives one set of limits for a permanent link and another for a channel. A channel includes the patch cords at both ends; a permanent link does not. When the tester applies the permanent link limit, the result leaves the patch cords out.

The two ends

The header states where each unit was connected, for example the main unit at the patch panel port and the remote unit at the outlet jack. Every “main unit” and “remote unit” in the report’s rows refers to those places. Every diagnostic distance is measured from the main unit.

The overall result

The overall result is PASS only when every result on the report passes; otherwise it is FAIL.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 2 Reading a Certification Report

527.4.4 What each result measures

Each result on a report measures one way a link loses, leaks, or reflects a signal. Most of them point to a part of the installation.

Length, propagation delay, and delay skew

The tester measures the delay of each pair and converts it to a length at the NVP. The four pairs differ slightly in length, because each pair is twisted at its own rate. Delay skew is the difference in delay between a pair and the fastest pair. A route longer than the limit allows, or excess cable coiled in the run, makes the link fail its length limit.

Insertion loss

Insertion loss is the signal lost along the link. It rises with length and with frequency. A long link, or a damaged or substandard cable, raises it.

Crosstalk

A signal on one pair couples into the pairs beside it. Twisting each pair keeps that coupling small, because the coupling from each twist largely cancels the next. Where the installer untwists a pair, or strips the jacket back, further than needed at a termination, coupling rises at that end. A jack or patch panel port not rated for the cable’s category does the same.

  • Near-End Crosstalk (NEXT) is signal coupled from one pair into another, measured at the end where the signal enters. The tester measures it at each end, because each end has its own termination.
  • Power Sum Near-End Crosstalk (PSNEXT) is the crosstalk into one pair from the other three together. 1000BASE-T Ethernet sends on all four pairs at once, so each pair receives crosstalk from the other three.
  • Attenuation to Crosstalk Ratio, Far End (ACR-F) and its power-sum form, PSACR-F, measure the coupling at the far end, relative to the insertion loss. They have the same causes as NEXT, usually with less effect.

Return loss

Return loss is the signal reflected back toward the tester wherever the link’s impedance changes. A cable crushed, kinked, deformed by an overtightened tie, or bent tighter than its minimum radius changes the impedance. So do excess untwisting at a termination and a component whose impedance differs from the cable’s. The owner’s cabling addendum sets the minimum bend radius at four times the cable’s outside diameter (A4). The tester reports return loss at each end.

The diagnostics distance

The diagnostics line gives the distance from the main unit to the largest disturbance on the link. That distance places the disturbance at one end or in the cable.

527.4.5 Margin and the worst case

The margin states by how much a result clears its limit, or misses it.

A certification tester measures each transmission parameter across the whole frequency range of the test. The limit changes with frequency. Each transmission row reports the worst point: the frequency where the measured value comes closest to the limit, or goes furthest past it.

The margin is the difference between the measured value and the limit, in decibels (dB).

  • Insertion loss: a lower value is better, so the margin is the limit minus the measured value.
  • Every other transmission parameter: a higher value is better, so the margin is the measured value minus the limit.

A positive margin passes and a negative margin fails. The row with the smallest margin holds the result closest to its limit; on a failing report, it is the result furthest past its limit.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 2 Reading a Certification Report

527.4.6 Accept or escalate

The overall result decides what happens to a link. The escalation states what failed and why.

Kestrel’s site lead sets the rule for the mock-up’s certification reports.

  • Accept a link whose report shows an overall PASS.
  • Escalate to the lead technician a link whose report shows an overall FAIL. The lead technician decides the repair. A report with an overall FAIL does not go to the owner.

What an escalation states

  • Each failing parameter, with its end and its pair or pairs.
  • Where the likely cause lies: the termination at one end, or a place in the cable. The diagnostics line gives the distance, where the report has one.
  • What in the installation produces the failure: the causes that parameter points to, narrowed by where the failure lies.

Record anything else you see in a report as a note; a note does not change the decision.

527.4.7 Pins, pairs, and T568B

A four-pair cable carries each signal on a twisted pair. A jack lands each pair on two assigned pins.

The links on the mock-up are Category 5e Unscreened Unshielded Twisted-Pair (U/UTP) cable (A2). The cable holds four twisted pairs: blue, orange, green, and brown. Each pair is a solid-colored conductor twisted with a white conductor striped in the same color. A signal travels on a pair, as the voltage difference between its two conductors.

ANSI/TIA-568 defines two pin and pair assignments for an eight-pin jack, T568A and T568B. The owner’s cabling addendum requires T568B on every jack, patch panel port, and patch cord (A1). A keystone jack carries a color label showing both, in rows marked A and B.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 3 The Split Pair

527.4.8 Why a split pair passes a continuity check

In a split pair, every conductor sits on the same pin at both ends, but each affected signal travels on two conductors that are not twisted together.

A split pair puts conductors from two different pairs on the pins of one signal pair. In this example, an installer exchanged the conductors on pins 2 and 3 at both ends of a link: white-green on pin 2, orange on pin 3.

Figure — A split pair: the same error at both ends
Text description of this diagram

The diagram is titled A split pair: the same error at both ends. Pins 1 to 8 at one end are joined by straight lines to pins 1 to 8 at the other end. Each line is labeled with its conductor: pin 1 white-orange, pin 2 white-green, pin 3 orange, pin 4 blue, pin 5 white-blue, pin 6 green, pin 7 white-brown, pin 8 brown. The lines for pins 2 and 3 are highlighted.

Three lines of text under the diagram read: Continuity check: each conductor reaches the same pin at the other end, so the check passes. Signal pair 1,2 runs on white-orange and white-green; signal pair 3,6 runs on orange and green. Neither signal pair is a twisted pair: each conductor is twisted with a conductor of the other signal pair.

The continuity check

A continuity check follows each conductor. White-green leaves pin 2 at one end and arrives at pin 2 at the other; every other conductor does the same. The check finds no open, no short, and no conductor on the wrong pin. It passes: the check never looks at which conductors share a twist.

The certification test

A network sends one signal on pins 1 and 2 and another on pins 3 and 6. In this link, each conductor of signal pair 1,2 is twisted with a conductor of signal pair 3,6, so the two signals couple into each other along the whole length of the link. A certification tester measures the crosstalk between pairs. On a split pair, NEXT between the two affected pairs fails at both ends, typically by a wide margin.

Some wiremap testers add a measurement that reports a split pair. A tester that checks only continuity and pin mapping cannot detect one.

A split pair needs the same error at both ends. With the error at one end only, the two conductors arrive on different pins, and the wiremap test fails.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 4 Finding and Repairing a Fault

527.4.9 Look before you test

Before testing a failing link, the installer looks at both of its ends and opens neither.

A link that fails its wiremap test holds its fault at a termination or in the cable. Most terminations cannot be seen without opening them, but the outside of each end can. An inspection reads both ends and changes nothing.

What to look at on each end of a link before testing it.
EndWhat you look at
OutletThe faceplate or cover fixed; the jack seated and latched; the label present and correct; the cable at the box entry neither crushed nor kinked
Patch panel portThe jack seated in its port; the port label present and correct; the cable behind the panel dressed, with no crushed or kinked jacket and no tie deforming it; no patch cord in the port

Record each end as found: the label you read, what you checked, and what you found. “Jack seated and latched, label correct, cable at the box entry undamaged” is a finding. “OK” records no check.

Why the test comes before any opening

The before-test is the wiremap and length test run on a failing link before any repair; the after-test is the same test run after the repair. The before-test records the fault as the link holds it. A termination opened first may no longer hold the fault that the test would have found.

A patch cord found in a panel port may connect the link to a switch in service. Leave it in place and report it to the site crew, Kestrel's staff at the MIR. Never plug a tester into a switch port.

527.4.10 The four wiremap faults and their causes

A wiremap tester names an open, a short, a reversed pair, or a crossed pair, with the pins it affects. Each fault has a usual cause at a termination.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 4 Finding and Repairing a Fault

527.4.11 Which end holds the fault

The tester names the fault. The type of fault decides how the installer finds the end that holds it.

An open or a short, with a length

Where the tester gives a length for the faulted conductor or pair, that length is the distance from the main unit to the fault. A length near that of the patch cord at the main unit puts the fault at the main unit’s end. A length near the link’s length puts it at the remote unit’s end.

Example — practice link 2-11, Dunmore Street Library (hypothetical)

The link is 64 ft long. The main unit is at the patch panel port, through a 1 ft patch cord, and the remote unit at the outlet jack.

The tester shows an open on pin 4, at 64 ft.

64 ft from the main unit is the far end of the link. The fault lies in the outlet jack’s termination.

A reversed or a crossed pair

The tester gives no distance for a reversed or a crossed pair. Open one end and compare its conductor order with T568B, which is the B row of the jack’s color label. If that end is in T568B order, the fault is at the other end.

Where the tester shows no length for an open or a short, open one end and inspect it in the same way.

The tone kit

A tone kit is a tone generator and a probe. With the generator plugged into an outlet, the probe sounds loudest at the cable that runs from that outlet, and fainter at the cables beside it. At the patch panel, run the probe along the cables behind the panel, find the loudest, and follow it to its port. The tone kit confirms which port an outlet’s cable reaches. It does not name a fault.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 4 Finding and Repairing a Fault

527.4.12 Repairing the termination

A repair puts the faulted end into T568B order, within the owner’s termination limits.

Repair the end that holds the fault, to T568B. Do not change the other end to match it. Two ends wired the same wrong way map pin to pin and pass a wiremap test, yet neither meets A1.

The termination limits

The owner’s cabling addendum sets these limits for every termination (A3):

  • The jack or patch panel manufacturer’s instructions govern how much jacket the installer strips.
  • Each pair stays twisted to within 0.5 in (13 mm) of its contact, the limit ANSI/TIA-568 sets for Category 5e.
  • The cable jacket reaches the back of the jack.

Re-seat, or a new jack

  • Re-seat where every conductor at the termination is undamaged and reaches its contact within the limits. Lift each conductor in the wrong place with the hook end of the punch-down tool, and remove any stray copper. Seat each conductor in its T568B contact, and trim it with the punch-down tool.
  • New jack where a conductor is nicked or broken, or will not reach its contact within the limits, or where the jack is damaged. Cut the cable back past the old termination, then terminate a new jack.

Cutting back shortens the cable. The 12 in of slack at the outlet and the 10 ft service loop at the MIR allow for it (A5). Where cutting back would remove the cable label, get a new label before you cut (A6). Wear safety glasses to cut, strip, or trim.

Photograph the termination before the cap goes on, with the conductor order and the jacket at the back of the jack in the frame. Once the cap is on, the photograph is the only view of the termination.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 4 Finding and Repairing a Fault

527.4.13 Retest and record

The installer proves the repair with the test that found the fault, run the same way.

Run the same wiremap and length test, with the main unit and the remote unit at the same ends as in the before-test. The two results then differ only by the repair. A pass shows that the fault the before-test named is gone, and that the repair has made no new wiremap fault.

If the after-test fails, open the termination again, repair it, and test again. Record the last result.

The record of a fault

  • End: the outlet or the patch panel port that held the fault.
  • Found: the fault, the conductors or pairs it affects, and what was in the wrong place.
  • Cause: what in the installation produced the fault.
  • Correction: re-seat or new jack, and at which end.
  • After-test: the result as the tester’s screen shows it.

Photographs of the before-test screen, the open termination, and the after-test screen support each entry.

Example — record entry, practice link 2-11, Dunmore Street Library (hypothetical)

End. Outlet jack.

Found. Open on pin 4: the blue conductor resting in its slot, not pressed into the contact.

Cause. Conductor not punched down.

Correction. Re-seated the blue conductor in its T568B contact and trimmed it, at the outlet jack.

After-test. Pass, 64 ft.

Put the work on a failing link in order, from first to last.

Look at both ends of the link without opening either
Run the before-test and photograph the screen
Open the termination at the end that holds the fault
Re-seat the conductors, or terminate a new jack, to T568B
Photograph the termination with its cap off
Fit the cap, refit the jack, and run the same test with the units at the same ends

Lesson 527.4 · Section 5 Checking an AI Tool

527.4.14 Checking an AI reading of a report

An artificial intelligence (AI) tool can turn a certification report into a paragraph in seconds. The installer who passes that paragraph on answers for every statement in it.

A general-purpose AI assistant, such as Microsoft Copilot, has not seen the link. It knows the report only from the prompt. Its reply reads the same whether a statement is right or wrong.

An AI reading of a report can call a passing row a failure, quote a figure or a limit the report does not print, place a cause at the wrong end, or recommend accepting a link whose overall result is FAIL.

The check

  1. Write your own interpretation of the report first.
  2. Paste the whole report into the prompt. Ask which parameters fail, what the likely cause is, and whether the link should be accepted. Keep the prompt and the whole reply as they were.
  3. From the reply, choose statements of fact or judgment about the link.
  4. For each statement, find the value in the report that bears on it. Mark the statement “agrees” where the report supports it, or “errs” where the report contradicts it or does not show what it claims. Enter the value or the reason.

The report decides each statement. A statement is not right because the tool wrote it, or because it matches your own reading.

Lesson 527.4 · Section 5 Checking an AI Tool

527.4.15 Checking an AI fault hypothesis

Before opening a failing link, an installer can ask an AI tool what the fault is likely to be and where, then check the answer against the termination.

Put the before-test result in the prompt: the fault the screen shows, the pins, any length shown, and which unit was at which end. Ask what the fault is likely to be and at which end to look. Keep the prompt and the whole reply.

For an open or a short with a length, the end follows from the distance, so the tool can work it out from the prompt. For a reversed or a crossed pair, the before-test shows no distance, so nothing in the prompt tells the tool which end holds the fault.

When you have found the fault, compare the tool’s fault type and its location with what you found, one at a time. Each one agrees or errs on its own.

Check Your Understanding

An AI tool’s hypothesis reads: “Reversed pair on pair 3,6, at the outlet jack.” You find the outlet jack in T568B order, and white-green and green exchanged at the patch panel port. How do you mark the hypothesis?

Lesson 527.4 · Section 6 Wrapping Up

527.4.16 What you covered

Each team verifies every link with a wiremap tester. The lead technician certifies every link at closeout. Where a wiremap test fails, the installer finds the fault, repairs it, and proves the repair with the same test.

Three kinds of test

Verification asks whether a link is wired correctly. A wiremap and length pass under A7 means all eight conductors are continuous, each reaching the same pin at the far end and connected to no other conductor, and the link is not over 295 ft. Qualification asks whether a link can carry one named application. Certification asks whether a link meets every limit of its category, across the category’s frequency range (A8).

Reading a certification report

The header says where the main unit and the remote unit were; every diagnostic distance is measured from the main unit. Crosstalk most often points to the termination at the end where it fails; return loss points to a deformed cable or a termination; length and insertion loss point to the run. The margin is the measured value minus the limit, or the limit minus the measured value for insertion loss; a negative margin fails. Accept a link on an overall PASS. Escalate an overall FAIL with each failing parameter, where its cause lies, and what produces it.

The split pair

T568B puts the orange pair on pins 1 and 2, green on 3 and 6, blue on 4 and 5, and brown on 7 and 8. A split pair puts conductors of two pairs on one signal pair’s pins, the same way at both ends. Every conductor reaches the same pin, so a continuity check passes. The signals couple between the pairs, so NEXT fails on certification.

Finding and repairing a fault

The four wiremap faults: how to find the end that holds each, and its usual cause.
FaultFinding the endUsual cause
OpenThe length to the fault, where shown; otherwise open one end and lookConductor not pressed into its contact, or broken
ShortThe length to the fault, where shown; otherwise open one end and lookCopper bridging two contacts
Reversed pairOpen one end and compare it with T568BThe two conductors of a pair exchanged at one end
Crossed pairOpen one end and compare it with T568BOne end terminated to T568A, or two pairs exchanged

Look at both ends before the test, and open nothing. Repair the faulted end to T568B within A3: re-seat undamaged conductors, or cut back and fit a new jack. Photograph the termination before the cap goes on. Retest with the same test, the units at the same ends. Record the end, what you found, the cause, the correction, and the after-test.

Checking an AI tool

Do your own reading first. Mark each AI statement “agrees” or “errs” against the report, with the value that decides it. Check a fault hypothesis’s type and location separately against what you find.
