527.3.1 Rated walls and the openings in them

Lesson 527.3

Firestop, Bonding and Link Verification

Three tasks close the cabling of a work area: sealing the sleeve where the cables cross a fire-rated wall, bonding the rack to the room’s busbar, and connecting a laptop at an outlet to show that the link reaches the network. Sections 1 to 3 cover the tasks in that order.

Where a cable bundle passes through a fire-rated wall, the installer seals the opening with a tested firestop system.

A fire-rated assembly is a wall or floor built and tested to hold back fire for a stated time, such as one hour. Each opening cut through it for a pipe, a duct, or a cable bundle is a penetration. An open penetration lets flame and hot gases through the wall. An unsealed sleeve does the same once the cable jackets in it burn away.

The National Electrical Code (NFPA 70) requires the openings around cables that pass through fire-rated walls and floors to be firestopped by approved methods that keep the assembly’s rating (section 800.26 in the 2023 edition, the edition adopted for this project). The authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) is the official who enforces the codes on a project, such as the building or fire inspector.

What the owner’s standard requires

Clause 27 05 28 3.4 of the owner’s standard sets the project’s rule for every penetration of a fire-rated wall or floor:

  • a sleeve where the drawings show one;
  • the firestop system scheduled on sheet T-501, installed to the manufacturer’s listed system drawing;
  • a label on each side giving the system number, the installer, and the date;
  • a photograph before the penetration is concealed;
  • inspection by the AHJ before the ceiling is closed;
  • the firestop restored the same day wherever cable is added through an existing penetration.

The drawings say which walls are rated. From the corridor, a rated gypsum wall and a non-rated partition can look the same. On Level 2 of the Harlow Street project, the corridor wall is rated for one hour. The firestop schedule on T-501 tags its sleeved cable penetrations FS-1.

527.3.2 Listed systems and the system drawing

A firestop system keeps its rating only when it is installed as it was tested. The listing records what was tested.

A firestop system is a tested combination: the wall or floor construction, the penetrating item, the sleeve where one is used, how much of the opening the item fills, and the firestop product with its quantity. A testing laboratory fire-tests the whole combination to the fire-test standard UL 1479 or ASTM E814 and publishes it as a listed system, with a system number and a system drawing.

The test gives the system an F rating: the time, in hours, for which it kept flame from passing through the opening. On sheet T-501, tag FS-1 is a cable bundle in a steel sleeve through a one-hour gypsum wall, sealed with a system in the W-L-3000 series that has an F rating of one hour.

Reading a system number

Read a UL system number part by part, from left to right.

The manufacturer’s installation instructions give the method: how to pack the product, how to finish its face, and which conditions to check before starting. A note on T-501 requires the contractor to submit the manufacturer’s listed system drawing for each tag before installation.

Where no listed system fits

Where a penetration on site does not match the conditions of the scheduled system, the installer does not seal it to the nearest drawing. The contractor reports the difference in writing, as a request for information (RFI), and waits for the answer before sealing the penetration (owner’s standard 27 05 00 1.1 and 1.2). The answer may name another listed system, or a written engineering judgment from the firestop manufacturer that the AHJ accepts.

527.3.3 What a training penetration shows

A penetration built on a bench shows how the installer works. It does not show that the installation would hold back fire.

A training penetration is a board with a short steel sleeve set through it and a bundle of cable offcuts through the sleeve. It stands for a sleeved penetration of a rated wall. The board is not a tested wall or floor construction. No listed system covers it, so putty packed into its sleeve carries no fire rating.

A listed system’s rating depends on every condition of its drawing at once. Change one, and the installation is no longer the system that was tested. Open each card for a change that takes a penetration outside its listing.

What the bench work does show

A bench penetration shows whether the installer reads the system drawing, checks the penetration against its conditions, packs the product to the stated depth with no void, labels both sides, and stops for inspection. An inspector checks a production penetration in the same way.

527.3.4 Packing putty and inspecting it

The installer packs putty into each end of the sleeve to the depth on the system drawing. The inspector checks depth, contact, and labels on both sides.

An intumescent putty is a moldable firestop compound that expands when heated. In a fire it swells into the space the burning cable jackets leave. Putty stays soft after installation, so the installer can remove it and pack it again when cables are added. Some firestop sealants cure hard instead; to add a cable, the installer cuts the cured sealant out.

Read the product’s safety data sheet before handling it. It states whether gloves are required.

Before packing: check the conditions

Read the system number, the conditions, and the putty depth from the system drawing. Compare each condition with the penetration in front of you, and record for each one whether the penetration meets it. Where a condition is not met, stop and report it. Packing putty does not bring a penetration within a listing it does not match.

Packing each end

  1. Press putty into one end of the sleeve, around and between the cables, until it reaches the depth on the drawing.
  2. Work it against the inside of the sleeve and against every cable jacket, leaving no gap or void at the face of the putty. Keep the cables where they lie in the sleeve.
  3. Finish the face of the putty as the manufacturer’s instructions describe.
  4. Repeat at the other end. Sheet T-501, detail 2, shows putty or sealant on both sides of the wall.
  5. Fix a label on each side, beside the sleeve, giving the system number, your name as the installer, and the date.

Lesson 527.3 · Section 2 Bonding the Rack

527.3.5 The bonding network in a telecommunications room

Every rack in a telecommunications room is bonded to the room’s busbar with a conductor of its own.

Bonding joins metal parts with conductors so that they stay at nearly the same electrical potential. In a building, the telecommunications bonding network runs back to the building’s grounding electrode system. A fault current or a surge on a rack, a ladder rack, or a cable tray then has a low-resistance path to ground. No large voltage builds up between two pieces of equipment that a technician can touch at the same time.

The chain, from the building to the rack

The owner’s standard, section 27 05 26, sets each link in the chain.

The parts of the bonding network in the owner’s standard, from the building to the rack.
PartWhere it isClause
Primary bonding busbar (PBB)In the equipment room, bonded to the building grounding electrode system at the service equipment by the electrical contractor2.1
Telecommunications bonding backbone (TBB)A copper conductor from the PBB to each SBB, not smaller than 6 American Wire Gauge (AWG), sized by its length2.3
Secondary bonding busbar (SBB)In every telecommunications room and in the entrance facility2.2
Rack bonding conductorFrom each rack, cabinet, ladder rack, and cable tray section to the SBB: its own 6 AWG copper conductor2.4

Older documents call the PBB the telecommunications main grounding busbar (TMGB) and the SBB the telecommunications grounding busbar (TGB). The owner’s cabling addendum applies clause 2.4 to the MIR: clause A10 requires a dedicated 6 AWG copper conductor from the rack to the SBB. The connection between the PBB and the grounding electrode system is the electrical contractor’s work. A telecommunications installer does not alter the building’s grounding.

One conductor for each rack

Clause 2.4 forbids bonding racks to each other in series (“daisy-chained”). In a series chain, the bond of each rack runs through every rack and every joint before it, so an open joint, or one rack moved and disconnected, leaves every rack beyond it unbonded. With its own conductor, a rack’s bond depends only on its own two joints. Sheet T-501, detail 3, shows each rack on its own lug at the SBB.

527.3.6 Crimping, landing, and torquing a bond

A rack bond has a joint at each end of its conductor. Each joint is a crimped lug, bolted to bare metal and tightened to the manufacturer’s torque.

A lug is a terminal crimped onto the end of the bonding conductor and bolted to the busbar or to the rack. Every figure for the joint comes from the manufacturers of the lug, the crimp tool, and the busbar or bolt hardware. On a job, the installer works from a card or a data sheet that collects them. It gives the lug part number, the crimp tool and die, the number and position of the crimps, and the strip length. It also gives the order of the bolt, washers, and nut, and the torque value.

Strip and crimp

  • Strip the conductor to the length the lug manufacturer gives, so that it seats fully in the lug’s barrel. Leave no strand outside the barrel.
  • Crimp with the tool and die the manufacturer names, with the stated number of crimps in the stated positions. A die made for another size can close the barrel too little to grip the conductor. Many dies emboss a code on the barrel, so an inspector can see which die made the crimp.
  • Pull the lug firmly by hand. It must not move on the conductor.

Land on bare metal

Paint and powder coat insulate. The lug lands on a bonding point of bare metal: a spot on the rack cleared of paint, or a fitting made for the purpose. At the busbar, the lug lands on a position of its own. The screw, washers, and any nut go on in the order the hardware or busbar manufacturer gives.

Torque and the torque mark

The torque value is the tightening torque the manufacturer sets for the joint. A torque wrench or torque screwdriver set to that value signals when the joint reaches it. A joint left loose can work looser and rise in resistance. A joint driven past the value can strip a thread or deform the lug.

After tightening, the installer draws a torque mark: a marker line across the screw head or nut, the washer, and the lug. A screw or nut that turns afterward breaks the line. An inspector sees the break without a tool.

527.3.7 Measuring a bond

A multimeter on its resistance range shows whether a bond is continuous. Many handheld meters cannot tell a sound joint from a weak one.

Measure resistance only on a conductor that is isolated from every other conductor. A bench bonding fixture is connected to nothing else. A bond in service is part of the building’s bonding network; measure it only by the procedure the owner or the electrical contractor sets.

The method

  1. Set the multimeter to resistance (Ω). Touch the two probe tips together. Where the meter has a RANGE button, press it until the display shows tenths of an ohm.
  2. Keep the probe tips together and note the reading: the leads-touched reading. It is the resistance of the leads and the probe contacts.
  3. Hold one probe on bare metal of the rack rail beside the lug and the other on the busbar beside the other lug. Note the reading: the rail-to-busbar reading.
  4. Subtract the leads-touched reading from the rail-to-busbar reading. The result is the net reading.
Worked net reading for a rack bond on another job, measured on a handheld multimeter.
ReadingOhms
Rail to busbar0.3 Ω
Less leads touched− 0.3 Ω
Net0.0 Ω

What the reading can show

6 AWG copper has a resistance of about 0.4 thousandths of an ohm per foot, so a 2 ft rack bond conductor measures under one thousandth of an ohm. A sound crimped and bolted joint adds thousandths of an ohm at most. Many handheld multimeters display resistance in steps of 0.1 Ω on their lowest range; the meter’s specification states its resolution. On such a meter, a sound bond and a weak one can both give a net reading of 0.0 Ω or 0.1 Ω.

The meter does show an open bond: a lug off its screw or a conductor broken in its barrel gives the meter’s over-range reading, shown as OL on many meters.

The acceptance figure

An owner, a busbar or lug manufacturer, or an electrical contractor may set an acceptance figure: the largest resistance a bond may show. Where the figure is set in thousandths of an ohm, contractors measure the bond with a low-resistance ohmmeter, which resolves far smaller values than a handheld multimeter. Where a figure is posted, compare the net reading with it and state whether the reading meets it. Where none is posted, record that none is posted. A statement that a bond passes needs a figure to pass against.

Lesson 527.3 · Section 3 Proving a Link on the Network

527.3.8 From the outlet to the switch

Traffic from a laptop at a work-area outlet crosses several cables and ports before it reaches the network. A wiremap test covers some of them. The network checks cover the rest.

A permanent link is the installed cable from the outlet jack to the patch panel port, with the termination at each end. It excludes patch cords. To reach the network, a laptop also needs a cord from the laptop to the outlet, a patch cord from the panel port to a port on the switch, and the switch port itself. Beyond the switch, the gateway is the router that connects the local network to other networks.

What a wiremap test covers

A wiremap and length test runs between the tester’s two ends. On a permanent link, a pass shows all eight conductors continuous, each reaching the same pin at the far end and connected to no other conductor, with a length not over 295 ft (cabling addendum A7). The test covers the jacks and the cable between them. The installer tests each cord on its own.

No cable test shows whether the switch port is enabled, which network the port belongs to, or whether a server on the network answers. A switch port that is shut down passes no traffic. A port assigned to the wrong network carries traffic to the wrong network. Every cable on the path still tests as a pass. The network checks, run from a laptop, cover the switch port and the network beyond it.

527.3.9 What the laptop reports

Once the laptop is plugged into the outlet, two Windows commands show whether it has a link to the switch port and which IPv4 address the network supplied.

Connecting

The network team assigns the switch port for each panel port. A patch cord runs from the panel port to that switch port and no other: other ports on a switch can carry other networks or the switch’s connections to the rest of the network. Test the laptop cord on a wiremap tester before you use it.

Turn the laptop’s Wi-Fi off before you plug in. With Wi-Fi on, Windows can send the test traffic over Wi-Fi instead of through the link. With Wi-Fi off, the Ethernet adapter is the laptop’s only path to the network. Change no other setting: the switch and the network belong to the owner’s network team.

Run both commands in Windows PowerShell. The prompt, such as PS C:\Users\installer>, shows that PowerShell is waiting for a command.

The link: Get-NetAdapter

Get-NetAdapter — a laptop on another network, Wi-Fi off
PS C:\Users\installer> Get-NetAdapter

Ethernet                  Realtek PCIe GbE Family Controller           12 Up           00-00-5E-00-53-2B        1 Gbps
  • Name and InterfaceDescription — the adapter’s name in Windows and its make and model. The wired adapter is usually named Ethernet.
  • ifIndex — a number Windows gives each adapter. MacAddress — the adapter’s hardware address, its Media Access Control (MAC) address.
  • Status — Up when the adapter has a link to the device at the other end of its cable; Disconnected when it has none. Disabled means the adapter is turned off in Windows.
  • LinkSpeed — the speed the adapter and the switch port agreed when the link came up.

The address: ipconfig /all

When the laptop connects, a Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) server on the network supplies its Internet Protocol version 4 (IPv4) address, its subnet mask, and its default gateway. The subnet mask tells the laptop which IPv4 addresses are on its own network. Traffic to any other IPv4 address goes to the default gateway.

PS C:\Users\installer> ipconfig /all

  • DHCP Enabled — Yes means the adapter is set to ask for its address. The line reads Yes whether or not a server has answered.
  • IPv4 Address — the address the adapter is using, marked (Preferred) by Windows.
  • Default Gateway — the router’s IPv4 address on this network.
  • DHCP Server — the IPv4 address of the server that supplied the address. The line appears only when a DHCP server has answered.

Where the adapter asks and no DHCP server answers, Windows gives the adapter an IPv4 address of its own, starting with 169.254, on a line labeled Autoconfiguration IPv4 Address. The Default Gateway line is then blank.

527.3.10 Ping and trace

A ping asks one IPv4 address for a reply. A trace lists each router between the laptop and an IPv4 address.

Ping

On Windows, ping sends four echo requests, using the Internet Control Message Protocol (ICMP), and prints one line for each request. A summary follows.

ping — a gateway on another job’s network replies
PS C:\Users\installer> ping 10.12.40.1

Pinging 10.12.40.1 with 32 bytes of data:
Reply from 10.12.40.1: bytes=32 time<1ms TTL=64
Reply from 10.12.40.1: bytes=32 time<1ms TTL=64
Reply from 10.12.40.1: bytes=32 time=1ms TTL=64
Reply from 10.12.40.1: bytes=32 time<1ms TTL=64

Ping statistics for 10.12.40.1:
    Packets: Sent = 4, Received = 4, Lost = 0 (0% loss),
Approximate round trip times in milli-seconds:
    Minimum = 0ms, Maximum = 1ms, Average = 0ms
  • Reply from — the IPv4 address that answered. bytes=32 is the size of the test data. time is the round trip, in milliseconds; time<1ms means under one. Time to live (TTL) is a count that each router on the way lowers by one; it plays no part in these checks.
  • Packets — requests sent, replies received, and requests lost. Record this line.

Where nothing answers, ping prints Request timed out. for each request. The summary counts every request as lost.

ping — an IPv4 address that does not reply
PS C:\Users\installer> ping 10.12.90.44

Pinging 10.12.90.44 with 32 bytes of data:
Request timed out.
Request timed out.
Request timed out.
Request timed out.

Ping statistics for 10.12.90.44:
    Packets: Sent = 4, Received = 0, Lost = 4 (100% loss),

Ping prints no round-trip times, because no reply came back.

A line that reads Reply from, followed by the laptop’s own IPv4 address and Destination host unreachable, means the laptop could not deliver the request. The reply did not come from the address pinged.

Windows counts each Destination host unreachable line as a packet received, so the summary can show no loss when nothing answered. Read the reply lines as well as the summary: a reply counts only when it comes from the address pinged.

No reply does not prove a device is down. A device’s firewall can be set not to answer ping; with its default settings, Windows Defender Firewall blocks ping requests from other computers. A network team names ping targets that are set to answer.

Trace

On Windows, the trace command is tracert. It lists each hop on the way to an IPv4 address, numbered from 1: each router the traffic passes through, then the target. The -d option stops Windows from looking up a name for each hop, so the trace shows IPv4 addresses only and finishes sooner.

tracert -d — a target on another network, reached through the gateway
PS C:\Users\installer> tracert -d 10.12.90.20

Tracing route to 10.12.90.20 over a maximum of 30 hops

  1    <1 ms    <1 ms    <1 ms  10.12.40.1
  2     1 ms    <1 ms     1 ms  10.12.90.20

Trace complete.
  • Hop 1 — the gateway, 10.12.40.1: the first router on the way off the laptop’s network.
  • Hop 2 — the target. The three times on each line are three separate probes to that hop.
  • Trace complete. — the target answered. If the target never answers, the command stops after 30 hops; Ctrl+C stops it sooner.

A probe that gets no answer within the time limit shows * in place of its time. Where all three probes to a hop get none, the line ends Request timed out. in place of an address. A target on the laptop’s own network appears as hop 1, with no router before it.

527.3.11 Cabling or network

Each check depends on the one before it. The first check that fails shows whether the fault lies in the cabling, which the installer built, or in the network and its services, which the owner’s network team runs.

A cabling fault lies in a cord, a jack, a termination, or the installed cable. A network or service fault lies in the switch, its settings, or a service on the network: the DHCP server, the gateway, or the target. Read the results in the order the laptop produced them.

Before you name the network, rule out what is in your hands: re-seat each plug, test the laptop cord again, and check the patch cord against the switch port assigned to it. Record what the laptop shows, and report a network or service fault to the network team. Do not change a setting to clear it.

527.3.12 Recording the path and the results

A verification record lets a reader who was not at the outlet check each entry against a screenshot.

The record holds the topology, the commands, and the results.

  • Topology. Each element of the path, in order from the laptop to the address traced, with its identifier, port, or IPv4 address.
  • Commands. Each command as typed, with its options: tracert -d, not “trace”.
  • Results. Each reading as the output shows it, with a screenshot of the whole output, from the command to its summary line. On Windows, Windows+Shift+S takes a screenshot.

Enter a failed check as the screen shows it, and go on to the next check. Keep the reading apart from the conclusion: the reading is what the screen showed, and the conclusion names the side it points to and the reading that decides it. Note anything done again, and why.

Lesson 527.3 · Section 4 Wrapping Up

527.3.13 What you covered

A sealed penetration, a bonded rack, and a link proved on the network each leave evidence an inspector can check: labels and photographs, torque marks and meter readings, screenshots and a record.

Firestopping a penetration

The installer seals a penetration of a fire-rated assembly with the system scheduled on the drawings, installed to the manufacturer’s listed system drawing. The system holds its rating only where the construction, the penetrating item, the sleeve, the fill, and the product all match that drawing; a bench training penetration matches no listed construction and has no rating. Check each condition before packing, pack both ends to the drawing’s depth with no void at the face, label each side with the system number, the installer, and the date, and leave the putty untouched until the inspector has checked it.

Bonding the rack

Each rack has its own 6 AWG conductor to the SBB, never a series chain. Strip, crimp, land on bare metal, and torque to the manufacturer’s figures; draw a torque mark after tightening. A handheld multimeter shows that a bond is continuous. Subtract the leads-touched reading, and compare the net reading with an acceptance figure only where one is posted.
