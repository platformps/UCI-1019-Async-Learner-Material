535.2.1 A path with named parts

Lesson 535.2

From the Outlet to the Switch

Seven elements lie between a device at a wall outlet and a port on a switch. This lesson names each element and its role, shows how a laptop and a switch report what is connected, and sets out a method for proving which switch port serves an outlet and checking that proof against the record.

4 sections  ·  10 topics  ·  9 activities

By the end of Lesson 535.2 you will be able to
  • Identify the named elements of a structured cabling path and the role of each.
  • Distinguish the roles of a switch, an access point, a gateway, a Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) server, and a Domain Name System (DNS) server.
  • Identify a laptop’s network adapter, its Media Access Control (MAC) address, and its link state.
  • Trace a run from a wall outlet to a switch port.
  • Confirm the switch port from the MAC address table.
  • Relate a port assignment to the physical position it describes.
  • Cross-check a physical trace against the cabling record.

In a building cabled to a standard, the same seven elements sit, in the same order, between every device and its switch port.

Five of the seven elements are cabling. ANSI/TIA-568, the structured cabling standard from the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA), names each one. The standard’s names are the ones to use on a work order, on a label, and on the phone to someone who cannot see the rack.

The other two elements are the ends that the cabling joins: the network adapter in the device, and the port on the switch. Neither is cabling. Either can be the fault.

One outlet and the cabling that serves it form a drop.

Open each card, working from the device toward the switch.

Two cords, counted separately

Elements 2 and 6 are both patch cords. They are the parts of the path handled every day. Many faults reported as a broken cable are in a cord. They differ in what is recorded: no record exists for the cord at the outlet, while a change to the cord in the rack belongs in the port map.

ANSI/TIA-568 names two stretches of the same path. The permanent link is elements 3, 4, and 5: the outlet, the horizontal cable, and the patch panel port. It is the fixed part, the part an installer usually certifies when the building is cabled. The channel is elements 2 to 6: the permanent link plus a patch cord at each end, the cabling the device uses.

The seven elements of the path, and which of them each standard term covers.
ElementPermanent linkChannel
1 Network adapterNo: equipmentNo: equipment
2 Patch cord, device to outletNoYes
3 Telecommunications outletYesYes
4 Horizontal cableYesYes
5 Patch panel portYesYes
6 Patch cord, panel to switchNoYes
7 Switch portNo: equipmentNo: equipment
A certified link and a dead connection

Certification at installation usually tests the permanent link. A drop can hold a valid certificate and still fail, because a patch cord at one end is damaged and the cords were never part of the test. When a link with a certificate behind it fails, check the cords first.

535.2.2 The horizontal run, end to end

One cable serves one outlet and lands on one patch panel port, within a length limit that counts the cords.

Once the walls and ceilings are closed, the horizontal cable cannot be inspected. Fixed rules govern how it is installed. The rule: no splices, no shared cables, no branches. Under that rule, the panel port identifies the outlet, and the outlet identifies the panel port. A branch or a shared cable anywhere breaks the trace for every drop beyond it.

Figure: The permanent link and the channel

A closing line reads: The 10 m difference is the allowance for both patch cords together.

The switch port in the figure is named Gi1/0/12. A Cisco switch names each port by type and number. The letters give the port type: Gi marks a port that runs at up to 1000 megabits per second (Mbps); Fa marks one that stops at 100 Mbps. The numbers locate the port. A Catalyst 3750G puts its switch number first, as in Gi1/0/12. A Catalyst 2960G names the same port Gi0/12.

Two length limits govern the run.

  • 90 meters, permanent link. For twisted-pair copper, 295 feet of horizontal cable, measured along the route it takes. A cable that rises up a wall, crosses a ceiling, bends around an obstruction, and drops down again is much longer than the distance on the floor plan.
  • 100 meters, channel. For copper, 328 feet for the whole path. The 10 meters between the two figures is the allowance for the cord at the outlet and the cord in the rack together. Cords are stranded cable, which loses more signal per meter than the solid horizontal cable. A meter of extra cord therefore needs more than a meter less of horizontal cable.

A cable damaged in the middle is replaced, not joined. The one permitted interruption is a consolidation point, a documented intermediate termination used where furniture layouts change often.

Fiber in the horizontal

The figures above are for twisted-pair copper, which most horizontal runs use. Fiber is also permitted in the horizontal, usually where electrical noise is a problem or the outlet feeds equipment that needs more bandwidth than copper carries.

In the usual horizontal design, the 90-meter limit applies to fiber as well, although fiber reaches much further. The limit fixes where telecommunications rooms go: every outlet within 90 meters of a room. A building whose rooms were spaced for fiber’s full reach would have no room close enough when someone later needed a copper drop, for example to a camera powered by Power over Ethernet (PoE), which fiber cannot carry. Holding both media to 90 meters keeps the building’s layout independent of the cable pulled through it. Fiber’s reach is used in the backbone, between rooms, floors, and buildings.

What an over-length run costs

An over-length link often works: it comes up, passes traffic, and goes unnoticed. It has lost margin (the difference between how the link performs and the limit it must meet), so it fails sooner under heat, electrical noise, and higher speeds. When it fails, it shows as an intermittent fault with no visible cause.

535.2.3 Horizontal and backbone

Horizontal and backbone cabling do different jobs. The number of people a fault affects points to one or the other.

Structured cabling divides into two kinds by what a run connects, not by what the cable is made of.

Horizontal and backbone cabling compared.
PropertyHorizontal cablingBackbone cabling
ConnectsWork areas to the telecommunications room that serves themRooms to each other: floor to floor, room to equipment room, building to building
How manyMany runs, one per outletFew runs, each carrying the traffic of many
Usual mediumTwisted-pair copperOptical fiber; sometimes twisted-pair over short distances
Length limit90 m permanent linkDepends on the medium and the application, and reaches much further
If it failsOne outlet stops workingEverything beyond it stops working

A fiber run to an outlet is horizontal cabling. A twisted-pair run between two telecommunications rooms is backbone cabling.

One person affected, with everyone nearby working normally, points to a horizontal run, a cord, a jack, or a switch port: one drop. A whole floor or a whole room losing service at the same moment points to something shared: a backbone run, an uplink (the link from that room’s switch to the rest of the network), a switch, or the power to the rack. Testing one person’s patch cord will not find a shared fault. Pulling cords at random while people are affected makes the outage worse.

535.2.5 The network adapter: link, speed, and MAC address

The network adapter is the first element of the path. Windows reports its link, its speed, and its MAC address.

A laptop’s network adapter, also called a Network Interface Card (NIC), is the hardware that connects the laptop to the network. A laptop may have a built-in Ethernet port, a USB-to-Ethernet adapter, and a Wi-Fi adapter, and each one is a separate adapter with its own MAC address.

When a cord joins the adapter to a live switch port, the two agree on a speed and bring up a link. Many switch ports and USB adapters have a light that comes on with the link and flickers with traffic. Many laptops have no link light, so read the link in Windows.

Reading the adapter in Windows

In PowerShell, Get-NetAdapter lists each adapter on its own line. The example comes from another laptop.

Get-NetAdapter
PS C:\Users\tech> Get-NetAdapter

Name        InterfaceDescription                ifIndex Status       MacAddress          LinkSpeed
----        --------------------                ------- ------       ----------          ---------
Ethernet 2  Realtek USB GbE Family Controller        18 Up           5C-11-BB-22-CC-33     1 Gbps
Wi-Fi       Intel(R) Wi-Fi 6 AX201 160MHz             7 Disconnected 8C-55-4A-0F-31-9E      0 bps
  • Name and InterfaceDescription: the name Windows gives the adapter, and the hardware behind it. Here, Ethernet 2 is a USB-to-Ethernet adapter, and Wi-Fi is the wireless adapter. ifIndex is the number Windows uses for the adapter internally.
  • Status: Up means the adapter has a link. Disconnected means it has none: no cord, a dead far end, or, for Wi-Fi, no wireless network joined.
  • MacAddress: the adapter’s MAC address.
  • LinkSpeed: the speed the adapter and the far end agreed, 1 Gbps here. A disconnected adapter shows 0 bps.

The command ipconfig /all shows the same MAC address, labeled Physical Address, with the adapter’s IP settings.

A link at the wrong speed

A link at 1000 Mbps uses all four pairs of the cable; a link at 100 Mbps needs only two. Where an adapter and a switch port that both support 1000 Mbps link at 100 Mbps, the cause can be a fault on a pair that only 1000 Mbps uses. Test the cabling before blaming the adapter.

One MAC address, three ways of writing it

A MAC address is 12 hexadecimal digits (0 to 9 and A to F). Equipment groups and separates the digits in different ways. The case of the letters carries no meaning.

The same MAC address as three systems write it.
Where you read itFormExample
WindowsSix pairs, joined by hyphens, in capitals5C-11-BB-22-CC-33
Cisco switchThree groups of four, joined by dots, in lowercase5c11.bb22.cc33
Many other devices and management toolsSix pairs, joined by colons5c:11:bb:22:cc:33

The digits and their order never change between the forms; only the separators and the grouping do. A Cisco switch expects the dotted form when you search its tables for an address.

Read the wired adapter

A trace of a wired path needs the wired adapter’s MAC address. Windows can give a Wi-Fi adapter a random hardware address, so its MAC address may change from one network to the next.

535.2.6 Reading a switch’s MAC address table

A switch learns which port each device is on from the frames it receives. Its MAC address table shows where a device is connected.

Reaching the switch’s command line

You type switch commands at the switch’s command line, reached through its console port. On a Windows laptop:

  1. Connect the USB console cable from the laptop to the switch’s console port.
  2. In Device Manager, open Ports (COM & LPT) and read the COM number of the USB serial port, for example COM4.
  3. In PuTTY, select Serial, enter that COM number, and check that Speed reads 9600. Under Connection > Serial, set Flow control to None.
  4. Open the connection and press Enter. The switch usually answers with its prompt: its name followed by >, for example Switch>.

A prompt ending in > allows commands that read the switch without changing it. A prompt ending in # also allows commands that change the switch. The show commands on this page usually run at the > prompt. If the switch rejects one, type enable.

On a Cisco Catalyst switch, show mac address-table lists every entry in the switch’s MAC address table. Older releases of the Cisco Internetwork Operating System (IOS) spell it show mac-address-table. Adding the keyword address, then a MAC address in the dotted form, narrows the list to that one device. The example comes from another switch, named Switch.

show mac address-table address 5c11.bb22.cc33
Switch> show mac address-table address 5c11.bb22.cc33
          Mac Address Table
-------------------------------------------

Vlan    Mac Address       Type        Ports
----    -----------       --------    -----
 140    5c11.bb22.cc33    DYNAMIC     Gi0/7
Total Mac Addresses for this criterion: 1
  • Vlan: the VLAN in which the switch saw the address, 140.
  • Mac Address: the address, in the dotted form.
  • Type: DYNAMIC means the switch learned the address from traffic. STATIC means the entry was configured, or belongs to the switch itself.
  • Ports: the port on which the switch saw the address, Gi0/7. That port is the device’s switch port.
  • Total Mac Addresses for this criterion: how many entries matched, one here.

Reading the table correctly

  • The table records traffic, not cables. An entry appears only after the device sends a frame. A laptop that has sent nothing for a while may be missing: make it send traffic, for example with a ping, and read the table again.
  • Entries expire. The switch removes the entry for a device that sends nothing for 300 seconds, the default on Catalyst switches. It also removes every entry on a port whose link goes down.
  • One device, one port. The port in the entry is where the switch hears the device now. A port with many addresses on it usually leads to another switch, or to an access point with wireless clients.
  • An empty result means the switch has not received a frame from that address. Before drawing a conclusion, check that you typed the address in the dotted form, that the device has a link, and that it has sent traffic.
A followed cord and the table

Following a cord through the rack by eye and by finger shows where the cord appears to go. Rack cords run in bundles, and two cords that leave neighboring panel ports together can land several ports apart. The MAC address table shows where the switch hears the device. Record both readings. Where they disagree, record the difference and report it.

535.2.7 Port assignment: where the path becomes a record

Three identifiers describe one drop. Two are fixed by the installation, and one changes whenever a cord moves.

Every element of the path so far is something you can touch. The path is also written down: a label on the faceplate, a label on the patch panel, and a row in the port map. A port assignment is the record of which switch port a patch panel port is patched to. The three identifiers are not interchangeable:

  • The outlet identifier, on the faceplate at the work area.
  • The patch panel port, where the drop’s horizontal cable is terminated. It has a position of its own, such as panel B, port 14, and carries a label with the identifier of the outlet it serves.
  • The switch port, which is whatever the patch panel port is patched to today.

The first two are fixed when the building is cabled. The third changes whenever someone moves a patch cord. The port map exists to record that one changeable relationship.

Text description of this diagram

The diagram, titled Three identifiers for one drop, from another job, shows three boxes side by side, each holding one identifier for the same drop. Reading an identifier. An identifier is built from a scheme. Under the scheme of the job in the figure, 2A-14 is outlet 14 in zone A on floor 2, served by the telecommunications room on floor 2. ANSI/TIA-606, the labeling standard, sets the format of identifiers. The site’s scheme decides the names. The standard requires each site to record its scheme. Find the site’s scheme before reading its labels. On the mock-up, section 4 of the network plan states the scheme and the outlet-to-port map.

Where the port map lives

A port map is a document, and where it is kept varies. On a small site it is a spreadsheet on a shared drive or a printed sheet inside the telecommunications room door. On a larger site it is a record in a cable management or asset system. Before you start, find out where the port map is kept, whether you may write to it or only read it, and who updates it if you may not. Where you may only read it, hand a clear note of each change to the person who owns it.

What ANSI/TIA-606 requires of your work

  • Every element has a unique identifier: every outlet, cable, patch panel port, pathway, and space.
  • Anything with two ends is labeled at both ends with the same identifier. A cable labeled at one end only has to be traced again.
  • The labels and the records agree. A label that no record mentions, and a record that names a label that cannot be found, are both defects.

Labels go on the faceplate, on the patch panel at the port, and on the cable at both ends, close enough to the connector to read without pulling the bundle apart. Machine-printed wrap-around or flag labels last; handwriting on tape fades and peels. Patch cords are usually not labeled: they change too often for a label to stay true, and the port map records where they go.

Label the drop, not the person at it

A label that reads “Reception PC” is wrong the day reception gets a different computer. The identifier names a fixed position in the building. Who or what uses that position goes in the port map, in a column that is expected to change.

Lesson 535.2 · Section 3 Records and the Trace

535.2.8 Tracing a live path without interrupting it

A trace proves which patch panel port and which switch port serve an outlet, on a network that other people are using while you trace it.

Most quick ways to trace a path break it. A broken path turns a ~5 min job into an outage.

Unplugging to see who complains

Unplugging a cord finds the port. It also drops a call, a camera recording, or a transaction, and the switch logs the lost link. Do not trace a live path by unplugging it.

A trace that leaves the link up reads the labels first, then confirms them at the switch.

Read the labels first. The identifier on the faceplate names the outlet, and under most schemes it also points to the room and the patch panel port that serve it. Where the labeling is complete and accurate, this part of the trace takes ~1 min.

A tone generator and probe identify an unlabeled cable that is out of service: the generator puts a tone on the cable at the outlet, and the probe picks it up at the patch panel. The generator takes the device’s place at the outlet, so toning is not a method for a path in use.

Then confirm. From the patch panel port, the patch cord leads to a switch. A label tells you what someone intended; a confirmation tells you what is connected. The two differ often enough that a trace without confirmation puts wrong values in the record. To confirm a live path, read the switch:

  • The switch’s MAC address table. Make the device send traffic, for example with a ping, then search the table for the MAC address of its wired adapter, read in Windows. The port in the entry is read from the switch itself, not inferred from a label or a cord.
  • The switch port’s status, read before and after you plug in. On a drop that only your own laptop uses, plugging the laptop in brings the switch port up. The switch’s show interfaces status output then reports the port as connected where it read notconnect before. On a drop someone else is using, this method interrupts them.

Record as you go. Write the outlet, the patch panel port, and the switch port down together, at the moment you confirm them. A record rebuilt from memory at the end of the day is mostly right, but the next technician cannot tell which parts are wrong.

Put the steps of a trace that leaves the link up in order, working from the outlet toward the switch.

Read the identifier on the faceplate
Go to the room that serves the outlet
Find the patch panel port labeled with that identifier
Follow the patch cord from that port to the switch
Make the laptop send traffic, then confirm the switch port from the MAC address table
Record the outlet, panel port, and switch port together

535.2.9 The record, and what a discrepancy means

A trace produces two findings: the path you found, and whether the record agrees with it.

Two documents describe the same cabling. The design drawing records what was intended before installation began. The as-built records what was installed. They often differ: a cable is rerouted around ductwork no drawing showed, an outlet moves two feet to clear a column, a patch panel port is skipped because its termination failed. Those changes are normal installation work. The mistake is leaving the record describing a building that was never built.

Record the path you found before you compare it with the record. A path written down after reading the record tends to repeat the record. Then compare the two, item by item, and mark each one as a match or a mismatch.

A discrepancy is one of three kinds. The kind decides what you may do about it.

Three kinds of discrepancy, and how each is handled.
What is wrongWhat it looks likeWhat you do
The labelThe path is correct and recorded, but a label is missing, unreadable, or names another dropCorrect the label to match the record and the path, and note that you did
The recordThe path is correct and labeled, but the record shows something elseWhere you may write to the record, update it with the date and the change. Where you may not, hand the same note to its owner. Leave the path alone
The pathThe cable or cord does not go where the record and the labels sayReport it before changing anything. Where the work order allows patching, you may reseat a cord or move it back to the port the record names

On the mock-up, a technician reports a discrepancy to the Kestrel site lead, who decides what changes and who changes it.

Repatch only as the work order allows

The cord may be where it is for a reason you cannot see, and someone may depend on it now. Where the work order allows patching, you may reseat a cord, or move it back to the port the record or the work order names. Any other move waits for a decision: report the difference, get the decision, then make the change. A cord moved to tidy a record can cause an outage the next technician cannot explain, because the change was never written down.

Lesson 535.2 · Section 4 Wrapping Up

535.2.10 What you covered

The path, the devices on it, the record, and a method for proving that the path and the record agree.

The path

A connection at an outlet is seven elements in series: the network adapter, a patch cord, the telecommunications outlet, the horizontal cable, the patch panel port, a second patch cord, and the switch port. The adapter and the switch port are the ends; the five between them are the cabling. The permanent link is the outlet, the horizontal cable, and the patch panel port: the part an installer usually certifies. The channel adds a patch cord at each end: the cabling the device uses.

The two length limits, and what each one covers.
MeasureLimitCovers
Permanent link90 m (295 ft)Outlet, horizontal cable, patch panel port
Channel100 m (328 ft)The permanent link plus the patch cord at each end

Horizontal cabling serves work areas from their telecommunications room; backbone cabling joins rooms, floors, and buildings. What a run connects decides its kind. One person affected points to a drop; a floor or a room affected at once points to something shared.

The devices

A switch forwards frames within a VLAN by MAC address. An access point joins wireless devices to the wired network. A gateway moves packets between networks. A DHCP server leases IP addresses; a DNS server answers names with IP addresses. Windows reports each adapter’s status, link speed, and MAC address, and a Cisco switch writes the same MAC address in dotted form. The switch’s MAC address table shows the port where the switch hears a device, once the device has sent traffic.

The record and the trace

Three identifiers describe one drop: the outlet identifier, the patch panel port, and the switch port. The first two are fixed; the third changes when a cord moves, and the port map records it. Under ANSI/TIA-606, every element has a unique identifier, anything with two ends is labeled at both, and the labels and the records agree.

The trace
  1. Read the identifier on the faceplate.
  2. Go to the room that serves the outlet.
  3. Find the patch panel port labeled with that identifier.
  4. Follow the patch cord from that port to the switch.
  5. Make the laptop send traffic, then confirm the switch port from the MAC address table.
  6. Record the outlet, panel port, and switch port together.

Record what you found before you compare it with the record. Where they disagree, the label, the record, and the path are three different problems. A label or a record may be corrected. A path is reported before anything changes, except a cord the work order allows you to reseat or move back to the port the record names.
