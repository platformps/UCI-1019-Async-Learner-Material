Lesson 535.1 - OSI as a Troubleshooting Ladder

How a technician uses the seven layers of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model to decide where to look first when a device on a network stops working, and where the technician’s own work ends.

Kestrel Communications holds the telecommunications contract for the Harlow Street Operations Center, project HSOC-26-014. The cabling on the installation mock-up is installed, labeled, and tested. The network team’s ticket for the next phase reads: “Circuit delivered. Verify and hand off.” Each installer now connects laptops and other devices to the outlets in their own bay, confirms that each one reaches the network, and reports what does not.

D. Okafor, the Kestrel site lead, sets one rule for every report: “‘The network is down’ tells me nothing I can act on. Tell me which layer you checked, what you read there, and where you stopped.”

OSI as a Troubleshooting Ladder

The OSI model describes a network in seven layers. Read as an order of work, the layers tell a technician what to check first, what to leave for later, and where the technician’s own work ends.

4 sections  ·  16 topics  ·  17 activities

By the end of Lesson 535.1 you will be able to
  • Identify the seven layers of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model and their functions.
  • Map cable, connectors, and patch panels to Layer 1.
  • Map switches and Media Access Control (MAC) addresses to Layer 2.
  • Map Internet Protocol (IP) addresses and the gateway to Layer 3.
  • Explain where a cabling fault appears in the model.
  • Relate troubleshooting order to the layer stack.

A report that the network is down says almost nothing about the cause.

A user reports that the network is down. The report does not say whether a cable is damaged, whether a switch port is shut down, whether a laptop has the wrong IP address, whether a server in another building has failed, or whether someone mistyped a password. It reports a symptom. The symptom is the same whatever the cause.

Without a method, the technician guesses. Each wrong guess costs something on site:

  • A working cable is replaced, and the fault is still there.
  • A switch that 20 other people depend on is restarted.
  • A fault the technician could have fixed is escalated to another team.
  • An hour goes into a fault that belonged to another team from the start.

The OSI model describes the movement of data as seven numbered layers. Each layer is a group of functions that the layers above it rely on. The model describes the job, not the equipment: no switch or laptop is built in seven parts. It serves several purposes:

  • A way of dividing a large job. Delivering data across a network is split into separate functions, so each one can be designed, built, and repaired on its own.
  • A framework for standards. Standards are written against the layers. A Cisco switch, a UniFi access point, and a laptop from a third maker work together because each follows the standards for its layers.
  • A common language. “A Layer 1 problem” means the same thing to the installer, the network team, and a manufacturer’s support line.
  • A troubleshooting method, which the rest of this lesson develops.
Every check can come back clean while the service still fails

What fails is a service: data that does not reach its destination. The cause can sit at any layer, or at more than one.

Suppose a device is patched into a switch port that has been shut down. The program runs normally, the firewall (a device or program that passes or blocks traffic by rules) allows the traffic, and the IP address is correct. The service still fails, because the fault sits below every layer that was checked. Each clean result removes one place from the search. It does not show that there is no fault.

A camera shows no link at its switch port. A technician could spend ~20 min checking its IP address, its subnet mask (which marks the part of the IP address that names the network), and its gateway (the router or firewall it sends traffic through to reach other networks). All three could be correct. None of them takes effect while no signal reaches the port.

A low voltage technician owns the lowest layer: the cable, the jack, the patch panel, the switch port, and the power that a Power over Ethernet (PoE) port puts on the cable.

535.1.2 The seven layers

The layers are numbered one to seven, counting up from the physical medium.

Layer 7 is where the program a person uses meets the network. Layer 1 is the physical medium: copper, fiber, or radio. When a device sends data, the data travels down from Layer 7 to Layer 1 and leaves as a signal. When a device receives data, the data travels up from Layer 1 to Layer 7.

The cards run from Layer 7 at the top to Layer 1 at the bottom, the order in which the layers are drawn. Open each card.

Numbers more often than names

On site, the layers are named by number: “a Layer 1 problem,” “a Layer 3 problem.”

Place each item at the layer it belongs to.

535.1.3 Wrapping and unwrapping: data in transit

The layers describe what happens to data on its way out of one device and into another.

As data leaves a device it travels down the layers, and several of them add something to it. When it arrives at another device it travels up, and each addition is removed in turn. Adding the wrappings on the way down is encapsulation. Removing them on the way up is de-encapsulation.

Posting a document works the same way. The page goes in an envelope, and the envelope is addressed. A courier puts the envelope in a sack with many others and labels the sack. Each wrapping carries its own address for a different handler. The handlers read the wrapping meant for them and pass the rest along; none of them reads the page. At the far end the sack is opened, then the envelope, in the reverse order, and the page reaches the person it was addressed to.

Data is handled the same way. On the way down, Layer 4, then Layer 3, then Layer 2 each add a small block of information at the front, a header, carrying the addresses that layer needs. The result has a name at each layer: a segment at Layer 4, a packet at Layer 3, and a frame at Layer 2. Layer 2 also adds a check value at the end, the Frame Check Sequence (FCS), which the receiving device uses to test whether the frame arrived intact. Every device on a network does both jobs: it encapsulates what it sends and de-encapsulates what it receives.

The Layer 2 header carries MAC addresses, and the FCS, the frame check sequence, is the check value. Row 5: no blocks, only a wave, labeled Bits, with the line: Layer 1 sends the frame as a signal: voltage, light, or radio.

An arrow down the left side is labeled Sending, for encapsulation. An arrow up the right side is labeled Receiving, for de-encapsulation, where each header is removed in the reverse order to the one in which it was added.

The diagram shows that the data is not opened on the way. A switch reads the Layer 2 header, chooses the port the frame leaves by, and does not look inside. It forwards an email and a camera stream the same way.

A signal crosses the cable, not a message: voltage on copper, pulses of light on fiber, radio between an access point and a laptop. Layer 1 promises one thing: the bits that go in at one end come out at the other unchanged. It promises nothing about addresses, switch settings, or whether a program works.

What a passing cable test proves

A wiremap test confirms that each conductor reaches the right pin at the far end, with no open, short, or crossed conductor. That is a Layer 1 result. A device on a cable that passes still reaches nothing if the switch port is shut down or the device’s IP address is wrong. Report what the test proved. Write “the run passed a wiremap test,” not “the network is fine.”

A fault also travels upward. Damage the cable and the frame arrives corrupted. The receiving device discards a corrupted frame, so the packet inside never arrives and the program never gets its reply. The user reports that the system is down: one physical fault, reported as a Layer 7 symptom.

535.1.4 The layers that are yours

One layer belongs to the low voltage technician. The technician reads the two above it.

The layers also sort problems by owner: they show which faults a technician fixes and which go to another team.

Where a low voltage technician’s work sits against the seven layers.
LayersWhoseWhat that means in practice
1YoursYou install the cabling, test it, and fix what is physical, including the power a PoE port supplies
2Yours to readYou read the switch port: its status, its VLAN, and which device is on it. You report a wrong VLAN, a disabled port, or any other switch or controller setting to the network team. You change one only where a work order assigns that change to you
3Yours to readYou read IP addresses and report them, and you set your own laptop’s IP address within the address plan. Designing the addressing belongs to the network team
4 to 7The network team’sYou escalate, with evidence of what you checked

Layer 1 is the cable or fiber you pulled, the jack you terminated, the panel it lands on, the switch port it plugs into, the link the adapter brings up, and the power a PoE port supplies. Layer 2 is the switch: which port a device is on, and whether that port passes traffic.

A password the program rejects sits at the top of the stack. No cable test reaches it. Report it to the team that owns the program, with a note of what you checked.

Layer 1 faults are common

A damaged cable, a loose jack, or a port that supplies no power is usually quick to fix once found.

535.1.5 Three ways to work through the layers

The direction of a search depends on what you know when the fault reaches you.

Three approaches to troubleshooting with the OSI model.
ApproachHow it runsWhen to use it
Bottom-upStart at Layer 1 and work upwardWhen physical work has just been done, or a physical fault is likely. A low voltage technician’s usual starting point
Top-downStart at Layer 7 and work downwardWhen the complaint is about one program, and every other program on the same device works
Divide and conquerStart in the middle, usually at Layer 3 with a ping. If the ping fails, work down. If it succeeds, work upWhen nothing points to a starting layer. One test removes half the layers from the search

With divide and conquer, a single ping that gets a reply shows that Layers 1 to 3 delivered data in both directions. The search then moves upward.

A low voltage technician starts bottom-up because the technician’s layers are at the bottom. A fault there is common and quick to confirm.

An approach is a starting point

No approach is more correct than the others. A technician who already has good evidence may start anywhere. Whichever approach you use, you must be able to say which layers you have ruled out, and by which reading.

535.1.6 Layer 1: what you install, and what it promises

Layer 1 promises that the bits that go in at one end come out at the other unchanged.

Layer 1 does not know what it carries. It holds no addresses, keeps no record of what it sent, and cannot tell a video call from a print job. It turns bits into a signal, carries the signal along a medium, and turns it back into bits.

Every part you install is Layer 1: the cable or fiber, the connectors, the keystone jack in the outlet, the patch panel, the patch cords, and the port at each end. The electronics that turn bits into a signal are Layer 1 as well: the network adapter in a laptop or a camera, and the matching circuit behind each switch port.

The link

When a good cable joins a network adapter to a live switch port, the two ends agree on a speed and bring up a link. The link light on the switch port shows it, as does the light on the adapter where it has one. Windows reports the adapter as connected. A link is a Layer 1 fact: a signal passes between the two ends. It does not show that any data reaches its destination.

What Layer 1 is responsible for, and what it is not.
Layer 1 is responsible forLayer 1 is not responsible for
Turning bits into a signal and backKnowing which device the data is for
Carrying the signal the required distanceChoosing which port the data leaves by
Delivering the bits unchangedDetecting or repairing corrupted data
Bringing up a link at an agreed speedAddresses, programs, or users

Power is a Layer 1 function. Power over Ethernet carries direct current to a camera or an access point on the same cable as its data. The power belongs to the physical layer with the signal, not to the switch’s decisions at Layer 2. On the mock-up, PoE comes from the UniFi PoE switch or from a PoE injector. The Cisco learner switches supply none.

Distance is a Layer 1 limit. A signal weakens as it travels. ANSI/TIA-568, the structured cabling standard from the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA), limits a horizontal copper channel to 100 meters: 90 meters of permanent cable and the rest in patch cords. A run beyond the limit may still link while it corrupts some of the bits it carries.

A link can be partly working. A link can be good, dead, or marginal. A marginal link passes most bits and corrupts some, often when the cable warms up or a nearby motor starts. The user sees a connection that drops for a second and returns, which is harder to find than a link that never comes up. IEEE 802.3, the Ethernet standard from the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), sets what the equipment at each end must handle. ANSI/TIA-568 sets how much loss and interference the cabling may add. A certification tester measures a run against those limits.

“Layer 0” is not a layer

Some technicians and vendors call the passive cabling (the cable, connectors, and pathways) “Layer 0.” The OSI model has no Layer 0. In the model, and on this page, the cabling belongs to Layer 1.

535.1.7 Copper, fiber, and radio on the model

Copper, fiber, and radio all sit at Layer 1 and make the same promise; each fails in its own ways.

The three physical media, and how each tends to fail.
MediumCarriesTypical failureSensitive to
CopperVoltage on twisted pairsA broken conductor, wrong pair order, corrosionElectrical interference, distance, tight bends, untwisted pairs
FiberPulses of lightA dirty or damaged end face, a break in the glassContamination, bend radius, physical shock
RadioRadio waves through the airA weak signal, congestion, interferenceDistance, walls, other radio sources, the number of devices sharing it

Copper carries an electrical signal, so a nearby electrical field can induce noise in it: a motor, a fluorescent ballast, or a power cable run alongside. Fiber carries light and no current, so electrical noise does not reach it. Where a run must pass heavy electrical equipment, fiber avoids the problem.

Radio is shared. A copper run serves one link, but every device within range of an access point uses the same air. A wireless link can slow down because more devices have joined, with nothing physically wrong.

The same layer, a different first question

On copper, the first question is about the terminations and what runs alongside the cable. On fiber, it is about clean end faces. On radio, it is about distance, obstructions, and how many devices share the access point.

535.1.8 Layer 2: what the switch decides

Layer 1 carries the signal. At Layer 2, the switch decides where each frame goes next.

A frame arrives on one switch port, and the switch decides which port or ports it leaves by. To decide, the switch reads the frame’s Layer 2 header, which carries the MAC address of the device the frame is for. A MAC address is the Layer 2 address built into every network adapter. A switch working at Layer 2 does not open the packet inside the frame, so it never reads the IP address.

The switch makes one of three decisions. Open each card.

VLAN forwarding

A switch forwards a frame only to ports in the same VLAN as the port it arrived on. A device in VLAN 140 and a device in VLAN 160 on the same switch cannot exchange frames, and a broadcast in one VLAN does not reach the other. One physical switch behaves as several separate switches. Traffic between two VLANs has to pass through a router or firewall, at Layer 3.

The discard case and the fault report

The cause is at Layer 1, the evidence is at Layer 2, and the complaint comes from Layer 7.

535.1.9 MAC addressing: how a switch learns where devices are

A switch holds no configured list of which device is on which port. It learns from the frames it receives.

Every network adapter is made with a MAC address: six pairs of hexadecimal digits (0 to 9 and A to F), such as 5c:11:bb:22:cc:33. The first half identifies the manufacturer; the second half is unique to the adapter. A wired adapter’s MAC address is set at manufacture and does not normally change. An IP address is assigned and can change.

A switch keeps its own record of where devices are, the MAC address table. It fills the table by observation: each time a frame arrives, the switch records the sender’s MAC address and the port the frame arrived on.

Figure: How a switch fills its MAC address table
Text description of this diagram

The diagram, titled A switch learns which port each device is on, shows a box on the left: Frame arrives on port 7, sender MAC address 5c:11:bb:22:cc:33. An arrow labeled recorded points right to a box titled MAC address table.

The table has two columns, Address and Port, and two rows. The first row, highlighted, pairs 5c:11:bb:22:cc:33 with port 7. The second row pairs 5c:11:bb:44:dd:55 with port 12.

Two lines below read: Next time a frame is addressed to 5c:11:bb:22:cc:33, the switch sends it out of port 7 only. A destination the table has never seen is sent out of every other port in the same VLAN.

The table drives two of the switch’s decisions. A destination in the table is forwarded out of one port. A destination missing from the table is sent out of every other port, and the switch learns its port from the reply.

Entries expire. A switch removes an entry when the device has sent nothing for a set time (300 seconds by default on Cisco Catalyst switches) and when the link on that port goes down. The switch then learns a moved device again on its new port.

What the MAC address table answers

An entry for a device shows that the switch has received intact frames from that device on that port, so the device has power and a link. No entry sends you back down to the cable, the connections, the device, and its power.

535.1.10 Layer 3: IP addresses and the gateway

Layer 2 moves frames within one network. Layer 3 moves packets between networks.

An IP address identifies a device and the network it belongs to. In the IPv4 address 192.168.70.23 with the subnet mask 255.255.255.0, the mask marks the first three numbers, 192.168.70, as the network. The last number names the device on it. Devices on the same network reach each other directly, through the switch.

To reach a device on any other network, a device sends its traffic to its default gateway: the address of the router or firewall that joins its network to the others. A device without a correct gateway reaches its own network and nothing beyond it.

The frame and the packet on the way to another network

A laptop at 192.168.70.23 sends data to a camera server at 192.168.80.30, on another network. The packet carries the server’s IP address the whole way. The frame around it carries the gateway’s MAC address, because the gateway is the next device on the local network. The gateway removes the frame, reads the IP address, and sends the packet on in a new frame toward the server.

Layer 2 and Layer 3 compared.
QuestionLayer 2Layer 3
AddressMAC address, built into the adapterIP address, assigned by a server or by hand
Device that forwardsSwitchRouter or firewall: the gateway
ReachWithin one network, and one VLANBetween networks
A test a technician readsThe switch’s MAC address tablePing

An IP address is assigned, by a Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) server or by hand, so it can be wrong in ways a MAC address cannot: a mistyped address, a mask that does not match the network, a missing gateway. On the mock-up, the project’s network plan lists each network and its gateway.

Ping sends a request to an IP address and waits for a reply. A reply shows that Layers 1 to 3 delivered data in both directions. No reply does not always mean no path: a device can be set to ignore ping, and on a default Windows installation, Windows Defender Firewall blocks incoming ping requests.

535.1.11 From a laptop to a switch port

The path from a laptop’s network adapter to a switch port has seven physical elements. A fault can sit in any of them.

Every element from the laptop to the switch port is Layer 1, the switch port included. Layer 2 is not a separate piece of hardware: it is what the switch does with the frames that arrive at the port. A fault report does not say which element is at fault. Know the elements in order.

On the mock-up, the horizontal cable runs from the outlet through the wall cavity and along the cable ladder to the patch panels in the Mobile Infrastructure Rack (MIR), which stands in for the telecommunications room. The horizontal cable is the hardest element to inspect, and the least often disturbed: it is terminated once and then left alone. The patch cords at each end are handled, moved, and swapped every day, so they are early suspects.

535.1.12 Where a cabling fault appears in the model

A cabling fault starts at Layer 1 and shows a different symptom at each layer above it.

One broken conductor, seen from four layers.
Looking atWhat you observeWhat it tempts you to conclude
Layer 1No link, or a link at 100 megabits per second (Mbps) instead of 1000 MbpsThat the cabling is at fault. Often right, but a switch port that is shut down also shows no link
Layer 2Where there is no link, no MAC address table entry for the portWrongly, that the switch is misconfigured
Layer 3Where there is no link, ping failsWrongly, that the addressing is the problem
Layer 7The program will not open, or times outWrongly, that the program or the server is at fault

A split pair behaves differently. In a split pair, two conductors from different pairs are swapped the same way at both ends, so each pin still connects to the matching pin at the far end. Continuity is intact, and the link often comes up. What suffers is the signal: the conductors that carry each signal are no longer twisted together, so crosstalk (signal leaking from one pair into another) rises and the link is unreliable, worst at higher speeds.

A split pair, seen from the same four layers.
Looking atWhat you observe
Layer 1A link is present. A continuity check and a basic wiremap test pass it, because each pin reaches the matching pin. A tester that measures crosstalk, such as a certification tester, finds it
Layer 2A MAC address table entry appears; damaged frames are discarded
Layer 3Ping gets replies sometimes and fails sometimes
Layer 7The program is slow, or drops out and recovers
The pattern behind the symptoms

A fault that stops everything is usually a broken connection. A fault that makes a link unreliable is usually a marginal or miswired one. Both start at Layer 1, and a Layer 7 complaint cannot tell them apart.

Lesson 535.1 · Section 3 Finding the Fault

535.1.13 Why one physical fault becomes a complaint about a program

The person reporting a fault sees only Layer 7.

Follow one damaged conductor up the stack and watch the symptom change at each step.

How one physical fault travels up the layers to become a user’s complaint.
LayerWhat happens
1A conductor is damaged, so the signal arrives distorted
2Frames arrive corrupted and fail their check, so the switch discards them
3The packets inside those frames never arrive, so nothing is reachable
4Delivery is never confirmed, so the sender retries and then gives up
7The program waits, shows an error, and the person says the system is down

Each layer in that chain reported what it could see. The person at the top saw only the program.

A fault report states the symptom, not the location. “The system is down” fits a damaged cable, a shut-down port, a wrong IP address, a failed server, and an expired password. Reading the report as the cause leads to replacing equipment that works.

The technician translates the report into a question a tool can answer, starting at the bottom of the stack.

Listen for clues in the report

A report that sounds vague can still carry evidence. “It was fine yesterday” and “only when I move my laptop” are Layer 1 clues, stated in the only words the person has. Ask what changed, and when.

535.1.14 The four questions, in order

Worked from the bottom up, the seven layers become four questions a technician can ask at the outlet.

Each question has a tool that answers it and a reading you can record. Ask them in order and record every answer. The lowest question whose answer is no is where the repair, or the report, starts.

Act on the lowest no

A no at step 1 is the next job, not a step to climb past. While no signal arrives, the port reports no device however it is configured, and the device has no address to read. Readings taken above a no look like evidence and are not.

Power belongs to step 1. A camera or an access point that takes all its power from the switch port cannot bring up a link until the port supplies power.

Lesson 535.1 · Section 3 Finding the Fault

535.1.15 Whose problem is it, and where your work ends

Step 1 of the ladder is yours to resolve. Steps 2 and 3 are yours to read and report. Step 4 belongs to another team.

The faults you resolve are physical: the cable, a connector, a cord, an outlet, and power to the device. You report a wrong VLAN, a disabled port, or any other switch or controller setting to the network team. You change one only where a work order assigns that change to you.

Reaching step 4 is a correct outcome, provided the fault is handed over in a state the next person can use. On the mock-up, a technician escalates to the Kestrel site lead, who makes the change or refers it to the network team. Evidence makes a hand-over usable. Compare two versions of the same escalation:

Two versions of the same escalation, from another job.
ThinUsable
“Cable is fine, must be a network problem.”“Run passed a wiremap test. Switch port Gi0/7 is connected at 1000 Mbps, full duplex, in VLAN 140, and the switch has the laptop’s MAC address on that port. Gi0/7 is the switch’s name for its port 7. Full duplex means the port sends and receives at the same time.

The usable version states what was tested and how. It gives the values, so the next person does not repeat the work. It names the layer where the technician stopped, so the next person knows what remains to examine. It does not need to name the cause at the layer above.

What a usable hand-over contains
  • What you verified: the steps you completed and the tool you used for each.
  • What you found: the values, not a judgment about them.
  • Where you stopped: the layer you are handing over at, and why.

Lesson 535.1 · Section 4 Wrapping Up

535.1.16 What you covered

The model, the troubleshooting questions, and where the technician’s work ends.

The OSI model describes the movement of data in seven layers. It divides a large job into parts, gives standards a common framework, gives technicians a common language, and gives a method for searching when something does not work.

The layers, and where a low voltage technician’s work sits.
LayersWhat happens thereYours?
4 to 7Delivery to the right program, sessions, formatting, and the program itselfNo: escalate
3IP addresses, networks, the gateway, routing between networksRead and report; set your own laptop’s IP address within the plan
2Switches, MAC addresses, the MAC address table, VLANsReport, unless the work order assigns it
1Copper, fiber, and radio; connectors, jacks, panels, ports; the adapter’s link; Power over EthernetYes

Data is wrapped on the way down (encapsulation) and unwrapped on the way up (de-encapsulation). Each device reads only the wrapping meant for it, so a switch forwards traffic without knowing what it carries. A switch learns where devices are from the frames it receives, and forwards a frame only within its VLAN. A gateway moves packets between networks.

A cabling fault starts at Layer 1 and shows a different symptom at each layer above. A broken connection stops everything; a marginal or miswired one makes the link unreliable. The person reporting either one sees only Layer 7.

Three search orders are available: bottom-up, top-down, and divide and conquer from a ping. A low voltage technician usually starts at the bottom.

The four questions
  1. Is it connected, is the medium good, and does it have power?
  2. Is the switch port up, and in the right VLAN?
  3. Does it have the right IP address, and can it reach its gateway?
  4. Anything above that? Escalate, with evidence.

Ask them in order, record every answer, and act on the lowest no. A fault report describes a symptom, not a location. Turn it into a question a tool can answer, and record what you checked, what you found, and where you stopped.
